Indecision at the Top: The Responsibility-Without-Control Problem

2026-10-26

A commentary in Military Medicine draws a line most executive coaching skips entirely — the line between hesitation that comes from inside you and hesitation that comes from the structure of the situation. If you have been circling the same decision for weeks, that distinction is the first thing worth checking.

The news in brief

A commentary published in Military Medicine in October 2026 identifies a structural distinction in leadership hesitation: some indecision originates inside the decision-maker — psychology, discipline, character — while some originates in the situation itself: missing authority, missing information, or missing candor from the people around you. The piece is written in a military context but names a mechanism that applies to every senior leadership role where the person nominally in charge does not fully control the outcome.

What happened

According to the Military Medicine commentary, the distinction between internal and structural hesitation is easy to miss and expensive to ignore. The publication draws a direct line between hesitation caused by personal factors and hesitation caused by situational gaps — specifically, an authority gap (the decision is not formally yours to make), an information gap (data you need exists but has not reached you), or a candor gap (the people around you are managing you rather than leveling with you). The commentary's primary context is military command, where authority is explicit and information gaps carry documented operational consequences. Its authors argue that diagnosing a structural problem as a personal failure is not just incorrect — it is counterproductive, directing effort toward the wrong intervention.

Why it matters

If you are a high-performing person who has stalled on a decision, the default diagnosis is almost always internal: more discipline, sharper thinking, better habits. That default has a track record, so it keeps running even when the actual problem is structural. The cost of that misdiagnosis is not small. It sends your attention inward toward discipline and willpower when it belongs outward toward scope and information. It is exhausting in a way that no amount of resilience practice fixes, because the load is not yours and resilience is designed for loads that actually belong to you.

The responsibility-without-control pattern — accountable for an outcome, without control over the variables that determine it — is the standard architecture of every senior leadership role and most entrepreneurial situations after a certain scale. The problem is not the architecture. The problem is when no one names it, including you. Before any inner work can land, the prior question has to be answered: is this decision actually mine to make? Do I have the authority it requires, the information it needs, and the candor from the people I depend on? Structural problems do not respond to inner discipline. Applying inner discipline to a structural problem harder is not progress — it is the loop.

Our perspective

The Military Medicine distinction maps directly onto a failure pattern we see consistently in high-achieving people who seek out coaching: decision paralysis diagnosed as a character flaw that is actually a process flaw, or a structural flaw, or both layered on top of each other.

Anger is one of the clearest signals this is happening. Anger in executive situations usually stems from a confidence breakdown. Confidence breaks down when you do not have a working process. In a responsibility-without-control situation, the process is broken by definition — you cannot close the loop because the variables you need are not yours to move. The emotional state that surfaces from that unresolved loop is not a temperament problem. It is not a stress response. It is a signal that something structural has failed. Treating it as a character deficit sends you to coping mechanisms. Treating it as a signal sends you to the process.

Decision paralysis happens in the theoretical space you construct — projections, frameworks, accumulated thinking, increasingly disconnected from your actual value set. More calculation does not resolve that. It reinforces the constructed space. The decisions that need to land from your gut keep getting routed through your head and come out the other side as more analysis.

The sequence that actually works is sequential, not binary. First: is this decision structurally intact — authority, information, candor? If no, that is where to go first. Second: given that the structure is as good as it is going to get, what in my inner environment is still producing the loop? The inner work has a precise target once those two questions are separated. Without that separation, you are carrying a structural problem as though it were a character flaw — and that has a cost that resilience practice is not designed to absorb.

This is interpretation based on the framework we apply in practice, informed by the distinction the Military Medicine commentary names.

What happens next

The research establishing a clean line between structural and internal hesitation is not exhaustive. In practice, the two are almost always layered — a genuine authority gap does not prevent someone from also carrying unhelpful patterns about how a decision should feel before it is right. The useful posture is sequential. Work the structural question first. Once that is as resolved as it is going to get, the inner environment becomes the precise and legitimate target.

What remains open: most executive coaching frameworks currently in circulation still do not make this distinction explicit. Until they do, high-achieving people will keep applying inner work to structural problems and concluding the work is not working hard enough — when the actual issue is that they are solving the wrong problem.
