Your Medicare Plan May Be Discontinued for 2027: What to Do Before December 7

2026-10-23

If your Medicare Advantage plan just sent you a notice saying it's ending or changing for 2027, the December 7 Annual Enrollment Period deadline is not a suggestion — and what you do before it determines your coverage starting January 1, 2027.

The news in brief

CMS data indicates that roughly 3.2 million Medicare Advantage enrollees are facing forced plan changes for 2027, as carriers exit counties, restructure benefits, or withdraw plans entirely. The Annual Enrollment Period (AEP) runs October 15 through December 7. If your plan is being discontinued and you take no action before that deadline, you will most likely be defaulted back to Original Medicare — Parts A and B only — with no prescription drug coverage, no out-of-pocket maximum, no dental, and no vision.

What happened

According to CMS data reported in Medicare industry coverage, Medicare Advantage has been under sustained financial pressure. CMS has been adjusting benchmark payments and tightening the risk-adjustment methodology it uses to calculate per-enrollee carrier payments. At the same time, medical utilization has been running higher than carriers projected when they priced their 2024 and 2025 plans. Plans that looked profitable on paper two years ago are not profitable now. Carriers responded by cutting or exiting — not uniformly, but county by county, wherever the math no longer works. The impact is deeply uneven: one ZIP code loses two options and gains three comparable ones; the next county over loses its only zero-premium plan and the replacement options carry deductibles and network restrictions that didn't exist before. The notice you received — an Annual Notice of Change (ANOC) or a plan discontinuation letter — is CMS-required communication confirming your current plan will not exist in its current form on January 1, 2027.

Why it matters

The default outcome if you do nothing is not a safety net. Original Medicare alone carries no out-of-pocket maximum, no prescription drug coverage, and no dental or vision benefits. Whatever your Advantage plan wrapped into a single premium is gone the moment that plan ends — unless you actively choose a replacement. Beyond the coverage gap, the network problem is real and getting worse. When a plan exits and you move to a replacement — even from the same carrier — you are moving to a different network contract. Your cardiologist, your orthopedic surgeon, your oncology team do not automatically carry over. Seniors routinely enroll in replacement Advantage plans only to discover mid-year that a specialist they are actively treating with is now out of network. And there is a second door that may be closing without you knowing it: if your plan is being discontinued involuntarily, you may have a guaranteed-issue right to buy a Medigap supplemental policy — meaning an insurer cannot deny you or charge you more based on your health conditions. That window is narrow and tied to your specific situation. If you let it close without investigating it, a pre-existing condition could make you ineligible or price you out of Medigap entirely, later, when you want it.

Our perspective

The generic instruction — shop around, compare plans on Medicare.gov — is technically correct and practically incomplete. Medicare.gov shows you premiums and star ratings. It does not know which doctors you see, which drugs you take, or how often you use your coverage. A plan with a four-star rating and a zero-dollar premium can cost you significantly more than a plan with a higher premium if your cardiologist is out of network or your maintenance medication lands on tier four instead of tier two. The comparison that matters is not plan versus plan on a chart — it is plan versus your actual health profile and your actual providers. That comparison requires a human who is not being paid based on what you pick. When your plan is discontinued, your carrier will typically offer you a replacement and may default you into it automatically. That replacement is not independently selected to fit your situation. It is whatever that carrier has available in your county. The carrier has an obvious interest in keeping your enrollment with them. That interest is not the same as your interest in finding the plan that actually covers your doctors and your drugs at the lowest total cost. Those are two different things, and nobody at the carrier is paid to tell you that. If you are weighing Advantage against Medigap, if your drug costs are significant, or if you are managing multiple providers, get a second opinion from someone who is not paid by any carrier. The complexity here is real. It is not a sign you are doing it wrong — it is a sign the system is built to be difficult to navigate alone.

What happens next

Here is the practical sequence before December 7. First, read everything the carrier sent you — understand your default before you do anything else. Second, pull your current drug list and your provider list; those two inputs drive every comparison. Third, use Medicare.gov's Plan Finder, filter by your specific drugs, and run the estimated annual cost — that number includes premiums, deductibles, and estimated drug costs and is more useful than the monthly premium alone. Fourth, verify network participation for your key providers directly with their billing offices, not the carrier's directory, which is often out of date — ask which Medicare Advantage plans they are contracted with for 2027 and document the answer. Fifth, determine whether your involuntary plan loss triggers guaranteed-issue rights for Medigap in your state; if it does, act within that window, not after it closes. Sixth, if the comparison is still unclear after all of this, get a second opinion from an independent source before December 7. A Special Enrollment Period may give you additional time after AEP closes, but waiting for it means making a pressured decision with fewer plans to compare and with carriers who know you are in a bind. Act during AEP. December 7 is the hard stop.
