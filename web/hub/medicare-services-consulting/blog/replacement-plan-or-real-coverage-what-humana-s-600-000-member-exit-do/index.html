Replacement Plan or Real Coverage? What Humana's 600,000-Member Exit Doesn't Answer for You

2026-10-27

Humana is exiting hundreds of Medicare Advantage markets, and 600,000 beneficiaries are holding a replacement plan letter. That letter is not the answer. Here is how to find yours.

The news in brief

Humana announced it would exit hundreds of Medicare Advantage markets, a move affecting approximately 600,000 beneficiaries. Displaced members received letters from Humana recommending a replacement plan and were granted a Special Enrollment Period — typically two months — by CMS to elect new coverage. The story made national headlines. What it did not do is answer the question those 600,000 people are actually sitting with.

What happened

Medicare Advantage plans are annual contracts between a private insurance company and the federal government. Carriers can and do exit markets when the economics stop working for them. When a carrier exits, CMS grants affected beneficiaries a Special Enrollment Period — typically two months — to pick something new. That window sounds generous. It is not, practically speaking. Two months is enough time to enroll in the wrong plan and not realize it until a claim gets denied in February.

As part of its exit, Humana sent affected members letters recommending a replacement plan. The letter names a plan, quotes a premium, and lists some benefits. What it does not do, according to the pillar analysis, is compare that plan to every other option available in a member's zip code for their specific situation — and it will not tell you whether your primary care physician is in the new network, how your drug list maps against the new formulary, or what competing carriers in your area are offering.

Why it matters

The replacement letter is not advice. It is a retention play. The carrier has a financial interest in keeping you in its ecosystem, and the replacement plan it recommends is the one that serves that interest first. It may also serve your interest — or it may not. The letter will not tell you which situation you are in.

Three things that replacement letter systematically omits:

Network continuity. Medicare Advantage is network-dependent in a way Original Medicare is not. A plan that covers your providers at zero cost-sharing and one that puts them out-of-network are not equivalent plans — they are different financial exposures wearing the same premium label.

Formulary equivalence. The relevant question is not whether the replacement plan has a Part D drug benefit. It is which tier your specific drugs land on, whether any require prior authorization your current plan did not, and what your total annual drug spend looks like. A $5 premium difference can evaporate instantly against a formulary that moves your insulin from Tier 2 to Tier 4.

Out-of-pocket maximum. The difference between a $3,400 cap and a $6,700 cap is not a detail. For someone with ongoing specialist visits or a chronic condition, it is thousands of dollars of real exposure you will not see until you need care.

There is also a rights issue most replacement letters do not mention. When a Medicare Advantage plan is discontinued — not changed, discontinued — federal rules give you a guaranteed-issue right to certain Medigap policies during a specific window tied to your disenrollment date. If you accept a replacement Medicare Advantage plan instead of exercising that right, you may waive it. If you later want to leave Medicare Advantage for Original Medicare with a Medigap supplement, you could face medical underwriting in most states. That is a real fork in the road, and the letter will not flag it.

Our perspective

The 'same company' frame is wrong in a specific, mechanical way. Medicare Advantage plans are filed individually with CMS. They have different plan IDs, different networks, different formularies, and different cost-sharing structures. The fact that Humana's name is on the replacement does not mean the replacement functions like the plan it replaced. Carriers sometimes use plan exits to consolidate members into higher-margin products. That is a business decision, not a conspiracy — but it means your job is to evaluate the replacement plan against the market, not just against what you had.

Equivalence is not a feeling. It is a checklist with numbers attached. A genuinely equivalent replacement plan would have the same or comparable contracted network for your specific providers, carry your current drugs on the same or lower tiers with no new prior authorization requirements, have the same or lower out-of-pocket maximum, carry comparable specialist cost-sharing for the service categories you actually use, and land at a similar total cost after accounting for any Part B giveback or supplemental benefits. That is a five-point comparison, not a one-line summary in a letter.

Decision paralysis on a replacement plan feels like caution. It is not. Passively accepting the default is a decision — one made without comparing your network, your formulary, or your out-of-pocket exposure to anything else on the market. The work of evaluating it now is not optional. It is the only way to know what you actually signed up for.

On the tools available: Medicare.gov's plan finder shows you what exists in your zip code. It does not cross-reference your specific drug list against multiple formularies, confirm whether your doctors are in-network, or model your total annual exposure based on your actual utilization. It shows you what exists. It does not translate what exists into what fits. Free broker advice has a structural ceiling for a different reason — a broker paid by commission cannot credibly recommend the option that costs them a commission, including the answer that no Advantage plan fits and Original Medicare with a Medigap supplement is the right call. Fee-based, independent Medicare consultation removes that ceiling because the advisor's income does not change regardless of which plan you pick.

What happens next

The Special Enrollment Period triggered by a plan discontinuation is real, and the deadline is real. If you miss it and enroll passively in the default replacement, you are in that plan until the next enrollment period gives you another exit — typically the Annual Election Period running October 15 through December 7. For a plan that turns out to be a bad fit for your providers or your drug costs, that is a year of exposure you could have avoided.

If the Humana exit letter has arrived and you have not yet reviewed the actual plan documents — not the marketing summary, the Summary of Benefits and Evidence of Coverage — you do not have enough information to make this decision with confidence. The questions that actually evaluate a replacement plan are concrete: Is each of my current providers contracted with the new network? How does my drug list compare tier by tier on the new formulary? What is the out-of-pocket maximum versus other plans available in my zip code? What is the total cost picture based on my actual utilization — not just the premium line? And if I accept this replacement and want to move to Original Medicare later, what do my Medigap options and underwriting exposure look like in my state? Those questions have answers. Getting them before the SEP window closes is the work.
