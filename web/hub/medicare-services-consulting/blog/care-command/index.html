Medicare Plan Review & Guidance

2026-10-22

You are already doing the work of a care manager. You are tracking appointments, chasing referrals, translating clinical notes into plain English for the rest of the family, and catching the things that fall between providers who do not talk to each other. You are doing it without the training, the contacts, or the hours. Care Management places one accountable person between your family and that chaos — someone who owns the whole board, keeps every moving part on a single visible track, and hands you back the role you actually want: family member, not logistics coordinator.

Who this is for

Adult children — typically in their 40s and 50s — who are managing a parent's or spouse's care alongside a full-time job, often from a distance, and seniors navigating a new diagnosis, a recent hospitalization, or a care system that has suddenly become too complex to manage alone. You are not helpless. You are a capable, organized person who has hit the wall where the system's moving parts outnumber the hours in your week. You want a map, a checklist, and someone who has actually done this before.

What's included

- An intake assessment covering your parent's current care needs, providers, medications, and open follow-up items — so the full picture is visible before anything falls through - A written care plan that maps current needs against what is coming, with specific next steps the family can act on - Ongoing coordination of appointments, referrals, insurance authorizations, and provider communication — one person owns the follow-up so you do not have to chase anyone - Plain-language translation of clinical information into decisions your family can understand and act on - Periodic reviews as needs change, with the plan updated to match

How it works

The engagement starts with a fit conversation — no commitment, no pitch — to understand your situation and whether care management is the right tool. If it is, you agree on a coordination scope together: what is included, what the handoffs look like, and what you keep. From there, one accountable person takes ownership of the operational layer — the calls, the follow-ups, the tracking — and you stay in every decision without being buried in the administrative work between decisions. It is a planned engagement, not an emergency hotline. The goal is to get ahead of the next problem, not to manage the current crisis from behind.

Why it works

Care management works because it puts one person — with provider relationships, organizational systems, and procedural knowledge — at the center of a situation that currently has no center. The mechanism is operational clarity: the same thing a project manager brings to a complex build, applied to a care plan. Nothing falls through the cracks because nothing is unowned. Appointments get tracked. Referrals get followed up. Medication lists live in one place. The family stops being the translator between providers who are not talking, and starts making decisions from a complete, organized picture instead of from panic and incomplete information.

Why the alternatives do not fill this gap

A hospital social worker is managing discharge, not your parent's ongoing life. Their job ends when the paperwork clears. A home health agency provides skilled clinical care inside a narrow window Medicare defines. A binder of pamphlets from a discharge nurse is not coordination. Medicare's Chronic Care Management billing gives the primary care practice twenty minutes per month of non-face-to-face coordination — meaningful, but it does not touch housing transitions, family communication, referral follow-through, or the gap between what a doctor recommends and what a family can actually execute. Every alternative is either clinical and inaccessible or generic and untailored, with no one holding the thread. This is the coordination layer that lives between those options — calm, organized, family-controlled, and proactive rather than reactive.

The main objection answered

**"We can handle this ourselves."** You have been handling it. That is the problem. The question is not whether you are capable — you clearly are — it is whether you have the hours and the system contacts to do it without something slipping. Most families cannot, and finding out the hard way is expensive: both in the crisis itself and in the reactive decisions made under pressure. The families who navigate this well are the ones who built a plan before things broke down, not after. Planned care management is cleaner and cheaper than emergency triage. And a care manager works for you, not for the hospital or the insurance company. You set the priorities. They do the legwork between decisions. You stay in every call that matters.

Terms and next step

Care Management is currently in development. Pricing, clinical scope, and qualifying credentials are not yet defined and will be published transparently once set — including a clear explanation of what Medicare or insurance may cover for your situation. The process begins with a no-commitment fit conversation: needs and goals review, agreed coordination scope, then ongoing care organization and follow-up with periodic reviews. If you are ready to talk through what your parent's situation actually requires, that is where this starts.
