Your Medicare Broker Is Paid by the Carrier You Choose

2026-10-23

You've done the right things. You searched for help, maybe filled out a form, possibly called a number from a mailer. Now you're getting fifteen calls a day and every person on the line sounds helpful, patient, and knowledgeable — and every conversation ends with a plan recommendation. What nobody mentions is the sentence that explains all of it: your broker is paid by the carrier whose plan you pick. That's not a scandal. It's just the structure. But if you don't know it, you can't account for it — and on a decision this consequential, you need to.

The short answer

Every Medicare broker and agent who helps you enroll in a plan is compensated by the insurance carrier — not by you. CMS sets maximum commission rates, and brokers earn those commissions when you enroll and in renewal payments each year you stay on the plan. The advice is legal, often genuinely useful, and sometimes excellent. But the business model means the broker's income is tied to what you pick. That's a structural conflict of interest, and understanding it is the first step to getting advice you can actually trust.

So how does broker compensation actually work?

CMS (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services) caps the commissions carriers can pay brokers for Medicare Advantage and Part D enrollments. For 2024, the maximum initial commission for a Medicare Advantage plan was $611 per enrollee; for a standalone Part D plan, it was $100. Carriers can also pay renewal commissions — typically around half the initial rate — for each year a member stays enrolled.

What this means in practice: a broker who enrolls you in a Medicare Advantage plan and keeps you there for five years earns roughly $1,800–$2,500 from that one relationship, paid entirely by the carrier. You pay nothing directly. That feels like a free service. It is, in the sense that no money leaves your wallet in the transaction. But the broker is not a neutral party. Their revenue depends on enrollment, and enrollment requires recommending a plan.

Brokers who work with multiple carriers are sometimes called independent — and many of them genuinely do shop the market on your behalf. But even an independent broker earns more from some carriers than others, and the plans that pay the highest commissions are not always the plans that fit your situation best.

Does this mean brokers give bad advice?

Not automatically. Many brokers are knowledgeable, well-intentioned, and genuinely helpful. The compensation structure doesn't make every recommendation suspect — it sets a ceiling on how far the advice can go in your direction. A broker who earns a commission on enrollment cannot advise you to delay enrollment, to choose a plan their agency doesn't contract with, or to skip Medicare Advantage entirely for reasons that have nothing to do with the carriers in their portfolio. The advice can be accurate and still be incomplete in ways you'd want to know about.

The more specific the conflict shows up in practice: a broker has no financial incentive to tell you that Medigap might fit your situation better than any Advantage plan they sell — because Medigap commissions are structured differently and, in many cases, the same broker doesn't sell both. If the only tool they have is Advantage, every problem looks like an Advantage problem.

What does CMS say about this, and has anything changed?

CMS has been paying attention. The BROKERS TIME Act was introduced in 2025 specifically to address broker compensation transparency and the pressures created by high-commission marketing structures. Separately, CMS complaints about Medicare Advantage marketing more than doubled in a single recent year — a signal that the current model is generating real consumer harm, not just theoretical conflicts.

CMS has also tightened marketing rules: carriers and their agents are not allowed to call you unless you've specifically requested contact, and the "Medicare" branding on mailers is regulated. In practice, enforcement is uneven and the lead generation ecosystem — the directories and comparison sites that sell your information the moment you enter it — operates in gaps the rules don't fully close. Entering your zip code on a Medicare comparison website is, in most cases, consenting to be contacted by every carrier in that site's network.

None of this is illegal. All of it is worth knowing before you pick up the phone.

What's the alternative, and what does independent actually mean?

The term "independent Medicare advisor" or "independent Medicare consultant" gets used loosely. In the broker world, "independent" usually means the broker contracts with multiple carriers rather than being a captive agent for one. That's better than the alternative — but it doesn't remove the commission structure.

A genuinely fee-transparent Medicare consultant charges a flat fee for the consultation itself and earns no commission from any plan you choose. The business model is structurally different: the advisor's income doesn't change based on what you pick, so the advice can go wherever your situation leads — including "wait," "don't switch," or "the plan you already have is fine." That's the advice a commission-paid broker literally cannot afford to give you.

The tradeoff is that you pay out of pocket for the session. Whether that's worth it depends on the complexity of your situation and how much you value advice that has no ceiling on where it can go.

How to apply it

Before your next conversation with any Medicare advisor, ask two questions directly:

"Are you paid a commission if I enroll in a plan through you?" A straightforward yes is honest. Evasion or redirection is information.

"Do you contract with all carriers in my area, or a subset?" This tells you whether the market they're shopping on your behalf is the full market or a filtered version of it.

If the answer to the first question is yes, the advice can still be useful — but treat it as one input, not a verdict. Cross-reference the recommendation against Medicare's Plan Finder tool at Medicare.gov, which is genuinely independent and lets you compare plans by your actual drug list and zip code.

If your situation is complex — you're still working at 65, you have a chronic condition and established specialists, you split time between states, or you're weighing whether to switch from Advantage to Medigap — the commission ceiling matters more. Those are the scenarios where the advice you don't get is as consequential as the advice you do.

Frequently asked questions

Are Medicare brokers required to disclose that they're paid by carriers?

Yes. CMS requires brokers to disclose their compensation structure. In practice, the disclosure often happens in fine print or is mentioned briefly at the end of a call. Asking directly — before the conversation goes anywhere — is the simplest way to get a clear answer.

Can a broker legally recommend a plan that pays them a higher commission?

CMS rules prohibit brokers from recommending a plan "based on the broker's or agent's financial interest rather than the beneficiary's best interest." That's the regulatory standard. But enforcing intent is difficult, and the incentive to favor higher-commission plans exists regardless of what the rules say. Your protection is knowing the structure and asking the right questions.

Is Medicare.gov plan comparison actually neutral?

Medicare.gov's Plan Finder tool is operated by CMS and does not receive carrier commissions. It compares plans by your specific drugs, providers, and zip code. It's the most independent comparison tool available. It doesn't replace personalized advice — it doesn't know your health history, your risk tolerance, or your travel situation — but it's a strong cross-reference for any plan recommendation you receive.

What's the difference between a captive agent and an independent broker?

A captive agent represents a single carrier — they can only sell that carrier's plans. An independent broker contracts with multiple carriers and can shop across them. Both earn carrier commissions. Neither is the same as a fee-only consultant whose compensation doesn't depend on enrollment.

If I got advice from a commission-paid broker and enrolled in a plan, should I be worried?

Not necessarily. Many people are enrolled in plans that genuinely fit their situation, even when a commission-paid broker recommended them. The question to ask is whether your current plan still fits — your providers, your drugs, your out-of-pocket costs in a real year, and your ability to switch if your health changes. Annual Enrollment (October 15–December 7) is the window to revisit that every year.
