Is Your Medicare Advisor Actually Independent?

2026-10-11

The person on the other end of that Medicare call sounds helpful. They ask about your doctors, your prescriptions, your budget. They say the review is completely free. None of that is a lie, exactly — but none of it tells you the one thing you need to know before the conversation goes any further: who is paying them, and what happens to their income depending on what you choose. That question, asked before a single plan option is discussed, is the whole game.

The Invisible Compensation Structure

Most people approaching Medicare assume they will be able to tell when an advisor is structurally tilted toward certain carriers. They imagine bias looks like a pushy sales pitch, a rushed enrollment call, someone who keeps circling back to one plan regardless of what they say. Sometimes it does. But the more effective version of the problem is quieter — a warm, knowledgeable voice who asks good questions and delivers a recommendation that sounds entirely reasonable, while the compensation structure behind it remains completely invisible.

That invisibility is not an accident. The broker model in Medicare is built on carrier-paid commissions, and the amounts are not small. Carriers pay recruiting fees, per-enrollment bonuses, and volume incentives that are legal, common, and almost never explained to the person on the other side of the table. The advisor does not need to lie to you. They just need to not explain the money. And in a system where that explanation is rarely required, most of them do not.

Here is the core problem: when a broker is paid by a carrier to place you in a plan, the recommendation they give you is delivered by someone with a financial outcome attached to it. That is not a character flaw — it is the structure. The broker's income changes depending on what you choose. An independent advisor's income does not. Those are different products dressed in the same language.

The complaint data confirms this is not a fringe issue. CMS documented a more than doubling of Medicare Advantage marketing complaints between 2020 and 2021. The pattern driving those complaints is not agents misrepresenting isolated benefits. It is the architecture: call centers that present as neutral resources, aggregator sites that generate leads and sell them to contracted brokers, directory listings that use the word 'independent' while functioning as referral pipelines for carrier-paid agents. Bipartisan Senate legislation introduced in 2025 — the BROKERS TIME Act — exists specifically because Congress looked at this pattern and decided regulatory pressure was necessary. That is not background noise. It is confirmation that what people experience as a vague sense that something is off has a documented, structural source.

The phrase 'independent Medicare advisor' is not legally protected the way 'fiduciary' carries specific legal weight in financial planning. Anyone can use it. Aggregator sites use it in their domain names while functioning as lead-generation pipelines for contracted brokers. Local agencies use it to mean they are not exclusively tied to one carrier — while still collecting commissions from five. The word has been stretched to cover a wide enough range of arrangements that it is nearly useless as a filter on its own. What it cannot substitute for is a direct, specific answer to the compensation question, asked before the conversation begins.

Four Questions to Ask Before You Discuss a Single Plan

Understanding the structure changes what you are looking for when you vet an advisor. You are not looking for someone who seems trustworthy. You are looking for someone whose compensation structure cannot compromise their recommendation — and you want to verify that structure before the conversation moves to plan options.

There are four concrete things to check.

First, ask whether they hold a National Producer Number and are licensed in your state. You can verify this independently through your state's Department of Insurance website — it takes about two minutes and confirms you are talking to a real licensed professional, not a lead-generation operation.

Second, ask whether they are contracted with specific carriers, and which ones. An advisor who is contracted with only a subset of carriers in your market cannot give you a complete picture of your options, regardless of how they describe their process.

Third, ask whether their compensation changes based on which plan you choose. If the answer is yes — which is the honest answer for a commission-based broker — you now know the frame you are in. That does not automatically disqualify them, but it changes what you should do next: get a second opinion from someone outside that compensation structure before you sign anything.

Fourth, ask what happens to your relationship with them after you enroll. Do they have a process for reviewing your plan before the following Annual Enrollment Period? Will they pick up when you call in March about a formulary change? Or does the relationship effectively end when the enrollment paperwork is submitted?

A genuinely independent advisor will answer the compensation question plainly. They will tell you they charge a fee, or they will tell you exactly what the commission structure looks like and from whom. What they will not do is change the subject, explain that their services are free without accounting for why, or tell you that all their compensation comes from carriers while also claiming to have no bias toward any of them. That last combination — carrier-paid and claiming independence — is exactly what the complaint data has been capturing.

If the answers are clear, specific, and consistent with what you can verify independently, you have the foundation for a conversation worth having. If they are vague, redirected, or replaced with reassurances about how free and helpful the service is — that is the answer too.

What Happens After Enrollment Is the Real Dividing Line

The fourth question — what happens after you enroll — reveals more than people expect, and it is where the difference between a broker and a genuinely independent ongoing advisor becomes most concrete.

The commission in Medicare is collected at enrollment. Once it is collected, the financial incentive that drove the recommendation is gone. What happens next — the mid-year formulary change, the denied claim, the specialist who quietly left the network — happens in the space where the broker has no economic reason to show up. That is not cynicism. It is the math of the commission model.

The commission model does not guarantee bad advice. A knowledgeable, thorough broker who is not steering you toward inferior plans can do competent work within that structure. But the quality of the advice and the independence of the advice are two separate things, and conflating them is how people end up in plans that were fine when they enrolled and genuinely wrong a year later, with no one to call. The distinction matters most not in the initial enrollment — where a good broker can do a solid job — but in the years that follow, when the plan changes and you need someone whose only financial stake in the answer is your continued trust.

What remains genuinely unclear — and what even careful research will not resolve before you talk to someone — is whether a specific advisor, however they describe themselves, has the depth of knowledge to catch the things that matter: formulary tier changes, network narrowing, the interaction between a drug added last spring and what your plan will cover next January. Credentials and compensation structure tell you the frame. They do not tell you the quality of what happens inside it.

That is why the advisor relationship, once you find one whose structure you can trust, is worth maintaining. Not because Medicare is always complicated, but because it becomes complicated at unpredictable moments — a diagnosis, a move, a spouse's retirement — and those are exactly the moments when you do not want to start the vetting process from scratch.

The checklist is not long: license verification, carrier contracting, compensation disclosure, and what happens after enrollment. Four questions, asked before a single plan option is on the table. Ask them directly, listen for specific answers, and verify what you can independently. The advisor who cannot answer them plainly is already telling you something important.
