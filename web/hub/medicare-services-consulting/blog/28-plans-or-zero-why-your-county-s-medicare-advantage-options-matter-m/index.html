28 Plans or Zero: Why Your County's Medicare Advantage Options Matter More Than the National Average

2026-10-23

The national average number of Medicare Advantage plans sounds like good news. It is not a number that describes your decision. Your decision is made in a single county — for your doctors, your drug list, and your income. For 2027, CMS data shows roughly 193 counties have zero Advantage plans available. Not few. Zero. Here is what that gap means, how to find your real local count, and how to run the comparison nobody in the Medicare sales market will run with you.

The news in brief

CMS preliminary data for the 2027 plan year shows that roughly 193 counties across the United States have zero Medicare Advantage plans available — not limited options, zero. At the same time, national news coverage continues to cite average plan counts in the range of 40-plus plans per beneficiary, a figure calculated across the entire country. Those two numbers coexist because Medicare Advantage plans are approved and priced at the county level, not nationally, and the national average conceals a distribution that leaves a meaningful number of beneficiaries with no Advantage path at all.

What happened

According to CMS preliminary Medicare Advantage plan data reported ahead of the 2027 plan year, the number of counties where zero Advantage plans have been approved sits at approximately 193. Carriers make county-level profitability decisions: they enter markets where hospital networks have signed contracts and enrolled populations are large enough to sustain margins. Urban counties with dense hospital systems attract more carriers; rural counties with thinner populations and fewer contracted providers attract fewer, and sometimes none. Two counties in the same state, an hour apart, can have plan counts that differ by a factor of ten. The 193-county figure reflects preliminary data — CMS releases bids in stages, and final approved plan counts can shift before the plan year opens in November.

Why it matters

If you are in one of those 193 counties — or in a county with three or four plans — the national average is not a useful number. It is a fiction that describes a reality you do not have access to.

The thinness of the local market changes how you evaluate every option in front of you. Medicare Advantage plans are network-based. In a rural county with three available plans, those networks may overlap significantly, reducing your real choice further. If the cardiologist or oncologist you already have a relationship with is not in any of the three networks, the plan's low headline premium becomes irrelevant — you either change providers or pay out-of-network rates.

Original Medicare — Parts A and B — is not network-based. It is accepted by approximately 93 percent of non-pediatric physicians in the United States. Paired with a standardized Medigap supplement and a standalone Part D drug plan, it covers you wherever you are, without referrals and without network boundaries. The tradeoff is a higher monthly premium than a $0-premium Advantage plan. But a $0-premium Advantage plan is not free coverage — it carries copays, coinsurance, and an out-of-pocket maximum that can reach several thousand dollars in a year where you actually use it. Which cost structure fits your situation depends on your health profile, your providers, and what your county's Advantage plans actually cost to use, not just to hold.

There is also a structural misalignment in how Medicare is sold that matters most when your local plan count is low. Agents and brokers who enroll you in a Medicare Advantage plan earn a commission set by CMS — several hundred dollars per enrollment, sometimes more for certain plan types. Medigap commissions are structured differently and can be lower. An advisor whose income depends on which plan you choose cannot give you truly unbiased guidance, regardless of intent. CMS complaints about Medicare Advantage marketing more than doubled between 2020 and 2021. The BROKERS TIME Act, introduced in the Senate in 2025, was written specifically to address predatory call center behavior targeting Medicare beneficiaries. When your county has three Advantage plans and the agent you are speaking with earns a commission on all three and nothing meaningful on the Medigap path, that structural misalignment is exactly what produces bad recommendations.

Our perspective

The comparison that actually changes how people think about Medicare is not Advantage Plan A versus Advantage Plan B. It is: what does it cost you to use your Advantage plan in a real year, versus what does Original Medicare plus a Medigap supplement cost you in the same year? Run the numbers for a healthy year. Run them again for a year with a surgery, a hospitalization, or an ongoing specialist relationship. Almost nobody in the Medicare sales market runs that comparison with you, because for half of the paths, they earn nothing.

The way to start without handing your phone number to a lead generator: go to Medicare.gov and use the plan finder tool. Enter your zip code and your Medicare number or eligibility date. What comes back is every plan approved in your county — not the national average, your county. Do that before you talk to any agent, before you call any 800 number, before you fill out any form that asks for your contact information. The moment you submit a lead form on most Medicare comparison sites, your phone number is sold. Fifteen to twenty calls a day is not an exaggeration. Run the plan finder first. What you find there is your actual local market, and that is the number that matters.

For beneficiaries in thin markets: after running the plan finder, check whether your primary care physician, current specialists, and preferred hospital are in-network for any plan that comes back. If a plan fails that filter, it is not a real option unless you are willing to change providers. For plans that pass, pull the Summary of Benefits document — not the brochure — and find the out-of-pocket maximum, specialist copays, and inpatient cost structure. Then get a Medigap quote for Plan G or Plan N, add a Part D drug plan quote from Medicare.gov for your specific drug list, and total the three monthly costs. Compare total cost of care, not just premium. That is the comparison that unlocks which path actually fits your situation.

What happens next

The 193-county zero-plan figure reflects preliminary 2027 data; final approved plan counts are not locked until the plan year opens in November. If your county currently shows one or two plans, check the Medicare.gov plan finder again when the plan year opens — counts can shift in either direction.

Beyond 2027, there is genuine uncertainty about what continued carrier market pullbacks mean at the local level. Several large carriers have exited markets or reduced service areas in recent cycles, citing reimbursement pressure. Whether that trend continues, stabilizes, or reverses depends on federal reimbursement rates set annually and lobbied heavily. If you are 62 or 63 right now, the plan landscape you enroll into at 65 may look different from what exists today.

That uncertainty is not a reason to wait. The Part B and Part D late-enrollment penalties are permanent — they attach to your premium for as long as you are on Medicare. Understanding your local market now, before a deadline forces a rushed decision, is the only way to make the choice with full information. The BROKERS TIME Act remains a bill to watch: if enacted, it would impose new restrictions on the call center marketing practices that have generated the bulk of CMS complaints over the past several years.
