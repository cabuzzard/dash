Medicare Open Enrollment 2026: Three Notices You Must Read Before December 7

2026-10-23

Medicare's open enrollment window is live now and closes December 7, 2026. Most beneficiaries spend this period comparing plans — but the first move isn't plan comparison. It's reading three specific documents your plan already sent you, before anyone calls you with an offer.

The news in brief

Medicare open enrollment runs October 15 through December 7, 2026. Miss the deadline and you are locked into your current plan — premium changes, formulary shifts, and all — for another full year. CMS requires Medicare plans to send current members three formal disclosure documents before the window opens every year. Those documents, not a broker's comparison call, are where the material changes to your coverage are disclosed.

What happened

According to the pillar content produced for this hub, every Medicare Advantage and standalone Part D plan is required to issue three notices to current members before October 15 each year: the Annual Notice of Change (ANOC), due by September 30; the Evidence of Coverage (EOC), the full legal description of plan benefits and cost-sharing; and the monthly Part D Explanation of Benefits (EOB). Together these documents contain every change — premiums, deductibles, drug formulary tiers, prior authorization requirements, and provider network shifts — taking effect January 1. Most beneficiaries discard at least two of them. The pillar reporting identifies that outcome as a structural problem: the plan's disclosure obligation runs to sending the notice, not to the beneficiary's understanding of it.

Why it matters

If you are on Medicare right now, these three documents are the only Medicare materials generated without a sales motive. Brokers and agents are paid by carriers when you enroll — that is a structural fact, not an accusation. Even a multi-carrier broker can only show you plans they are contracted to sell. The ANOC, EOC, and Part D EOB exist outside that incentive structure entirely.

What the documents surface is specific and consequential:

  • Premium and deductible changes. A $0-premium Medicare Advantage plan can become materially more expensive without the premium itself moving — costs shift into deductibles and copays. The ANOC shows you the new numbers.
  • Drug formulary changes. Your medication may have moved to a higher tier, been removed entirely, or gained quantity limits. The ANOC will flag the category of change; you must then verify your specific drugs against the plan's updated formulary before December 7.
  • Network changes. Provider contracts are renegotiated annually. Your cardiologist or rheumatologist may no longer be in-network. The ANOC notes network changes broadly; you have to verify specific providers in the plan's online directory.
  • Prior authorization expansions. Plans can add prior auth requirements to services that previously needed no approval. That change appears in the ANOC as a single line. The EOC spells out the documentation requirements and timeline. Reading it before December 7 means you catch the change while you can still switch plans — not after a denial letter arrives in February.
  • Coverage phase position. Your October Part D EOB shows cumulative out-of-pocket spending against the 2026 phase thresholds. That data directly affects which plan is actually cheaper for your specific drug list — a number the monthly premium alone will not tell you.

The practical threshold for active plan comparison: your total estimated annual drug costs have increased materially under the new formulary; a specialist managing a chronic condition has dropped out of network; or a service you use regularly has had prior authorization requirements added. Any one of those is a reason to open the Plan Finder at Medicare.gov, enter your drugs and providers, and look at total estimated annual cost — not just premium — across available plans.

Our perspective

The enrollment window is designed around disclosure, not guidance. The system's assumption is that you received the ANOC, read it, and made an informed decision. In practice, most people re-enroll by default — silence is treated as re-enrollment for most Medicare Advantage and Part D plans — and find out about material changes only when a claim gets denied or a drug costs twice what it did in December.

That gap is not accidental. There is no commission in teaching you to read your own mail. Reading these three documents yourself, before anyone calls you, is the one move that puts you in the position of a buyer who already knows the product's terms. The December 7 deadline is fixed. After it passes, whatever your plan says is what you are in for 2027.

What happens next

The December 7, 2026 deadline is the only hard date that matters here. Before it arrives:

1. Locate your ANOC (should have arrived by September 30) and check the five line items that change most frequently: monthly premium, deductible, drug formulary tier placements, network status of your regular providers, and prior authorization requirements for ongoing treatments. 2. If the ANOC flags a change category, cross-reference the specific terms in your EOC — not just the summary. 3. Pull your October Part D EOB to see where you stand in the coverage phases and what your year-to-date out-of-pocket spending looks like. 4. If any of the three warning signs above apply, use Medicare.gov's Plan Finder to compare total estimated annual cost — not monthly premium — across plans available in your zip code. 5. If the comparison is close or your situation involves multiple conditions, multiple prescriptions, or a recent change in work status, get a second opinion from someone without a carrier contract before you act.

One important boundary: open enrollment covers existing beneficiaries switching plans. It does not resolve a missed initial enrollment period. If you are newly on Medicare or navigating a late-enrollment situation, the stakes and the applicable rules are different — and some penalties are permanent.
