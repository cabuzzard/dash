Switching From Medicare Advantage to Medigap: What to Know

2026-10-23

You enrolled in Medicare Advantage because the math looked right at the time — low premium, benefits bundled in, easy to say yes. Now something has shifted. Maybe your specialist left the network. Maybe you had a hospital stay and saw what "out-of-pocket maximum" actually means in real dollars. Maybe you're planning to spend half the year in a different state. Whatever moved you, you're asking the same question: can you get out, and can you get into Medigap? The answer is yes — but the path has a gate, and your health history is the key that may or may not fit it.

The short answer

You can switch from Medicare Advantage to Medigap, but in most states, Medigap insurers are allowed to medically underwrite you once your initial Open Enrollment Period has closed. That means they can review your health history, charge you a higher premium, or decline you outright based on pre-existing conditions. If you're healthy, you'll likely qualify. If you've had a significant health event since you first enrolled, you may find the doors narrowed — or closed. A handful of states have year-round guaranteed issue protections, but most do not.

When exactly can you switch — and what windows protect you?

There are two situations where you can move from Medicare Advantage to Medigap without medical underwriting, meaning an insurer cannot turn you down based on health.

The first is your Medigap Open Enrollment Period. When you first enroll in Medicare Part B, you have a six-month window during which every Medigap insurer operating in your state must sell you any plan they offer at standard rates, regardless of your health history. This window opens automatically on the first day of the month you're both 65 and enrolled in Part B. It does not renew. It does not pause. Once it closes, it's gone.

The second is a guaranteed issue right triggered by specific circumstances. Federal law grants guaranteed issue rights — no underwriting, no denial — in a defined set of situations. One of those situations is relevant here: if you enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan for the first time when you turned 65 and you decide within the first 12 months that you want to switch back to Original Medicare and buy a Medigap policy, you have a guaranteed right to do so. This is sometimes called the "trial right." It applies once, in that first year, and it covers Plan A, B, C, F, K, or L — not every Medigap plan letter.

Outside those two windows, you're in open market territory. An insurer in most states can ask about your health, decline your application, or quote you a rated-up premium based on what they find.

What medical underwriting actually looks like

Underwriting is not a formality. Medigap insurers review your medical records and ask detailed health questions on the application — conditions diagnosed, medications prescribed, procedures performed, hospitalizations, pending treatments. Common automatic declines include end-stage renal disease (ESRD), certain heart conditions, recent cancer treatment, and diabetes with complications, though the specific list varies by carrier and state.

If you've been on Medicare Advantage for several years, you've been using your coverage — which means there's a medical history to review. The insurer isn't guessing. They're looking at what actually happened, and they price or decline accordingly. Some carriers will approve you with a higher premium (called a rated policy). Others will simply decline. And once you're declined by one carrier, that decline itself may appear on your record with MIB (Medical Information Bureau), which other carriers can query.

This is why the timing of the original enrollment decision carries so much weight. The choice you make at 65 — and specifically whether you use the Medigap Open Enrollment Period then — has consequences you may not feel for years.

Which states give you more flexibility?

A small number of states have enacted guaranteed issue protections that go beyond the federal floor. New York, Massachusetts, and Connecticut require Medigap insurers to accept applicants year-round regardless of health status. Maine, Vermont, and Minnesota have additional protections as well. If you live in one of these states, the switching calculus looks meaningfully different — underwriting is not a barrier you need to plan around.

If you don't live in one of those states — and most people don't — the federal rules apply, and medical underwriting is the default once your protected windows close.

Before you make any move, confirm the rules in your specific state with a source that is not a carrier or a broker. Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) is a federally funded, free counseling resource with no carrier affiliation. Medicare.gov also maintains current state-by-state information on guaranteed issue rights.

How to apply it

If you're currently on Medicare Advantage and thinking about switching, here's how to think through the decision before you act.

Step one: Identify which window you're in. If you're within your first 12 months on a Medicare Advantage plan and you're already second-guessing the decision, your trial right is still active. Use it. You will not get this window back.

Step two: Check your state's rules. If you're in New York, Massachusetts, Connecticut, or another guaranteed-issue state, you can apply for Medigap at any time without health-based risk. Confirm this with your state's SHIP office before assuming it applies to you.

Step three: If you're outside a protected window and not in a guaranteed-issue state, get a realistic read on your eligibility before you disenroll from your Advantage plan. Talk to an independent Medicare advisor — not a carrier agent — who can walk through your medical history against the underwriting guidelines of specific Medigap carriers. Some carriers are more lenient than others. You want to know where you stand before you pull the trigger on disenrollment, because disenrolling from Advantage doesn't automatically get you into Medigap.

Step four: Time the switch correctly. You can only disenroll from Medicare Advantage during specific periods — the Annual Enrollment Period (October 15–December 7, with coverage starting January 1) or the Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period (January 1–March 31, with coverage starting the first of the following month). Medigap applications can be submitted outside those windows, but your Advantage disenrollment has to happen within them.

Worked example: You're 68, you've been on a Medicare Advantage plan for three years, and you're being referred to an out-of-network specialist your plan won't cover. You want to move to Medigap Plan G. You're in a state with standard underwriting rules. You submit a Medigap application, disclose your hypertension and the cardiac stress test you had last year, and the carrier comes back with a rated premium — 20% higher than the standard rate. You can accept it, shop a different carrier, or stay on Advantage. That's the real decision. Knowing your options before your health changes is always cheaper than learning them after.

Frequently asked questions

If I'm declined for Medigap, do I have any other options?

Yes. If you're declined in a state without guaranteed issue protections, you can remain on Medicare Advantage, or you can return to Original Medicare and manage without a supplement — accepting full exposure to the 20% Medicare coinsurance and hospital cost-sharing. Some states also have high-risk pools or special programs for people who can't qualify for Medigap privately. Your state SHIP office can tell you what's available where you live.

Does switching from Medicare Advantage to Medigap affect my Part D coverage?

Yes. Most Medicare Advantage plans include drug coverage (Part D) bundled in. If you leave Advantage for Original Medicare plus Medigap, you'll need to enroll in a standalone Part D plan. You can do that during the Annual Enrollment Period (October 15–December 7). If you don't enroll in Part D when you first become eligible — or when you leave a qualifying plan — you may face a late enrollment penalty: 1% of the national base premium for every month you were without creditable drug coverage, added to your Part D premium permanently.

Can I be denied Medigap because of a condition I had before I turned 65?

During your Medigap Open Enrollment Period — the six months starting when you're both 65 and enrolled in Part B — no. Insurers cannot deny you or charge you more for pre-existing conditions during that window. Outside that window, in most states, pre-existing conditions are fully reviewable under medical underwriting. The Open Enrollment Period is the only period where a pre-existing condition history is irrelevant.

What is a "trial right" and does it apply to me?

A trial right is a federal guaranteed issue right that allows you to switch from Medicare Advantage back to Original Medicare and purchase a Medigap policy without underwriting, if you're within your first 12 months on the Advantage plan. It applies once — when you first enroll in Medicare Advantage at 65. If you've been on Advantage for longer than 12 months, this right no longer applies to you. There are other guaranteed issue situations, but they're triggered by specific circumstances (plan termination, moving out of service area) rather than personal preference.

Is there any guaranteed issue right that applies after the first 12 months?

Federal law does provide additional guaranteed issue rights in specific situations: your Medicare Advantage plan leaves your service area or stops operating, you move out of the plan's service area, the plan violates its contract, or you lose employer coverage that was supplementing Medicare. These are event-triggered, not calendar-triggered. If none of those apply to your situation, the standard underwriting rules in your state determine your Medigap eligibility.
