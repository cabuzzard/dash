Medicare Advantage vs. Medigap: Which One Actually Fits You

2026-10-23

You've read the mailers. You've watched the TV ads. You've possibly spent an afternoon on Medicare.gov and emerged more confused than when you started. That's not a sign you're bad at research — it's a sign that almost every source you've found was built by someone who earns money when you enroll in a specific plan. The comparison isn't neutral, and the decision isn't simple. But it is knowable. Here's what the choice actually turns on, in plain language, without a carrier logo in the footer.

The short answer

Medicare Advantage replaces Original Medicare with private-carrier coverage — lower monthly premiums, but real costs when you use care, and a network that controls which providers you can see. Medigap supplements Original Medicare — higher monthly premiums, but near-zero costs when you use care and freedom to see any provider who accepts Medicare, anywhere in the country. The right choice depends on your health, your providers, your geography, and your risk tolerance — not on which plan runs the better commercial.

What does each plan actually do to your coverage?

This is the structural difference most comparisons bury in fine print.

Medicare Advantage (Part C) is a substitution. A private carrier takes over your Medicare coverage entirely. It usually bundles Part D drug coverage, manages your care through a contracted network, and may require referrals or prior authorization for specialist visits and procedures. Monthly premiums are often very low — sometimes zero — but your costs when you use care are real: copays, coinsurance, and an annual out-of-pocket maximum. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) caps that maximum at $9,350 for in-network care in 2025, though many plans set theirs lower.

Medigap (Medicare Supplement) is an addition. Original Medicare stays your primary coverage. The Medigap policy pays what Original Medicare doesn't — the 20% coinsurance, hospital deductibles, and in some plans, foreign travel emergencies. Monthly premiums are higher, typically $150–$300 depending on your age, plan letter, and state. But your financial exposure when you use care approaches zero. You can see any provider in the country who accepts Medicare — no referrals, no network restrictions, no prior authorization for procedures your doctor orders.

The structural difference matters more than the premium difference. One plan controls your care; the other doesn't.

How do out-of-pocket costs actually compare across a full year?

Here's the mistake most people make: they treat the monthly premium as the cost of their coverage. It isn't. It's the entry fee. The cost of your coverage is what you pay when you use it.

A healthy year on a $0-premium Advantage plan can absolutely cost less than a year on Medigap Plan G at $220 a month. That math works — until it doesn't. A year with a hospital stay, specialist visits, and an imaging series can push you toward that out-of-pocket maximum quickly. On Medigap Plan G, you pay the Part B deductible ($257 in 2025, per CMS) and almost nothing else, no matter how much care you consume.

Run the break-even math for your own situation. If your Medigap premium is $220 a month, that's $2,640 a year before you use a dollar of care. On a $0 Advantage plan, you'd have to accumulate $2,640 in copays and coinsurance before Medigap starts looking cheaper. For a healthy person in their mid-60s, that may not happen most years. For someone with a chronic condition, active specialist relationships, or anyone who gets hospitalized, the Medigap premium often looks small against what they'd owe out-of-pocket on Advantage.

The comparison that matters is your worst-case year on each path — not your average year.

Which plan fits which situation?

Rather than a generic features table, here are the scenarios that actually push toward one choice or the other.

You want predictability and provider freedom. You've planned for a fixed monthly expense, you want to walk into any hospital or specialist's office in the country without checking a network directory, and you want zero financial surprises after a health event. Medigap Plan G or Plan N fits this profile.

You're healthy, your income is fixed, and you want low monthly costs. Medicare Advantage may serve you well — with conditions. Choose a plan with a low out-of-pocket maximum. Confirm your specific doctors are in-network (not just the hospital — the specific physician). Verify your prescriptions are on the formulary before you enroll. Recheck every year during Annual Enrollment (October 15–December 7), because networks and formularies change annually.

You split time between two states or travel frequently. Medicare Advantage is a serious problem here. Most plans cover out-of-network care only in genuine emergencies. If you spend winters in Florida and summers in Michigan on a Michigan Advantage plan, you are effectively uninsured for non-emergency care in Florida. Original Medicare plus Medigap covers you anywhere in the country that accepts Medicare.

You have a chronic condition and established specialist relationships. Confirm those specialists accept your Advantage plan's network before you enroll. Understand that Advantage plans use prior authorization for many procedures — your cardiologist may be in-network while the imaging facility they refer you to is not. Medigap removes that variable entirely.

You're still working at 65 with employer coverage. This is the secondary payer coordination situation that most agents get wrong. If your employer has 20 or more employees, your employer plan pays first and Medicare pays second — delaying Part B may be an option, and a Special Enrollment Period lets you enroll penalty-free when you leave that coverage. If your employer has fewer than 20 employees, Medicare pays first, and you likely need Part B immediately. Getting this wrong creates permanent premium penalties. This is the scenario where independent medicare enrollment help pays for itself many times over.

Can you switch from Medicare Advantage to Medigap later?

This is the part the TV commercials never mention, and it's the most consequential piece of the entire decision.

When you first turn 65 and enroll in Medicare Part B, you have a six-month Medigap Open Enrollment Period during which insurers cannot deny you coverage or charge you more based on your health. That window closes — and in most states, it doesn't reopen. After it closes, Medigap is medically underwritten, meaning a carrier can decline you or charge you higher premiums based on your health history. If you're on Medicare Advantage, have a significant health event, and then try to move to Medigap a few years later, you may find yourself uninsurable for supplement coverage.

A handful of states — New York, Massachusetts, Connecticut, and a few others — maintain guaranteed-issue protections year-round. Most states do not. The decision you make when you first enroll carries more weight than most people realize, because the ability to course-correct later is not guaranteed.

How to apply it

Before you choose, work through this specific checklist — not a general comparison, but your situation:

1. List your doctors and call their billing offices. Ask whether they're accepting new Medicare Advantage patients on your specific plan. In-network status and accepting-new-patients status are two different things. 2. Run your drug list against each plan's formulary. Use Medicare's Plan Finder at Medicare.gov, or work through it with an advisor. Confirm the tier and cost-sharing for each medication, not just that the drug is covered. 3. Find the out-of-pocket maximum, not just the deductible. The deductible is the floor. The maximum is the ceiling on your worst-case year. Know both numbers before you enroll. 4. Ask about prior authorization requirements for any care you're likely to need — specialist visits, imaging, outpatient procedures. 5. Run a bad-year scenario. If you had a hospital stay and several months of follow-up care, what would you pay on the Advantage plan versus Medigap? That number belongs in your decision. 6. Identify your geographic situation. If you spend significant time in more than one state, confirm whether each plan you're considering covers non-emergency care in both locations.

If every comparison you've run so far started and ended with the monthly premium, you haven't compared plans yet. You've compared marketing materials.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between a Medicare consultant and a Medicare broker?

A broker is licensed to sell insurance plans and is typically paid a commission by the carrier whose plan you enroll in. That commission structure is legal and disclosed, but it creates an incentive shaped by the carrier's interests. A fee-transparent Medicare consultant charges a flat fee for the consultation and earns no commission from any plan you choose — the advice is structurally independent because the business model doesn't depend on what you pick.

Can I have both Medicare Advantage and Medigap at the same time?

No. Medigap is designed to pay the costs that Original Medicare leaves unpaid. If you're enrolled in Medicare Advantage, Original Medicare is no longer your primary payer — so a Medigap policy has nothing to supplement. Carriers are not permitted to sell you a Medigap policy if they know you're enrolled in Medicare Advantage.

I'm turning 65 but still working. Do I have to enroll in Medicare?

Not necessarily. If your employer has 20 or more employees, you can typically delay Part B without penalty and use a Special Enrollment Period when you leave that coverage. If your employer has fewer than 20 employees, Medicare pays first and you likely need Part B immediately to avoid coverage gaps and permanent late enrollment penalties. This is one of the most commonly mishandled situations in Medicare — get specific advice before the deadline passes.

Is Medicare Advantage actually better if I'm in good health right now?

For a healthy person with low healthcare utilization, Medicare Advantage can cost less in a given year — particularly with a low out-of-pocket maximum and confirmed network coverage. The risk is what happens if your health changes after your Medigap Open Enrollment Period closes: in most states, you may no longer qualify for supplement coverage. Healthy people tend to underweight that future-state risk when making the initial decision.

What does a useful Medicare plan comparison actually need to include?

At minimum: your specific prescriptions against each plan's formulary, your specific providers against each plan's network, the annual out-of-pocket maximum, prior authorization requirements for care you're likely to need, and a worst-case cost scenario for a serious illness year. A comparison that starts and ends with the monthly premium is not a comparison — it's marketing.
