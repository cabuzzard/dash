Preconstruction Checklist for Small General Contractors

2026-10-11

Winning a bid when you are already running three jobs comes with a specific kind of dread. The contract is signed, the start date is six weeks out, and somewhere between now and the morning your crew mobilizes, a wall of work has to get done that has nothing to do with building anything. Large firms have preconstruction departments for this. You have Tuesday night.

Where the Margin Goes Before the First Shovel Moves

The preconstruction phase — everything that has to happen between contract execution and mobilization — is where margin gets protected or quietly bled away. When that phase gets compressed, skipped, or handled in fragments, the job starts with invisible risk baked in. Schedule float disappears before the first sub is even confirmed. Scope gaps that could have been caught in a document review surface instead as change order disputes at the worst possible moment.

This is not a complaint about how hard contracting is. It is a description of a structural problem that costs small general contractors real money on nearly every job. The checklist itself is not complicated. What makes it hard is that it requires protected time and dedicated attention at exactly the moment when a contractor has the least of both.

The contract documents have to be indexed first — drawings, specifications, addenda, the owner-contractor agreement itself — organized into a single file structure with version control in place from the start, not retroactively after someone sends the wrong drawing to a sub. That sounds simple. It almost never gets done before mobilization, which means the first two weeks of every job include at least one conversation about which version of the plans is current. The cost of that conversation is usually absorbed silently into the owner's time.

From there, the scope of work for every subcontractor has to be pulled out of the documents and written down — not paraphrased from memory, written down and cross-referenced against the specification sections that govern the work. Each sub's contract needs to match what the documents actually require, not what was discussed at bid time. This is where scope gaps get caught before they become disputes. When a sub's contract is silent on something the drawings show, that silence will eventually cost someone money. The question is whether you catch it in week one or week eight.

Permit status is its own line item here. Knowing the permit is submitted is not the same as knowing when it will be issued. Some jurisdictions move in days; others move in weeks. If your mobilization date is tied to permit issuance and you do not know where you are in the review queue, you are scheduling around a variable you have not actually confirmed. That is a recoverable problem when you catch it three weeks out. It is an expensive problem when you catch it on day one.

The Outputs That Have to Exist Before Day One

Procurement tracking has to start immediately. Long-lead items — structural steel, specialty glazing, custom millwork, mechanical equipment — have lead times that do not compress to fit a schedule that was built without accounting for them. A six-week delivery window does not care that your superintendent assumed four. The procurement tracker is not a sophisticated document. It is a list of every item that has to be ordered before it can be installed, with the order date, the confirmed lead time, and the delivery date mapped against the schedule. If that list does not exist before the job starts, procurement runs on memory and optimism, and one missed order can push a substantial completion date by weeks.

The subcontractor confirmation process has its own sequence. Insurance certificates have to be collected and checked before any sub sets foot on the site. Scope of work agreements have to be signed, not just verbally confirmed. Safety requirements and site rules have to be transmitted in writing. None of this is complicated, but all of it takes time and back-and-forth that does not happen on its own. When the owner is the only person managing it, it happens in gaps — between site visits, after hours, whenever there is a window — which means it happens slowly and sometimes incompletely.

The schedule built during preconstruction is not the document you present at the kickoff meeting. That is the output. The work is pulling apart every trade sequence, identifying the dependencies, mapping durations against the lead times already confirmed in the procurement tracker, and then building a two-week lookahead that is actually executable on day one. The master schedule is a planning document. The lookahead is what the superintendent actually runs against. Both have to exist before mobilization, and the lookahead especially has to be grounded in real confirmed durations — not estimates built on optimism.

The inspection tracker and the drawing log round out the functional minimum. Every required inspection has to be listed, sequenced, and tied to the schedule so that inspection readiness is built into the work sequence rather than called in at the last minute and hoped for. Drawing version control — knowing which revision is current, which subs have it, and what changed from the previous version — is not glamorous work, but it is the kind of work that prevents a sub from installing to a superseded detail and having to tear it out.

The client communication infrastructure also has to be set up before the job starts, not improvised once questions begin arriving. Who sends the weekly update? In what format? How does a client question get routed and answered? If the answer to all three is 'the owner,' that is still a system — it is just a system where every client-facing task runs through one person who is also supposed to be running the field. Setting up even a minimal reporting cadence during preconstruction means the client has a contact point and a rhythm, and the owner is not fielding calls mid-afternoon on a job site because no one has heard anything in twelve days.

The Structural Problem Behind the Checklist

None of this is secret knowledge. Every experienced contractor knows these deliverables exist. The problem is producing them under the constraints of an operation where the principal is already committed elsewhere and there is no dedicated preconstruction function to carry the load. A large GC has an estimating department, a preconstruction manager, and a project engineer whose job for the first several weeks is exactly this output set. A five-person GC has the owner, who has two other jobs closing out and a third that is mid-build and running behind.

The structural answer is a coordination function that can absorb this work without requiring the owner to supervise it at a level of effort that defeats the purpose. That is not a project manager at seventy thousand dollars a year, and it is not a generalist virtual assistant who needs to be taught what a submittal log is. It is a retained, field-literate coordination layer that knows what these deliverables look like, knows why they matter, and can produce them on a live job without a six-month onboarding period.

The work is concrete, not abstract. Build the contract document index. Set up the drawing version log. Draft the procurement tracker. Write the sub scopes. Establish the inspection sequence. Set up the client reporting cadence. These are specific outputs that have to exist before day one — not back-office support in the vague sense, but a defined stack of deliverables that either gets produced in preconstruction or gets paid for in the field at a much higher rate.

The jobs that start clean tend to run clean. Not always — there are owner decisions and site conditions that no amount of preconstruction work will prevent. But a job that mobilizes with organized documents, confirmed subs, a realistic schedule, and a client who has already received their first update starts with a margin it can protect. A job that starts with none of that is already behind, and catching up in the field is the most expensive way to do preconstruction work there is.

The checklist is not the hard part. The hard part is carving out the time and function to actually run it on every job, not just the ones where things happen to go smoothly. Small GCs who solve that problem — not by working more hours but by getting the right coordination layer in place — are the ones who protect margin before a single framing nail goes in.
