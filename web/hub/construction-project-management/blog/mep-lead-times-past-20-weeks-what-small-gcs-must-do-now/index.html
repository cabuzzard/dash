MEP Lead Times Past 20 Weeks: What Small GCs Must Do Now

2026-10-23

Electrical switchgear, air handling units, and specialty mechanical equipment that used to ship in 8–12 weeks are now running 20–30 weeks — and in some cases longer. For small GCs managing 3–10 active jobs without dedicated procurement tracking, that gap is no longer a footnote. It's a critical path item, and it's baked into your schedule before the first shovel hits the ground.

The news in brief

MEP equipment lead times have stretched well past 20 weeks and are not returning to pre-2021 norms. Transformer and switchgear manufacturers are running structural backlogs driven by grid modernization, data center construction, and federal infrastructure spending — all of which pull manufacturing capacity away from the commercial and multi-family work small GCs typically run. A small GC bidding a light commercial or multi-family job is now competing for the same manufacturing slots as large utility and industrial projects that move faster and order in higher volumes.

What happened

Supply chain disruption layered on top of surging construction demand hit electrical and mechanical equipment manufacturers hard between 2021 and 2023. Factory shutdowns, shipping bottlenecks, and raw material shortages created backlogs that most contractors expected to normalize. They haven't — not fully. The backlog has become structural. Lead times that were once a minor scheduling assumption are now a hard constraint on nearly every commercial job. The contractors most exposed are the ones running multiple active jobs with no dedicated procurement tracking, which describes most small GCs operating today.

Why it matters

The math is direct and unforgiving. If you start a commercial tenant improvement or mid-size addition in November 2026 and your electrical panels and HVAC units carry a 22-week lead time, you need approved submittals and purchase orders placed before Christmas to receive equipment by late May. If owner approval of the mechanical design slips to January, the equipment doesn't arrive until July at the earliest — and that's two months of mechanical rough-in and trim pushed, cascading into ceiling close-in, inspections, and certificate of occupancy.

Most schedules don't show this. They show 'HVAC delivery' as a single bar somewhere in the middle of the job. Nobody tracks back from that bar to ask: when does the submittal go out, when does the engineer review it, when does the owner approve the product data, and when does the purchase order get placed? On a 22-week lead time, that submission deadline often lands in preconstruction — before the foundation is poured.

When equipment lands late, most GCs focus on the gap — the four weeks the AHU didn't show up. That's not where the money goes. The money goes in the ripple: trades sitting idle, crews pulled and remobilized, an owner expecting a TCO date that's no longer reachable, and compression costs when you try to buy time back at the end. That compounding effect was priced into the original schedule as zero.

Our perspective

The practical fix isn't software. It's a discipline shift in how you build the schedule from the start — specifically, a long-lead equipment log built during preconstruction, before the schedule is baselined and before you commit to a substantial completion date.

That log is a seven-column spreadsheet: item, sub, vendor, current lead time, submittal due date, PO trigger date, delivery date. It takes two hours to build and thirty minutes a week to maintain. Once it exists, you sequence backward from required delivery dates to find critical submission deadlines, and those deadlines go into the schedule as hard predecessors — not floating administrative tasks.

One discipline point that matters more than most GCs realize: when your mechanical or electrical sub quotes you a lead time, they're often quoting memory — the last job they ran, the last vendor they used, maybe six months ago in a different market. Lead times on transformers, switchgear, and VRF systems are moving fast enough right now that a number from earlier this year may already be wrong. Get a current, written lead time confirmation from the actual supplier before you lock the schedule. One email. It takes the biggest unknown off the list.

The owner conversation has to happen before the job starts, not after. Frame it plainly: 'Before I give you a substantial completion date, I need lead time confirmations on your major MEP equipment. Switchgear and air handling units are running 20–24 weeks from approved submittal right now. That means the mechanical and electrical designs need to be far enough along for submittal prep before we break ground. If they're not, the equipment delivery date moves, and the schedule moves with it.' Most owners don't know this. Document it in a preconstruction summary. It protects you when something slips and the owner assumes it was your execution that caused it.

For an owner-operator running 4–8 active jobs — who is also the estimator, site super, change order writer, and client contact — the procurement tracking system described above is the right system and also the system that never gets built. A fractional PM running your procurement tracking and submittal log doesn't add complexity to your operation. It removes the version that lives in your head and puts it somewhere you can actually see it.

What happens next

The structural drivers of extended lead times — grid modernization spending, data center construction, federal infrastructure programs — are not winding down in the near term. Small GCs who treat 20-plus week lead times as a temporary condition and plan to 'deal with it when it comes up' are building delays into every job they start right now.

The open questions worth watching: whether transformer and switchgear manufacturers add domestic capacity in response to sustained demand, and whether the federal infrastructure pipeline accelerates or slows under current appropriations. Neither answer changes what you need to do today. The submittal deadline that triggers your switchgear order exists whether or not the backlog eventually shortens. Build it into the schedule before you sign the contract, get lead time confirmations in writing from the actual vendor, and have the owner conversation before you commit to a completion date — not after.
