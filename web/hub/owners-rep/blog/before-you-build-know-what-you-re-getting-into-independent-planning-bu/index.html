Before You Build, Know What You're Getting Into-Independent Review

2026-10-23

You have plans, a bid, and a contract draft on your desk. Your architect reviewed the design. Your contractor priced the work. Neither one is hired to tell you where the budget is soft, where the scope is missing, or where a contract clause quietly hands the schedule risk to you. That is not a failure of trust — it is a structural gap built into how every construction project is assembled. The Build Watcher Pre-Construction Project Review closes that gap before a single shovel moves, at the one moment when every finding is still free to fix.

The People Explaining Everything to You Are Also the People Billing You

You are smart. You have done the homework. You have read the bid, asked questions, and chosen a contractor you believe in. And you still cannot see the bottom of the pool.

That is not a failure of diligence. It is the nature of the position you are in. Your architect was hired to protect the design intent. Your contractor was hired to build the project. Neither one is paid to sit at your side of the table and tell you which allowances are too thin, which line items are missing, or which contract clause transfers open-ended cost risk to you in language that is easy to miss if you are not reading for it specifically.

The specific anxiety you are carrying right now — excited about the project, unsettled by the contracts, aware that the people explaining everything to you are also the people billing you — is the correct read of the situation. What you want is not reassurance. You want clarity. The felt difference between *I hope this is right* and *I know what I am walking into.*

Scope Gaps Before Signing Are Negotiations. After Signing, They Are Change Orders.

Every ambiguity that exists in your documents before you sign is an ambiguity your contractor can price later — at a time when you have no leverage and the project cannot stop. The typical change order load on a custom home or major renovation runs 10–20% of the original contract value. On a $500,000 project, that is $50,000 to $100,000 in additions that were avoidable — not because your contractor is dishonest, but because nobody was paid to close the gaps before construction started.

The most common places those gaps live are not obvious. Allowances set at figures that would not survive a real supplier quote. Scope that the drawing implies and the bid does not include. Contract clauses that let the contractor define what counts as an owner-caused delay. Payment schedules that front-load cash to the contractor before the work justifies it. A contingency sized for a project with complete drawings and a tested subcontractor list — not the one actually in front of you.

None of that is visible on the surface of a bid that looks reasonable.

What an Independent Set of Eyes Actually Does

Build Watcher runs a fixed-scope review of everything that exists before construction starts: available plans and specifications, full bid packages, budget assumptions, proposed contract terms, timeline structure, and the decision gaps your team has not flagged.

The output is a written risk register and a prioritized question list — specific to your project and your documents, not a general assessment. Named line items. Specific contract clauses. Allowances tested against current market pricing for the category they cover. The questions framed to get actual answers from your contractor, your architect, and your attorney, rather than deflections.

Delivered as a written report with a scheduled debrief, so you understand what you are looking at and what to do with it.

A Different Position to Be in Before You Commit

Before this review: documents you cannot fully evaluate, a budget you cannot verify, and a contract that was written by or for someone other than you — and the project starts in weeks.

After: a clear risk register, a specific question list, and a defined picture of where your exposure is and where it is not. You are not a construction expert. You are an owner who now knows what to approve, what to push back on, and what to hand to an attorney before you sign. That is a different position to be in.

One Agenda in the Room — Yours

Build Watcher is engaged before construction starts, paid a flat fee confirmed before work begins, and has no ongoing financial relationship with your contractor, your architect, or your project. There is nothing to protect, no retainer to preserve, and no reason to soften a finding.

Every other firm in this space — construction managers, owner's reps, project management consultants — is either hired for the full build on a percentage or retainer fee that runs parallel to contractor billings, or is structurally incentivized to keep the project moving rather than stop it. That is not a criticism. It is the structural condition that makes genuine independence impossible once construction starts. Build Watcher is not that firm. The finding is the finding.

What the Review Covers

The engagement begins with a project intake — you describe the project stage, share available documents, and confirm scope. From there, the review examines:

Plans and specifications against the full bid — what the drawings require versus what the contractor priced, including line items that are missing, vague, or covered by allowances set below current market. Budget assumptions against comparable project costs in your category and market. Proposed contract terms for clauses that transfer schedule risk, cost risk, or dispute resolution to you on terms you did not negotiate. Timeline and milestone structure for gaps that leave you exposed if the project slips. And the decision gaps — questions your team has not answered yet that will cost money the moment construction starts.

Scope, fee, and deliverables are confirmed in writing before work begins. No hourly drift. No open retainer. One project, one review, one deliverable set.

*This feels like an extra cost on an already large budget.* One identified scope gap, one thin allowance caught, one contract clause renegotiated — any single finding will return the fee. The question is not whether the review costs money. It is whether skipping it costs more.

*The project is about to start.* The pre-construction review is only available before construction starts. That is not a scheduling inconvenience — it is the entire point. If the timeline is tight, the review happens now or the leverage is gone.
