What Your Construction Contract Actually Requires on a Pay Application — and How to Check Before You Approve

2026-10-26

Your contractor just sent a pay application. Before you write the check, your signed contract has something to say about whether that application is even complete — and most homeowners never look.

The news in brief

A pay application is not a bill. It is a contractual claim, and most standard construction contracts — including those built on AIA A201 General Conditions language — define exactly what that claim must contain before an owner is obligated to honor it. The gap between what the contract requires and what actually arrives in the owner's inbox is one of the most common places a residential construction project begins to slip out of the owner's control.

What happened

According to the pillar analysis on this topic, every standard construction contract contains a payment section that does more than establish a draw schedule. It defines what a valid payment request looks like: what documentation must accompany it, what certifications the contractor must make, and often what specific format or line-item structure is required. The AIA A201 General Conditions — incorporated by reference into most custom home contracts — requires that a pay application include a schedule of values, a line-by-line breakdown of the contract sum allocated to specific scopes of work, and the contractor's written certification that the work billed has been completed to the extent claimed. That certification carries legal weight, not just administrative weight. Contracts may additionally require conditional lien waivers from subcontractors and suppliers, stored materials documentation, cost breakdowns tied to approved change orders, and a retainage deduction — typically 5% to 10% — withheld from each draw until substantial completion. Most owners have never read the payment section of their own contract. They check the dollar amount against their gut sense of site progress and approve the draw.

Why it matters

For a homeowner managing a custom build or major renovation, an unreviewed pay application is not a minor administrative gap — it is a financial exposure. Billing can happen faster than work. A contractor billing 100% complete on framing before framing inspections are passed, or billing full allowance amounts before fixtures are selected and ordered, is front-loading the draw: pulling money forward against work that has not yet happened. Without a schedule of values to compare against, the owner has no reference point to see it. Lien waivers matter for a separate reason entirely: paying a contractor who has not paid their subcontractors and suppliers leaves the owner exposed to mechanic's liens on the property — even after the draw has been paid. The contract's lien waiver requirement is the structural protection against that outcome. Change orders compound both problems. An approved change order should appear as a discrete line item with its own completion status. An unapproved change order should not appear in the billing at all — but it frequently does, embedded in existing line items or listed with a pending status that implies authorization that does not yet exist.

Our perspective

The practical gap here is not technical — it is structural. Your architect owns the design. Your contractor runs the site. Your lender is protecting the collateral. None of them were hired to protect your contract rights or verify whether the draw you are about to approve maps accurately to actual site progress. That gap is not a failure of any individual; it is a description of what each party was engaged to do. An owner can run a meaningful basic check without professional help: pull the current pay application, the approved schedule of values, and the signed change order log, and trace every line item backward. Every line in the pay application should correspond to a line on the schedule. Every change order appearing in the billing should carry an approval number and written scope. Every lien waiver the contract requires should have arrived with the package. When something does not trace back, the contract — not the owner's personal judgment — provides the documented basis to pause the payment and request the backup before signing. Where independent review adds value is in the interpretive layer: whether a missing lien waiver is a paperwork delay or a sign subcontractors have not been paid; whether a billing percentage that outruns site progress is aggressive cash flow management or a pattern; whether the contract's own lien waiver language is enforceable as written. Those are questions that require someone reading the documents without a financial stake in the outcome of the draw.

What happens next

Most construction contracts include a notice and cure window — often seven to fourteen days — within which the owner must raise any objection to a pay application. Letting that window pass without response can constitute approval by default and waive the right to dispute the draw later. Owners who have already approved draws without running this comparison are not necessarily without recourse, but the leverage diminishes with each subsequent payment. The practical decision point is the next pay application that arrives: is the schedule of values current, do the completion percentages reflect what is visible on-site, are the lien waivers present, and do the change order line items carry written authorization? Those are answerable questions before the check is signed — not after.
