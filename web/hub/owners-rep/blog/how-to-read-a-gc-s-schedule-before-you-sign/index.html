How to Read a GC's Schedule Before You Sign

2026-10-15

A construction schedule handed to you during the proposal phase looks like a planning document. It has columns, dates, and percentage-complete logic. The natural instinct is to read it as evidence that the contractor has thought this through. That instinct is wrong, and it costs owners real money. What you are actually holding is a negotiating artifact — and before you sign, your job is to know the difference.

The Schedule Is Built to Win Your Signature — Not to Reflect Reality

A baseline schedule presented before contract execution is built to close the deal. The optimism baked into it — compressed durations, sequential trades that will somehow never wait on each other, lead times for custom materials that assume every supplier has nothing else going on — that optimism does not belong to the contractor. The moment you execute, it belongs to you.

Every slippage that follows gets framed as a site condition, a weather event, a design change, something your team introduced. The original timeline becomes the standard against which change orders are measured. And the original timeline was never realistic to begin with. That is the problem you are signing.

This is not a claim about bad contractors. Reputable GCs hand owners optimistic schedules because the market rewards optimism. When an owner is choosing between two firms, they pick the shorter timeline and the lower number, and both contractors know it. A realistic schedule — one that prices in float, acknowledges vendor lead times honestly, and accounts for the actual sequencing constraints on a custom project — looks slower and more expensive than one that does not. So the realistic one loses. The optimistic one becomes your contract.

What you are actually reading when you look at a pre-contract schedule is a set of embedded assumptions. Your job, before you sign, is to surface those assumptions and force an answer on each one. Most owners do not have a framework for doing that. Here is one.

Three Assumptions to Pull Apart Before You Execute

Start with procurement. On any custom build or major renovation, long-lead items — windows, doors, structural steel, mechanical equipment, specialty fixtures — will either arrive on time or they will not, and the schedule almost never acknowledges the difference. Ask the GC to show you the lead time they used for every item on the critical path. Then ask for a recent confirmed lead time from an actual supplier for that same item in the current market. If they cannot produce it, the schedule is a fiction. If the confirmed lead time is longer than what the schedule assumes, every downstream activity moves with it — framing, rough-ins, inspections, interior work — and the slippage compounds.

Next, look at trade sequencing. A schedule that shows electrical, plumbing, and HVAC rough-in running simultaneously in the same area of a house is showing you something that cannot happen in the field. Trades fight for space. Inspections gate progress. A framing inspection has to close before insulation begins; insulation has to close before drywall. Ask the GC to walk you through the inspection sequence embedded in the schedule and tell you which inspections are on the critical path. If they cannot do it, the schedule was not built by someone who has managed inspections — it was built by someone who manages presentations.

Then look at weather contingency. On any project with exposed structure, the schedule should carry explicit allowances for weather delay. Ask how many weather days are built in and what months they are distributed across. If the project is starting in October and the schedule carries five weather days between October and February in a climate that routinely delivers forty, the contingency is cosmetic. It is there to satisfy a contract requirement, not to reflect reality. Those missing days do not disappear — they transfer to you as justified delay claims that extend your timeline, shift your occupancy date, and give the GC cover to submit additional time-related cost requests.

Finally, read the delay classification language in your contract. The schedule should define — and the contract should reinforce — which categories of delay entitle the contractor to additional time only, versus additional time and additional money. Owner-requested changes typically trigger both. Unforeseeable field conditions may trigger both. Weather may trigger time only, or may trigger nothing if the contingency was already embedded in the schedule. If the contract is silent on this, every slippage becomes a negotiation. The contractor enters that negotiation holding the documents. You enter it with a gut feeling. That asymmetry is not accidental — it is what the original document set produced, and it was available to be named and resolved before you signed.

The Window to Fix This Closes When You Sign

The adversarial read — the one that pulls procurement assumptions apart, maps the inspection sequence against the critical path, tests weather contingency against actual climate data, and checks delay classification language against your contract — has to happen before you execute. After execution, a bad schedule assumption is a project management problem. Before execution, it is a negotiation point, and you still have leverage.

This is the specific problem that pre-contract document review exists to solve, and it is a problem the market systematically ignores. Every construction management firm you will find if you search for construction management services is hired after execution — retained to manage an active project whose foundational documents are already locked. They are excellent at what they do, and what they do begins too late to catch the schedule assumptions that are already embedded in your contract.

Your architect was not paid to flag this. Your attorney read for legal enforceability, not construction-specific financial exposure. Nobody caught it because nobody had that job. The question is whether anyone on your team was positioned to find it before you signed — and if the answer is no, you absorbed the risk without knowing you had agreed to it.

The window is narrow. It closes the moment you execute, and the optimism that was the contractor's to manage becomes the exposure that is yours to absorb. Name it first, while you can still do something about it.

What remains genuinely uncertain is the one thing no document review can fully resolve: whether the GC you are hiring has the field capacity and subcontractor relationships to execute the schedule even if the schedule itself is sound on paper. A realistic timeline built by an honest contractor with stretched crews is still a problem. But a compressed timeline built by anyone is a guaranteed one. Start with the document. The field reality comes next — and you are in a far better position to evaluate it once you know what the document actually promises.
