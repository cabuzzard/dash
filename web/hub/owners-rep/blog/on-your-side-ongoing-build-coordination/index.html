Your Project. Your Money. Someone Watching Out for You.

2026-10-22

You hired an architect. You hired a general contractor. You are still the least-informed person in every meeting about your own project.

That is not a coincidence — it is a structure. Your architect is hired to protect the design. Your contractor is hired to manage the build. Neither one is hired to protect your budget, question a pay application, or tell you a change order doesn't hold up. The gap between what your design team covers and what your contractor is accountable for is larger than almost anyone tells you going in. That gap is where money moves, scope drifts, and decisions get made without a written record.

Build Watcher installs an independent coordination layer on the owner's side, before the first draw arrives, with a scope and fee defined in writing before work begins.

You Approved It Because You Felt You Had To

The change order landed on a jobsite. The GC needed an answer. You didn't have the contract language, the site history, or the time to push back — so you signed. And you have not been sure since whether it was legitimate.

That is how budget overruns happen in residential construction. Not in one catastrophic moment. In four quiet, repeating patterns: change orders that are legitimate but overpriced; change orders that are not legitimate at all; pay applications billed ahead of documented progress; and scope creep that accumulates through verbal approvals with no written log. Every one of those depends on the owner not having an independent record to push back with.

What you want is not to become a construction expert. You want someone with no stake in the contractor's outcome who has read the pay application, tracked the schedule, and will tell you the truth before you sign.

What Changes When Someone Is Actually on Your Side

When independent coordination is in place before the first draw, the dynamic shifts entirely. Every pay application is reviewed against documented site progress before you approve it. Every scope change is logged with cost and schedule impact before you are asked to sign anything. Every decision that changes the budget or the timeline gets written down, with context — who requested it, what it costs, what it changes.

The paper trail that feels like bureaucracy is the thing that makes your approval mean something and your pushback hold up. Without it, you are the owner on paper and a bystander in practice.

After: you know what you approved, why you approved it, and what it changed — every time. Change orders get challenged before you sign them, not after they are built into the next draw. Pay applications reflect actual documented progress, not the GC's billing schedule. You stop being the least-informed person in every conversation about your own project.

What You Get, and What It Actually Does for You

Written scope before work begins. Deliverables, site attendance frequency, reporting cadence, and fee structure are defined and signed before the first review. No ambiguity about what is covered — for either side.

Pay application review. Every draw is reviewed against documented progress before you touch it. If what is billed does not match what is built, you find out before you approve it, not after.

Change-order and decision log. Every scope change is logged with cost and schedule impact before you are asked to approve anything. Nothing is verbal-only. Nothing disappears into someone's email thread.

Budget and milestone tracking. A live record of approved changes against the original baseline — so overruns surface in real time, not at closeout when there is nothing left to contest.

Written reporting to the owner. Regular progress reports, open-decision logs, and structured contractor communication — not phone calls that leave no record, not emails copied to seven people with no follow-through.

Closeout coordination. Lien waiver collection, punch-list tracking, and a documented record of the project that belongs to you, not the GC.

Engagement depth is set in writing at the start. Lighter engagements cover periodic draw review and decision-log support. More involved coordination includes active budget and milestone tracking, change-order review, contractor communication management, and regular written owner reports. Scope can be adjusted as the project demands it.

How It Starts

The starting point is a scoping conversation — not a canned service pitch, but a specific conversation about your project's actual gaps: size, current stage, what coordination is missing, and what level of involvement makes sense. From there, a written scope and fee agreement is signed before any work begins.

A baseline budget and schedule review comes next — establishing the written record that every future draw and change order gets measured against. Then regular site attendance as scoped, draw review before every approval, change-order logging before every signature, and written reporting on a defined cadence. The engagement runs through closeout or any defined phase.

The One Thing That Makes This Structurally Different

Every construction management firm with a financial relationship to the contractor's workflow — or a fee tied to a percentage of project cost — has a structure that makes silence cheaper than confrontation. More cost means more fee. A working relationship with the GC means friction has a price.

Build Watcher represents only the owner, bills flat or by phase, and has no financial relationship with the GC, subs, or material suppliers on the project. That is not a positioning claim. It is the only arrangement in residential construction where the advisor's interest and the owner's interest are structurally the same. There is no relationship to protect, no working arrangement to preserve, and no financial reason to stay quiet about something you need to know.

The Objections Worth Answering

"My GC is reputable — I probably don't need this." Reputation is real and it matters. It is not the same thing as alignment. A reputable GC still has a financial interest in approved draws and minimal friction on change orders — that is the structure of the relationship, not a reflection of character. Independent coordination is not a check on honesty. It is a check on structure, and that structure needs an independent reviewer regardless of who the GC is.

"My architect is already watching the project." Most residential architect-of-record agreements explicitly exclude budget management, contract administration, and contractor oversight in any substantive form. Architects are hired to protect design intent. The gap between what they cover and what the contractor is accountable for is exactly where this engagement operates.

"I'm already mid-project — is it too late?" It is almost never too late. Earlier is better — a baseline established before the first draw creates leverage that cannot be created retroactively. But starting mid-project is materially better than finishing without any independent record of how money was spent and decisions were made.

"This will create conflict on my jobsite." Defined communication structure reduces conflict; it does not create it. Most disputes in residential construction happen because nothing was written down. A documented change-order log and a clear reporting cadence give everyone on the project less to argue about, not more.

One Agenda in the Room: Yours

Engagements are flat-fee or structured by phase — not a percentage of construction cost, not open-ended hourly. The scope, fee, and deliverables are defined and signed before work begins. What that looks like depends on your project's size, stage, and gaps — that is what the initial conversation is for.
