What a Contractor's Cross-Claim Can Do to a Payment Dispute — and Why Your Contract's Dispute Language Matters Before You Withhold a Draw

2026-10-27

When a homeowner withholds a draw, most assume the standoff is simple: you stop paying, they push back, somebody blinks. A 2026 UK High Court decision involving a contested payment application and a contractor cross-claim shows exactly why that assumption is expensive — and why the contract language you never read closely is the document that decides everything.

The news in brief

A 2026 UK High Court decision examined what happens when a contractor does not simply defend a disputed payment demand — they file a competing financial claim against the owner at the same time. The case turned on whether the owner had followed the contract's payment notification requirements strictly enough to preserve the right to offset the contractor's claim. The decision does not apply to US residential construction law, but the financial structure it exposed is directly relevant to any homeowner managing a contested draw.

What happened

As reported through the pillar analysis sourced from the case, the dispute centered on a certified payment application and a cross-claim filed by the contractor against the employer — the UK contract term for the owner equivalent. The court examined whether the employer could use a cross-claim to reduce the sum owed under that certified payment, and whether the contract's payment notification requirements had been followed in the correct form and within the required window before the offset could be exercised. UK construction law operates under its own payment legislation framework, distinct from US state law. The structural question the case raised, however, is not jurisdiction-specific: does your contract give you a documented, procedurally compliant right to offset a disputed amount against a payment you otherwise owe — and what do you have to put in writing before you act on that right?

Why it matters

Most US homeowners managing a custom build or major renovation have never read the withholding, set-off, or dispute resolution sections of their contract closely enough to answer three basic questions before stopping a check: Does the contract give an explicit right to offset a disputed amount, or does it require payment in full with separate recovery? What written notice is required — in what form, within what window — for a withholding to be procedurally valid? And if the contractor files a cross-claim, what does the contract say about how competing claims are sequenced?

The answers matter because withholding a draw without documented, contract-compliant notice does not automatically protect you. AIA A101/A201 structures — among the most common in residential custom construction — include specific notice requirements that must be satisfied within a defined window after receiving the pay application. Miss that window, or fail to specify the correct amount with correct documentation, and you may forfeit your procedural right to withhold even if the underlying complaint is completely legitimate. Contractor-drafted agreements, which are more common in residential work than most owners realize, frequently go further — restricting offset rights, requiring disputes to be arbitrated before payment can be reduced, or defining payment as earned upon substantial completion of a line item rather than upon owner verification.

The cross-claim is where a simple payment dispute becomes an expensive one. If you withhold $15,000 for a documented defect and the contractor cross-claims for $30,000 in alleged owner-caused delay costs, the total disputed amount on both sides of the table now exceeds your original withholding. You are no longer the aggrieved party asking for accountability. You are a defendant in a competing financial dispute, and the contract language you never closely read is the document every argument runs through.

Our perspective

The UK case is useful precisely because it is not a US case. It lets us look at the financial mechanics of a payment standoff without the jurisdictional noise, and what it shows is a pattern that appears in contested residential projects everywhere: owners act on instinct — they stop the check because something is wrong — without first confirming that their contract gives them the procedural right to do exactly that, in exactly that way, at exactly that moment.

The notice window is the most commonly missed piece. Most standard construction agreements give you seven to fourteen days from receiving a pay application to issue formal written notice of a disputed amount — not a text message, not a comment at a site meeting, but a written notice identifying the basis, the dollar amount, and often the contractual provision being invoked. After that window closes, the payment is frequently treated as undisputed. Not because the contractor earned it. Because you did not object in writing within the time the contract required.

The dispute resolution sequence compounds this. If your contract requires mediation before arbitration, and requires work to continue while the dispute is pending, you may be obligated to keep partial payments flowing while the cross-claim works its way through whatever process the contract mandates. That sequencing shapes leverage. Understanding it before you withhold money — not after — is what separates a contractually valid withholding strategy from one that hands the contractor a procedural advantage before the dispute has even been named.

A written contract review before you act is the cheapest version of this problem. Getting those questions answered after you have already withheld a draw, after the contractor has filed a cross-claim, and after your notice window has closed is the expensive version. The contract did not hide the deadline. It was in the agreement you both signed.

What happens next

For homeowners currently managing a contested draw or approaching a payment they are unsure about, the immediate open questions are procedural: Has the notice window on the current pay application already closed? Does the contract require mediation or arbitration before any offset can be exercised? Has a formal written defect notice — with a cost estimate attached — been issued, or only a verbal complaint? If the contractor has already responded with a cross-claim or delay allegation, what does the contract say about the sequencing of those competing claims? None of those questions can be answered from memory. They require reading the specific agreement that governs this specific project, before the next payment decision is made.
