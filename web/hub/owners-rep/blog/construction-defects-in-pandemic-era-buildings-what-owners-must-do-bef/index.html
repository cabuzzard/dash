Construction Defects in Pandemic-Era Buildings: What Owners Must Do Before Their Legal Window Closes

2026-09-03

The buildings that broke ground in 2020, 2021, and 2022 were constructed under conditions that had no modern precedent — fractured supply chains, thinly spread labor, abbreviated inspections, and owners pressured into decisions they didn't fully understand. Those buildings are now three, four, and five years old, and the defects baked into them during that period are starting to show. What follows is what every owner of a pandemic-era build needs to understand before their window to act closes.

Why Pandemic-Era Construction Created a Defect Crisis That's Only Now Surfacing

The construction boom of 2020 through 2022 shouldn't have been possible during a global pandemic, but it was — and it produced buildings whose underlying problems were disguised, for a time, by the sheer pace of everything. Supply chains were fractured. Lumber arrived late or not at all, so contractors substituted. Inspections were postponed, abbreviated, or waived under emergency provisions that varied county by county. Project meetings moved to video calls, and the oversight that happens naturally when experienced people are physically present — noticing the wrong product on the pallet, questioning a field decision before it's buried behind drywall — happened less, or not at all.

The mechanism by which these conditions are becoming legal disputes is worth understanding precisely, because it directly determines what an owner can and cannot prove now. When a general contractor substitutes a material — say, switching to an off-spec weather-resistant barrier because the specified product had a sixteen-week lead time — the substitution is not automatically a defect. Most construction contracts allow substitutions with owner approval. The problem is what happened to that approval process during 2020 and 2021.

RFIs and submittals were processed faster and less carefully than they would have been under normal conditions. Some substitutions were disclosed in the paper trail; many were not. Some were approved orally and never reduced to writing. And some were never disclosed at all, because a subcontractor made a field decision and the general contractor either didn't know or didn't ask. When that undisclosed substitution is now at the center of a moisture intrusion problem or a structural concern, the contractor's first legal defense is almost always that the owner approved it. If there is no documentation proving otherwise — no written objection, no independent inspection report contemporaneous with construction, no third-party observer who can testify to what was actually installed and when — the owner is fighting uphill from the start.

The Clock Is Already Running: Statutes of Limitation, Repose, and the Warranty Trap

Construction defect claims are governed by statutes of limitation and repose that can cut off an owner's right to sue regardless of when the damage becomes visible. In most states, the clock starts running at substantial completion. Owners who finished pandemic-era builds in 2021 or 2022 may already be inside the final years of their legal window — and many don't know it.

The relationship between a warranty claim and a legal claim under a statute of repose is not always intuitive, and it is where owners most commonly lose ground without realizing it. In some jurisdictions, pursuing warranty remedies can toll the legal deadline; in others, it does not. An owner who spends eighteen months going back and forth with a contractor under warranty provisions and then hires a lawyer may discover that the legal window has narrowed or closed in the meantime. This is not a situation where moving slowly is safe.

The distinction between a warranty claim and a legal claim matters in another way as well: failing to pursue warranty remedies aggressively and in writing can undermine a later legal position. It allows the contractor to argue that the owner accepted the work. A warranty is not a guarantee of recovery. Most residential and commercial construction warranties are written by the contractor and interpreted narrowly. A one-year workmanship warranty does not automatically cover a moisture failure that results from a combination of an improper substrate and a substituted membrane — the contractor will argue the membrane meets general industry standards, and that argument is not frivolous.

Getting a warranty claim to result in actual remediation typically requires a written demand that identifies specific failures tied to specific contract provisions or specified materials. That is much harder to do without construction records. Owners who are now surfacing these issues need to understand that the sequence in which they pursue remedies — and the speed at which they do it — has legal consequences that compound over time.

The Construction Triangle No One Explained to You

Owners hire an architect and a contractor and assume that between the two of them, someone is watching out for the owner's money and the owner's building. Neither party is — at least not in the way owners typically assume.

The architect's professional obligation runs to the design, to code compliance, and to the client relationship. It does not extend to monitoring every material that arrives on site, and it does not include generating the kind of contemporaneous documentation that protects an owner in a defect dispute. Architects routinely disclaim responsibility for means and methods of construction and for contractor substitutions made in the field. When improper flashing or an off-spec membrane is now at the center of a claim, the architect points to the drawings and specifications as proof that something other than what was specified was installed — which is true, but it doesn't solve the owner's problem.

The general contractor's obligation runs to the contract scope, within which there is significant room for the GC to manage its own margin. The result is that no one in the room during a typical construction project is specifically tasked with documenting what was approved, what was substituted, what was inspected, and what was waived — on behalf of the owner, as a contemporaneous record the owner actually controls.

Owners who had independent representation during their pandemic-era builds are now in a fundamentally different evidentiary position than those who didn't. An independent owner's representative generates a paper trail throughout construction as a normal function of the job: site visit reports, material approval logs, RFI tracking, change order analysis. These documents are not glamorous. They are, however, exactly what determines whether an owner can reconstruct what happened inside a building during construction when a dispute surfaces three years later. Owners who didn't have independent representation are now learning what it would have cost, relative to what they are spending to pursue claims without it.

Forensic Investigation Is Not a Home Inspection — And the Difference Is Decisive

For owners currently sitting with a completed building they suspect has defects, the first practical step is understanding what kind of investigation the situation actually requires. A home inspection and a forensic building investigation are not the same thing, and conflating them is a mistake that can cost an owner their ability to establish causation in a legal or insurance claim.

A home inspection is a surface-level visual assessment. It will identify visible signs of moisture, obvious installation errors, and systems that aren't functioning — but it will not explain why those conditions exist or establish that they resulted from a specific deviation from the contract documents or from industry standard practice. That distinction matters enormously. The difference between 'this building has water in the walls' and 'this building has water in the walls because the flashing at the window heads was installed in a manner inconsistent with the specified detail and inconsistent with industry standard practice' is the difference between a suspicion and a claim.

A forensic building investigation typically involves opening assemblies to expose what cannot be seen from the surface, testing envelope penetrations, engaging licensed engineers to assess structural or mechanical systems, and generating a documented report that can support legal proceedings or an insurance submission. The cost of this work is real, but it is often the only way to produce the causation evidence a defect claim requires.

Owners who proceed without forensic documentation — relying instead on visible damage alone — give contractors and their insurers the ability to dispute causation indefinitely. The contractor's position will almost always be that the damage resulted from owner maintenance failures, weather events, or deferred repairs rather than construction defects. Forensic documentation, generated by qualified professionals and tied specifically to the contract documents and specified materials, is what defeats that argument. It is also what triggers the conversation with a construction defect attorney on terms that give the owner actual leverage.

What Owners Can Do Now, Before the Window Closes

The aggregate scale of the pandemic-era defect problem remains uncertain. Defect claims take time to develop, longer to litigate, and longer still to appear in data that allows for broad conclusions. The pattern is visible in the claims being filed and in conversations among construction attorneys — but it is not yet possible to say precisely how many pandemic-era buildings have latent defects, how many owners have already been time-barred before they understood their situation, or how courts will ultimately treat the novel circumstances of the period: the emergency waivers, the supply chain documentation gaps, the accelerated schedules.

What is possible to say is this: the contractors and architects who built pandemic-era buildings are not waiting to prepare their defenses. Owners who are waiting for certainty before they act are making a choice with real consequences.

For owners who completed construction between 2020 and 2023, a few concrete steps are worth taking now regardless of whether visible problems have surfaced. Pull whatever construction documentation exists — RFIs, submittals, change orders, meeting minutes, correspondence — and organize it. Identify what's missing, because gaps in the record are themselves informative. If visible defects exist, engage a forensic investigator rather than a general contractor to assess them — and do it before making any repairs that would eliminate evidence. Send warranty claims in writing, with specificity, and retain records of how and when they were sent and received. Consult a construction defect attorney to understand the specific statutes of limitation and repose that apply in your jurisdiction, because those deadlines are not uniform and they are not forgiving.

The buildings with problems exist. The window to address them is finite. And every month spent hoping the problem resolves itself is a month the legal clock does not stop counting.

Pandemic-era construction produced buildings under conditions that systematically eroded the documentation, oversight, and accountability that owners depend on when defects surface years later. The legal framework governing those claims does not accommodate owners who move slowly or act without the right evidence. If you own a building completed between 2020 and 2023 and you have not yet assessed your exposure, the time to do that is now — not when the damage becomes undeniable, and not after the window has closed.
