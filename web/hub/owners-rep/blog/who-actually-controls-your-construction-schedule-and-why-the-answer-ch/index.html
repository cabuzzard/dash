Who Actually Controls Your Construction Schedule — and Why the Answer Changes Everything About Your Project Risk

2026-10-26

Your contractor gave you a completion date. What they may not have told you is that the date was handed to them — not set by them. On a large share of custom builds, financed projects, and phased developments, the schedule your GC committed to was determined upstream by a lender, a developer, or a prime contractor. That distinction is invisible in most residential contracts. And it is the first thing that gets obscured when the timeline starts to drift.

The news in brief

On a significant share of custom builds, major renovations, and owner-builder projects, the general contractor does not set the construction schedule — they work inside one. The schedule is handed down before ground is ever broken, often by a developer, a lender, or a prime contractor sitting upstream. That distinction is almost never explained at signing, and it determines everything about who can actually be held accountable when a timeline drifts.

What happened

According to the project documentation reviewed for this piece, a standard residential construction contract names a project start date, a substantial completion date, and a set of conditions that allow the contractor to extend the schedule. What that contract does not name is where those milestone dates came from.

On projects financed through a construction loan, the lender's draw schedule determines when inspections happen and when funds release. If framing is not complete before the next inspection window, the draw does not come — and if the draw does not come, the GC cannot pay their subcontractors. That sequence is not set by your contractor. Your contractor is managing inside it.

On developments where a single prime contractor oversees multiple properties or phases, the schedule is set at the prime level. The builder on your specific unit is effectively a subcontractor in that arrangement, whether or not the word 'subcontractor' ever appears in anything the owner signed. They control the craftsmen. They do not control the master calendar.

The schedule extension provisions in most residential contracts are written to protect the contractor — covering weather, material delays, scope changes, and acts of God. What almost no residential contract includes is a provision naming the upstream schedule authority, confirming the contractor's independence from it, or giving the owner any mechanism to verify that the schedule committed to was actually theirs to give.

Why it matters

When a timeline slips and the contractor controls the schedule, the accountability chain is direct: there is a contract, a completion date, and one party responsible for missing it. When the schedule was set upstream, that chain breaks.

The contractor surfaces with a reason — a delayed inspection, a material substitution requiring lender approval, a sequencing issue from the phase ahead. The reason is real. The delay is still real. And the party who determined the original schedule is not in the owner's contract at all.

You cannot hold a developer accountable for your timeline if you have no contract with the developer. You cannot challenge a lender's inspection schedule if your contract does not name it as a constraint. What you can do is argue with your GC, who is also constrained by a system they did not design, and get answers that are technically accurate and practically useless.

The problem compounds at the change order stage. When a contractor is working inside a schedule they did not set, any upstream shift — a phase delay, an inspection failure, an unanticipated lender condition — arrives at the owner's desk as a change order with a dollar amount and a vague explanation. The owner is being asked to approve a cost that originated in a system they were never shown. That is not always dishonest. It is often structural. But 'genuine cost' and 'cost you should approve without documentation' are different things.

Our perspective

The accountability gap described here is not a contractor character problem — it is a contract structure problem. Most residential contracts are drafted to name outcomes (milestone dates, completion dates) without disclosing the upstream constraints that produced those outcomes. Owners read a completion date and assume their contractor controls it. In a significant number of projects, that assumption is wrong, and no one is obligated to correct it.

The practical implication: the questions worth asking are not about what went wrong after the fact. They are structural questions worth asking before signing — or before approving the next draw.

Who set the milestone dates? A contractor who controls their own schedule can show you how they built it. One working inside someone else's generally cannot. What triggers a schedule extension? Extension clauses that reference 'lender inspection scheduling,' 'prime contractor sequencing,' or 'development phase completion' name the upstream authority explicitly. Is there a liquidated damages clause? Its presence means the contractor has real skin in the schedule. Its absence — common in residential contracts — means a late completion costs them nothing except a difficult conversation. Who are the other parties with authority over this project timeline? On a project with a construction lender, a developer, or a prime, you should know who they are before you are in the middle of a delay trying to figure it out.

For owners mid-project, the same logic applies to the explanation already received. If the delay was caused by a lender inspection window or a development sequencing issue, that should be documentable. A verbal account with no paper trail behind it is not the same as a supported delay claim.

An independent document review — not a site inspection, not a new construction manager on retainer — reads what already exists: the schedule language, the extension clauses, the change order backup, the pay application line items, the communication record. It names what is missing and flags what does not connect. It identifies whether the contractor's delay explanation is supported by the contract terms being cited, or whether it is a verbal account with no documentation behind it. If the review finds nothing wrong, that is a finding worth having. If it finds a discrepancy — a change order billed ahead of work completed, a schedule extension claimed without a qualifying event in the contract, a pay application line item with no approved scope behind it — the owner knows before signing the next check, not after.

What happens next

The open question in most delayed projects is not whether the contractor's explanation is true. It often is. The question is whether the documents support it — and whether the accountability structure in the contract gives the owner any real leverage over the party who actually controls the calendar.

For owners in pre-construction: the time to surface the upstream schedule authority is before signing, not after ground is broken. Ask to see the document the contractor's milestones were built from. Read the extension clauses for language that names lender or developer schedules. Confirm whether a liquidated damages clause exists.

For owners mid-project: pull the paper. The contract, the change orders, the pay applications, and the email record where the answers started getting less specific are already in your possession. What most owners lack is someone to read those documents against what a properly structured project actually requires — without a financial stake in the outcome. That review is the one step that converts a verbal explanation into a documented finding — or flags the discrepancy before the next check is signed.
