Construction Project Health Check—Independent Review

2026-10-23

You signed a contract. The bid made sense. Then the change orders started arriving, the schedule explanations got vague, and the number on the current projection stopped matching anything you originally agreed to. You are not a developer. You do not have a construction department. And neither your architect nor your contractor is hired to tell you when something is wrong on your side of the table. That is the gap Build Watcher fills — one fixed-scope, owner-side document review, delivered as a written summary with a prioritized list of exactly what to address next.

The feeling that something does not add up is usually right

The warning signs are not dramatic. Most homeowners do not wake up one morning knowing they have been overcharged. The feeling is subtler — a low-grade awareness that the numbers are drifting, combined with the frustration of not having the vocabulary to name it.

You signed a contract and a scope. Then a change order arrived — the first one felt reasonable. Now there are four, and the explanations do not map back to anything you remember agreeing to. Or the draw schedule said framing would be done by week six, and it is week nine, and the answers you are getting are vague. Or the original bid looked like one number and the current projection, assembled from a dozen separate line items and change orders, looks like a different one entirely, and no one has sat down and shown you why.

That gap between what you were told and what you can verify is exactly where an independent review works. The gut feeling is usually right. The problem is not instinct — it is documentation. A health check turns the feeling into findings.

No one at the table is working for you

Your architect protects the design. Your general contractor runs the site. Nobody in that arrangement is hired to protect your budget, verify your pay applications, or flag a change order that does not match the original scope. That is not a failure of your architect or your GC — it is just not their assignment.

Every construction management firm in your search results has a financial relationship with the build. They bill by the project, they work alongside the GC, and they have no economic reason to challenge a padded change order or reject a questionable draw. Build Watcher has one client in the room: you. The fee is fixed and the scope is fixed, which means there is nothing to gain from keeping quiet about a problem. That is not a feature — it is the only structure in which an honest review is possible.

What you have after a review

You know exactly where your money went and whether the next payment request is justified. You have a written record of every discrepancy — usable in a contractor conversation, a mediation, or as the factual foundation legal advice requires. You stop approving documents you do not understand and start making decisions from a position of actual knowledge. You get a prioritized action list so you are not paralyzed deciding what to tackle first. And you have an independent professional opinion that is not connected to your contractor, your architect, or your lender.

The project does not fix itself. But you are no longer operating blind.

What the review actually covers

Build Watcher reads the documents you already have — contract, bid, scope of work, pay applications, change orders, schedule, and relevant project communications — and maps what is present against what should be present.

The contract. Most residential construction contracts are missing or vague on lien waiver requirements, change order approval procedures, schedule milestones, and dispute resolution language. A contract that is silent on these items is not neutral — it is a gap that benefits whoever holds the pen when a dispute arises.

The bid and scope of work. The bid you accepted and the scope attached to it are the baseline. Every change order and every draw gets measured against that baseline. If the scope was loosely written — allowances undefined, exclusions buried — those are findings, not accidents.

Pay applications. A pay application is a billing request. Like any invoice, it can be submitted for work not yet completed, for materials not yet on site, or at a markup that was not disclosed in the original contract. Reviewing it means checking what was billed against what the schedule and available documentation say was actually done.

Change orders. Each one gets read against the original scope. Is this work that was always required and should have been in the base bid? Is the markup rate consistent with the contract? Was it approved in writing before the work started?

Schedule. Stated milestones are compared against actual progress, and delays are assessed against what the contract defines as excusable versus what represents a breach.

Project communications. Emails and texts are often where scope decisions and verbal approvals live. They are also where a pattern of evasion becomes visible.

What comes out of the review is a written summary — not a legal opinion, not a site inspection report — that names every discrepancy, flags every missing document, and closes with a prioritized list of the specific things to address before you approve another payment or sign another change order.

Starting is simpler than you think

No site visit is required to begin. Submit the documents you already have. If your files are not fully organized, that is not a reason to wait — a missing document is itself a finding. A contract with no signed change order procedure, pay applications with no supporting schedule of values, or absent lien waivers from paid subcontractors: those gaps are part of the deliverable.

One fixed scope. One deliverable. One fee, published before you commit — no hourly billing, no open-ended retainer, no scope creep in the engagement itself. Turnaround is defined at intake.

The objection worth answering directly

"My contractor is reputable — I don't think there's a problem."

A review is not an accusation. Reputable contractors still produce contracts with gaps, change orders without proper backup, and pay applications that bill ahead of completed work. Those are standard industry patterns, and they cost owners real money when no one is reading the documents on the owner's side. If everything in your project is clean, the review confirms it in writing and costs you a flat fee. If something is wrong, you found it before you signed the check. Either outcome is worth more than the alternative.

The same logic applies if you already have an architect. Your architect is hired to protect the design intent — not your budget, not your draw schedule, and not your contract rights. Those are different jobs, and the gap between them is exactly where this review lives.
