Why it works

Most garden shops and dried-flower sellers in the East Bay cannot answer the simplest question a buyer can ask: where did this come from? Not because the answer is complicated, but because the supply chain makes it irrelevant to them. Plants arrive from regional wholesalers, move through distribution, and land on a shelf with a generic tag. Dried flowers ship from distant sellers you'll never be able to verify or revisit. The staff at a chain store cannot tell you how a start was handled before it hit the rack, or whether it will survive Concord's summer heat in your specific backyard. That is not a staff problem — it is a structural one.

Sunflower Acres runs the opposite model. The inventory is smaller because it is real. One farm, one address in Concord, one growing season. What's listed is what the fields actually produced — plant starts suited to this microclimate, flowers cut from these rows, dried stems harvested on a specific date. Every listing carries materials, size, care instructions, and pickup or shipping options, because that level of item-level detail is the sourcing proof. It is not a styling choice.

The shop's availability is listed in real time against the actual harvest cycle. Seasonal goods are seasonal — that's honest, not a flaw. You know what's there before you make the trip, which is not how most local flower sources work. Most require you to show up and find out.

For the gardener who has brought home an unlabeled flat from a chain store and watched it fail, that specificity matters. For the crafter who has pieced together dried botanical orders from distant sellers and received something inconsistent with no one to call, having a named farm with a real address changes the sourcing equation entirely. For the gift-giver who wants something local and real and has been driving to a farmers market and hoping the vendor showed up — this is a shop you can come back to, season to season, because you know what it carries and you trust what it tells you.
