Fruit Tree Cleanup: A Mini Chainsaw and a Mulcher That Actually Work

2026-11-09

You've got a fruit tree to take down, and then a pile of branches to deal with. That's the whole job, and it's the job I just did in my own garden: take the tree down, cut it into manageable pieces, and turn the branches into mulch I can actually use, instead of hauling them away or leaving a pile to rot.

How these were chosen

These are not picks I researched from a list. They are the two tools I actually used for this one job, in my own garden, after working for tree companies for several years. I chose them because they held up on that job, and because I was surprised by both of them — the chainsaw because I did not expect a little Amazon saw to do real tree work, and the mulcher because it handled a real pile of fruit-tree branches without turning into a chore. I am only including tools I used here. I am not adding other tools to fill out a list.

Mini Cordless Chainsaw

What it is: a small, battery-powered, cordless mini chainsaw, bought on Amazon. Not a full-size professional saw, and not the expensive version of this kind of saw either — I did not even get the expensive one.

Who it suits: a homeowner or gardener who needs to take down a small to medium fruit tree, or cut up branches and logs for cleanup, and who wants a light, one-hand-friendly saw for that kind of work rather than dragging out a big gas saw.

Why it fits this goal: I took down the entire tree with it, and then chopped the whole tree down into 2-foot logs. It never jammed up or walked up on me once, and I never even had to change the battery during that job. Coming from tree-company work, where I have high expectations of chainsaws, that is what surprised me. It did the cutting part of a fruit-tree cleanup in one go, without stopping to clear jams or swap batteries.

One honest limitation: it is a small saw. I used it on a fruit tree, and I am not presenting it as a replacement for a full-size professional saw for large timber or heavy professional tree work. Its strength here is small-tree and cleanup cutting, not big-tree felling.

Yeryork Mulcher

What it is: a green, electric Yeryork wood chipper and shredder, the one in the photos in front of the pile of fruit-tree branches. It chips branches and leaves into mulch, with a collection bag underneath.

Who it suits: a gardener with fruit trees and a regular pile of prunings and branches to clear, who wants that material turned into mulch for the garden instead of bagged for disposal.

Why it fits this goal: I am surprised about how well it works. Fed with the branches from the tree I had just taken down, it turned a large, messy pile of fruit-tree branches and leaves into mulch. That closes the loop on a fruit-tree job: the tree comes down, the logs are cut, and the branches do not become waste — they become mulch.

One honest limitation: it works best for fruit trees, in my opinion, because the pieces you put in it need to be very straight, or it gets hung up. Crooked or tangled pieces are not what this machine likes. Feed it straight pieces and it works well. Feed it tangled pieces and you will be stopping to clear it. Also, be careful when opening it — the blades are really sharp.

Which one is right for you

If your job is mostly cutting — you have a tree to take down, or branches and logs to cut into manageable pieces — start with the mini chainsaw. That is the cutting tool, and it is the one that surprised me most, given my tree-company background.

If your job is mostly cleanup — you already have a pile of fruit-tree branches, and you want them gone and useful — start with the mulcher. That is the cleanup tool, and it is at its best on straight fruit-tree material.

If your job is the whole thing, like mine was — take the tree down, cut it up, and clear the branches — you need both, in that order: chainsaw first to bring it down and cut it into 2-foot logs, mulcher second to turn the branches into mulch. Neither one replaces the other, and neither one on its own finishes a fruit-tree cleanup.

Next step

Pick the part of the job you are actually facing first — cutting or cleanup — and start there. If you are facing the whole tree, start with the chainsaw, then move to the mulcher once the branches are down and you can feed them in straight.

That order matters in a home garden, not just in a work yard. Cutting first gives you logs you can stack and move, and branches you can sort as you go. Mulching second gives you a use for the branches that would otherwise sit in a pile, waiting for a green-waste run. When the two tools are used in that order, a fruit-tree job that starts as a tree in the way ends as logs and mulch, both useful, with the garden clearer than it started.
