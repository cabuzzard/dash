Growing Baby Blue Eucalyptus in Concord — Dried Flower Cutting Garden

2026-11-14

I grow things in Concord. The garden here is clay soil, hot dry summers, mild winters — and it turns out that's exactly the kind of place decorative eucalyptus likes. This is the plan I'm starting now: Baby Blue eucalyptus grown as a cutting shrub, plus a cutting garden of dried flowers, all heading toward the same place — dried arrangements and craft packs in the Sunflower Acres garden shop.

The short answer

Grow Eucalyptus pulverulenta 'Baby Blue' as a coppiced shrub, not a tree. Plant it in the ground on a raised mound, in full sun, and prune it to keep it at cutting height — three to six feet. Around it, grow strawflower, statice, gomphrena, and lavender in the beds. Cut in summer, dry for two to three weeks, and the material becomes mini bouquets, DIY craft packs, small wreaths, and supply bundles.

Why Baby Blue, and why a shrub, not a tree

Baby Blue is the pick because of the leaves: dense, powdery, silver-blue, and they hold their round shape when cut and dried. It's the first choice for cut foliage, and it takes regular pruning without complaint. The other two worth knowing are Silver Dollar (Eucalyptus cinerea), the classic round silvery leaf with a strong scent, great for wreaths but it wants to become a substantial tree; and gunnii 'Azura', blue-gray juvenile foliage with good cold tolerance, naturally tree-forming but prunable.

The shrub part matters more than the variety. Left alone, Baby Blue becomes a real tree — ten feet and up. Cut back regularly, it stays a productive bush around three to six feet tall, throwing fresh juvenile stems all season. That's the whole game: long, straight stems with round silver-blue leaves, not a shade tree. Harvesting means cutting stems when they're about eighteen to thirty inches long and the tips have firmed up, leaving healthy growing points behind so the plant keeps producing.

Where it goes in the garden

In the ground, on a raised mound — not crowded into a four-by-eight raised bed. I have four 4×8 beds and I asked about putting eucalyptus in them; the honest answer is that the roots need room and the drainage matters more than the bed. An open-bottomed bed can work for a trial, but for long-term production, in-ground on a mound is better. It wants full sun, six to eight hours a day, and well-drained soil. My clay stays wet in winter, so the mound is not optional.

Two placement rules: keep it away from foundations, pipes, and retaining walls — it develops woody, spreading roots — and keep lavender and rosemary in their own well-drained permanent spots too. The beds are for the annuals that can take more water. The shrubs live where they can drain.

When to plant

Right now. November is a good time to plant nursery stock in Concord — the soil is still warm, the winter rain helps young plants put down roots before summer heat arrives, and fall through early spring is the planting window. I'm starting with established one-gallon plants rather than seed; they give a head start, and the first year is about establishment, with more substantial stem production after that.

The safer fallback is March, especially if the plants are very small or the yard gets hard frosts. Concord occasionally gets a damaging freeze, so young plants need protection from a hard one. December through February is possible but don't plant right before a freeze. June through September is the worst time — the summer heat here is no place to establish anything.

The cutting garden: five starters, four beds

The eucalyptus is the greenery crop. The color comes from four more plants, and together they cover everything a dried arrangement needs:

Strawflower. Papery blossoms in pink, gold, white, orange, and red. One of the best flowers for dried bouquets, grown as an annual in the sunny beds. Harvest before the flowers fully open.

Statice. Purple, pink, blue, and white sprays that hold their color exceptionally well when dried. The filler in nearly every dried arrangement.

Gomphrena. Little globe-shaped flowers in brilliant colors. Loves heat and produces stems all summer with regular harvesting. Small enough for craft packs and small packages.

Lavender. Fragrant stems for bouquets, sachets, wreaths, and bundles. 'Grosso' is the one for long-stemmed harvesting, grown in its own well-drained permanent spot, not in the beds.

Baby Blue eucalyptus. The evergreen greenery. Excellent for elegant arrangements, though dried leaves can be fragile and need more protective packaging than the flowers do.

The bed plan is simple. Bed one: strawflowers, the colorful focal flowers. Bed two: statice, the long-stemmed filler. Bed three: gomphrena and celosia, summer flowers and texture. Bed four: yarrow and craspedia — perennial flowers and yellow accents. Eucalyptus, lavender, and rosemary stay out of the beds in their permanent spots.

The calendar runs like this: November through February, establish the perennials and the eucalyptus and start cool-season flowers. February through April, plant statice, craspedia, and spring cut flowers. April through May, plant strawflower, gomphrena, and celosia after the frost risk passes. June through October, harvest summer flowers regularly and dry the surplus.

Drying and making

The business side is built around dried botanicals, not fresh bouquets. Dried is lightweight, colorful, and cheap to ship — and you can harvest all summer, dry and store the material, and make products all year. Cut stems on a dry morning before the heat. Hang small bunches in a dark, ventilated space, generally for two to three weeks. Strawflower heads can also be dried upright.

Four products to test first: mini dried bouquets, about eight to twelve inches tall; DIY flower craft packs, assorted flower heads for resin, wreaths, and crafts — the imperfect flowers still become craft packs; small botanical wreaths, higher value but harder to protect in shipping; and dried flower supply bundles, raw materials for other floral designers. Prices aren't set yet — these are products to test, not a price list.

For shipping: a rigid corrugated box, stems secured so they don't move, flowers cushioned with tissue, blossoms never compressed. Test-pack and ship a sample to yourself before selling a single one.

What I'm still deciding

Size. Small arrangements that fit in a twelve-inch shipping box, or full-size dried bouquets with eighteen- to twenty-four-inch stems? Smaller is easier to ship. Larger could command higher prices. That's the one open question, and it's the one I'll answer by making both and seeing which one people actually want.

Frequently asked questions

Can I grow Baby Blue eucalyptus in a raised bed?

Possible, if the bed is open-bottomed and the roots can grow into the ground — but in-ground planting on a raised mound is better for long-term production. Don't put several plants into one 4×8 bed; plant them separately in the ground and prune them into productive shrubs.

How tall does Baby Blue eucalyptus get?

Left unpruned, it's a small tree — ten feet and up. Kept as a cutting shrub with regular pruning, three to six feet. Don't treat it as a naturally tiny shrub; the pruning is what keeps it small.

Is eucalyptus invasive or a fire concern?

Some species are, and eucalyptus can grow surprisingly large — that's exactly why the pick is the compact Baby Blue, kept pruned as a shrub, rather than a full-size eucalyptus tree. Manage the size and it stays a garden plant, not a problem.

When will the first products be ready?

The first year is establishment. The beds get prepped now, the perennials go in, the warm-season flowers start in spring, and the first real harvest comes next summer. Dried products follow the harvest — this is a longer build, not a this-week launch.
