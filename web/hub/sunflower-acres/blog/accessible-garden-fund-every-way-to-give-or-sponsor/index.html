Accessible Garden Fund: Every Way to Give or Sponsor

2026-10-23

You've been in research mode long enough to know that "accessible" rarely means what it says. You've called ahead, read the reviews, arrived at the paved edge near the thing, and loaded back into the car. When a fund asks you to support accessibility, your first instinct is reasonable skepticism — not because you don't care, but because you've cared before and watched the money land nowhere you could verify. That's the problem the Sunflower Acres Accessible Garden Fund is designed to solve, starting with how it takes contributions.

The short answer

Every way to support the Sunflower Acres Accessible Garden Fund — individual donation, named sponsorship, in-kind contribution, or co-funding through a community organization — is tied to a specific infrastructure project with a published scope, a target budget, and a named receiving organization. You are not giving to accessibility in general. You are closing a line item: a path segment, a seated cutting station, an accessible restroom upgrade. The project is defined before contributions open. Progress is tracked publicly. The result is a physical place in Concord you can drive to and confirm.

What does a donation actually pay for?

This is the question a cautious donor asks first, and it deserves a straight answer. The fund backs infrastructure — the construction-level items that determine whether a wheelchair user has the same experience at the farm as everyone else, or a shorter, roped-off version of it.

That means compacted decomposed granite paths at a measurable grade, raised and seated-height cutting stations, accessible restroom builds with specific interior clearances, loaner chairs staged at the farm entrance, and free companion entry. These are not soft amenities or symbolic gestures. They are scoped, costed projects — the same way a contractor scopes a ramp or a path upgrade.

Sunflower Acres in Concord is already built on this model. The farm is step-free end to end — not step-free to a nearby bench, but into and through the fields. Path grades, surface types, restroom dimensions, and loaner chair availability are published on the farm's own site. You can verify the existing infrastructure before you give a dollar to the fund. The fund extends and protects what's already there.

Each project listed in the fund has its scope defined, its target budget stated, and its receiving organization named before contributions open. You see the progress tracker before you give, not after.

Who benefits — and why does the specificity matter to them?

The families who need this most do more advance research than anyone should have to. Before every outing, someone is cross-referencing accessibility claims, calling ahead, reading reviews to find out whether "accessible" means anything at the destination. They load the car. Sometimes it works. Often they arrive at the paved overlook.

Wheelchair users and their families in the East Bay have almost no outdoor destination that is genuinely step-free end to end — not mostly flat, not mostly paved, but actually in the field, cutting flowers, taking the workshop, moving through the space without a separate route or a reduced experience. Sunflower Acres is one of the only farms of its kind in the country at this level of specificity.

The specificity matters because the families who need this are not in impulse mode. They are not moved by general claims. They need a path grade, a restroom dimension, a stated policy on companion entry — something concrete enough to trust before they commit the logistics of an outing. The same specificity that earns their trust is the same standard applied to the fund: scope, budget, named organization, progress tracker.

How do the different ways to support actually work?

Individual contributions are accepted at any amount toward any listed project. The project page shows the scope, the target, the current total, and the receiving organization. When the project closes, progress is reported against the published budget. You're completing a named line item, not contributing to a concept.

Named sponsorships for local businesses and community organizations are tied to specific infrastructure — a cutting station, a path improvement, a visitor access upgrade — not to a calendar year or a renewal cycle. The sponsorship tracks to a deliverable. When it is built, the sponsor can drive to Concord and see it. For businesses with CSR budgets who are tired of gala banners and logo placements that go nowhere, this is a physical address their employees, customers, and community members can visit and experience directly.

In-kind and co-sponsorship arrangements are available for organizations that want to co-fund a project alongside other contributors or fold a scoped project into an existing grant conversation. The pre-scoped format is designed for that use: scope, cost, and receiving organization already documented. A community organization serving people with mobility disabilities in Contra Costa County can walk into a board presentation or grant conversation with a named project that already has its case built.

There is no volunteering program listed in the current fund structure — the projects are infrastructure builds, not garden workdays. If that changes, it will be listed as a named project with a defined scope.

How to apply it

Before you give to any accessible garden fund — this one or any other — confirm four things:

Receiving organization and nonprofit status. Contributions to the Sunflower Acres Accessible Garden Fund are processed through a named 501(c)(3) or fiscal sponsor. The organization name, EIN or fiscal sponsorship documentation, and payment route are disclosed before you give — not on a confirmation email afterward. For tax documentation, retain the acknowledgment letter with the organization's EIN and consult your tax advisor for your specific situation. (Source: IRS Publication 526 governs charitable contribution deductibility in the United States.)

Project scope and budget. A trustworthy fund shows you the scope and target before the ask. If a project page requests money before it shows you what the money builds, that is the flag.

Progress reporting. Check whether the fund reports against the published budget as contributions arrive, not just in an annual impact summary. Project-level tracking — not organization-level — is what tells you the line item actually closed.

Physical verifiability. The Sunflower Acres fund has an advantage most donation pages do not: you can get in your car, drive to Concord, and walk or roll through the farm before you give. The existing infrastructure is the operating model, not a promise. The path grades and restroom dimensions are already published. Drive out and check.

If you're a community organization: the pre-scoped project format is designed for your funding conversations. You don't need to build the case from scratch. If you're a local business: the named sponsorship ties to a deliverable, not a banner. When the project closes, you return to the farm and confirm what you funded is still there. That is what local CSR looks like when it has a physical address.

Frequently asked questions

Is a donation to the Accessible Garden Fund tax-deductible?

Contributions are processed through a named 501(c)(3) organization or a documented fiscal sponsorship arrangement. Donations structured this way are generally tax-deductible in the United States — retain your acknowledgment letter with the organization's EIN and consult your tax advisor for your specific situation.

What happens if a project doesn't reach its target budget?

Projects are scoped and costed before contributions open. The progress tracker shows real-time totals against the published target. If a project's funding status changes, that is reported at the project level — not buried in an annual report.

Can a community organization co-fund a project alongside individual donors?

Yes. The pre-scoped project format is designed for exactly this use. Community organizations can contribute alongside individual donors, present a named project to their own funders using the existing documentation, or inquire about co-sponsorship arrangements for larger projects.

What does a corporate sponsorship include beyond recognition?

Named sponsorships tie to a specific infrastructure deliverable — a path segment, a cutting station, an accessible restroom upgrade. Recognition is tied to that physical result, not a calendar year. Depending on project scope, sponsorship may include impact reporting and co-branding tied to the specific improvement funded. The result is a working farm location your stakeholders can visit and experience.

Is there a minimum donation amount?

Individual contributions are accepted at any amount toward any listed project. Named sponsorships have amounts and structures defined at the project level — the project page states the sponsorship tiers available for that specific improvement.
