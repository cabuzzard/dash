Your Caregiver Support Programs Are Expiring in 2026

2026-10-15

The clock ran out quietly. There was no press conference, no headline that landed in most caregivers' feeds, and no letter in the mail. The American Rescue Plan Act money that expanded home and community-based services across every state — funding that paid for respite hours, workforce training slots, adult day programs, and in-home support coordination — expires at the end of 2026. Most of the 53 million unpaid caregivers in this country do not know it is coming. Some of the programs keeping them functional right now will not survive January.

What ARPA Built — and What Expires With It

When Congress passed the American Rescue Plan Act in 2021, one of its least-discussed provisions gave states a temporary 10 percent increase in federal matching funds for Medicaid home and community-based services — what the policy world calls HCBS. States were required to use those enhanced dollars to expand or strengthen existing programs, not simply to plug budget gaps. What followed was a genuine, if uneven, expansion. States stood up new caregiver respite programs, funded training pipelines for home health aides, extended eligibility for adult day services, and in some cases created coordination infrastructure that had never existed before. The operative word in every single one of those expansions is temporary. The enhanced match was always time-limited. States built programs on a foundation they knew — or should have known — would eventually be pulled.

That pull is now happening in real time. October 2026 means the planning window for a managed drawdown has largely closed. Programs that needed a two-year runway to find alternative funding sources have had that runway. Many used it. Many did not. The ones that did not are now making decisions — quietly, administratively, without press releases — about which services to contract, which waitlists to freeze, and which staff to lay off. Caregivers who depend on those services are not receiving advance notice commensurate with the disruption. They are finding out when a coordinator calls to say the respite slot is no longer available, or when a renewal letter simply does not arrive.

This is the piece of the story that never gets told in Medicare or caregiving coverage: the person being hit hardest is not the person enrolled in the plan. It is the person sitting next to them, managing the medication schedule, driving to appointments, sleeping with one ear open. When a respite program closes, the caregiver loses the only four hours a week that belonged to them. When an adult day service cuts its roster, the family caregiver loses the only window in which they can work, sleep, or see a doctor themselves. The care recipient's Medicare eligibility is unchanged. Their Medicare supplement plans, their Part D coverage, their Medicare Advantage plan — none of that moves. What moves is the invisible scaffolding keeping the unpaid caregiver upright. That scaffolding is what ARPA funded. That scaffolding is what is now being disassembled.

Your Programs, Your Risk — How to Find Out Before It's Too Late

The practical question most caregivers are not yet asking — because most do not know to ask it — is whether the specific programs they rely on are ARPA-funded, and therefore structurally at risk. The honest answer is that finding out requires work. State Medicaid agencies maintain HCBS spending plans that were filed as a condition of receiving the enhanced match, and those documents are public. Area Agencies on Aging, which coordinate much of the ground-level caregiver support infrastructure, often know which of their contracted services have ARPA-dependent budgets and which are funded through more stable, longer-term mechanisms. Calling your state's Medicaid office directly and asking which HCBS expansions were funded through the enhanced FMAP — the Federal Medical Assistance Percentage increase — is a blunt but effective approach. It is not the conversation most caregivers have the bandwidth to initiate, but it is the one that tells you whether the floor beneath you is temporary.

If you are currently using a state-funded respite program, an adult day service, a caregiver support group that receives public funding, or any form of in-home coordination support, ask the coordinator directly: is this program's current capacity dependent on federal funding that is expiring? A good coordinator will tell you. A program in the middle of a funding crisis may not volunteer the information unless you ask. You do not have time to find out six months after the fact. The moment a service closes is the worst possible moment to begin looking for alternatives, because your reserves — physical, emotional, cognitive — will already be running at their lowest. The planning has to happen now, while the program still exists and while you still have enough margin to think.

What remains genuinely uncertain is how far the contraction will travel before states respond with their own budget adjustments. Some states entered the ARPA period with stronger baseline HCBS infrastructure and may absorb the expiration with modest service reductions. Others built aggressively on a federal foundation and are now looking at structural shortfalls their general fund was never designed to cover. The split will not be geographically predictable in the way people expect — it will follow the decisions individual states made in 2021 and 2022 about how to design their spending plans and how much of their own revenue they committed alongside the federal match. Caregivers in states that built conservatively may not notice much. Caregivers in states that built ambitiously and did not plan for the drawdown will feel it hard.

What Private Philanthropy Is Being Asked to Replace

What private philanthropy is being asked to do in this environment is significant, and the ask is arriving faster than most institutional donors anticipated. Foundations that fund aging services, disability advocacy, and caregiver support are fielding requests from programs that built capacity on ARPA dollars and now need sustained, multi-year philanthropic commitments to survive. Community foundations in states with large rural populations — where the HCBS expansion reached places commercial and nonprofit services had never previously touched — are under particular pressure. The calculus for donors is not simple. Funding a program that has already expanded to serve a larger population is not the same as funding a startup. You are being asked to sustain an operational reality, not to launch one. The program has staff, clients, and infrastructure. Without replacement funding, all of that contracts. The people on the other end of that contraction are caregivers who were already at capacity before the service existed.

For donors and philanthropic investors trying to determine where to direct attention and capital, a few markers are worth watching. Programs that received ARPA expansion funding and simultaneously developed community co-funding or earned revenue during the enhancement period are better positioned than those that remained wholly dependent on the federal match. Caregiver respite programs with documented outcome data — reduced hospitalization rates for care recipients, measurable reduction in caregiver-reported burnout — carry stronger cases for foundation support than those without it. And programs embedded in Area Agency on Aging infrastructure have a structural advocacy advantage: the AAA network has a direct federal policy relationship that purely independent nonprofits lack, which matters when the argument being made is that private philanthropy is filling a gap that federal policy created.

It is worth naming what caregiver burnout actually is. Peer-reviewed research links prolonged unpaid caregiving to clinically elevated anxiety, depression, disrupted sleep, and measurable physical health decline. The respite hours, the adult day slots, the peer support groups — these are not luxuries. They are the thin margin between a caregiver who is depleted and a caregiver who has broken. When that margin closes because a funding deadline passed without a replacement in place, the person absorbing the cost is the one who was already invisible inside the system. Fifty-three million people are carrying this weight without a paycheck, without a schedule, and without anyone stopping to ask how they are doing. The ARPA cliff does not make headlines because the people it harms the most are the ones who have never once been the subject of a headline. That does not make the harm less real. It makes it easier to ignore — and that is exactly the problem.

The programs disappearing at the end of 2026 were built to fill a real gap, and the people who will feel their absence are the ones already running on empty. If you rely on a publicly funded caregiver service, find out now whether it carries ARPA-dependent funding — not after the waitlist freezes, not after the coordinator stops calling. And if you are a donor or foundation weighing where sustained capital does the most good, this is the gap. Not a new initiative. A floor that is being pulled out from under people who never had a spare inch to fall.
