Your Medicare Plan Won't Protect Home Care — The $911 Billion Medicaid Cut Will

2026-10-11

The number that should be keeping Medicare-eligible caregivers awake right now is not a plan code or a premium figure. It is $911 billion — the scale of Medicaid reductions projected over ten years under current federal budget trajectories, cuts that carve directly into the home and community-based services that millions of disabled and aging adults depend on to stay out of nursing facilities. For the 53 million Americans already providing that care unpaid, at home, without a break, this is not a fiscal abstraction. It is the difference between having any relief at all and having none.

What Is Actually Being Cut — And Why It Hits Caregivers First

Home and community-based services — HCBS — are the Medicaid-funded supports that allow adults with disabilities and aging individuals to receive care in their homes or in community settings rather than in institutional placements. They include personal care attendants, adult day programs, skilled nursing visits, and the coordination infrastructure that holds a care plan together. When Medicaid funding contracts, states face immediate structural pressure to reduce these services first. Unlike acute hospital care, HCBS waivers are optional under federal Medicaid rules — meaning states have the legal flexibility to cut them even when cutting them causes real harm.

The people most exposed to that harm are not the policy analysts tracking the budget reconciliation. They are the unpaid family caregivers who have been filling in the gaps informally, and who lose their only window of relief when a home health aide hour is cut or an adult day slot disappears from the schedule. You do not lose a line item when home care funding gets cut. You lose the three hours on Tuesday when someone else was there and you could sit down. You lose the respite that was keeping you functional enough to keep showing up.

The $911 billion figure is not arriving as a single event. It accumulates slowly, through state-level waiver reductions, enrollment freezes, and service-hour caps that each look modest in isolation and together hollow out the support system underneath the person you are caring for. The erosion is already underway, and it does not announce itself.

This matters in a specific way for anyone navigating Medicare eligibility decisions right now. Medicare and Medicaid are structurally separate. Medicare is the federal health insurance program available at age 65, or earlier for qualifying disabilities. Medicaid is the joint federal-state program that funds long-term care services, including HCBS. Many disabled and aging adults who qualify for Medicare also rely on Medicaid as a secondary coverage source, and the interaction between the two programs is where the real complexity lives. A person who transitions to Medicare — or whose caregiver is crossing the Medicare eligibility threshold and making plan decisions — may not realize that the home care services they depend on are sitting entirely on the Medicaid side of that line and are therefore exposed to cuts that no Medicare policy change can protect them from. Understanding Medicare Part A and Part B is necessary. It is not sufficient.

Medicare covers hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care for short-term recovery, physician visits, and medically necessary home health visits under specific conditions. It does not cover the ongoing personal care — bathing, dressing, mobility assistance, medication management — that keeps a disabled adult living in their home over years rather than weeks. That care is Medicaid. And that is precisely the category of spending facing the deepest pressure under the projected reductions. If you are comparing Medicare Advantage plans or Medicare supplement plans right now and assuming that coverage locks in your loved one's home care, it does not. The two systems do not substitute for each other, and no Medicare plan finder result changes that structural exposure.

Three Things Caregivers Must Do Before the Cuts Make Action Harder

The practical urgency is this: caregivers need to act before the reductions make action harder, not after. That means three things in parallel — and none of them can wait for the policy fight to resolve, because the systems you need to access have lead times measured in months.

First, find out now what HCBS services the person you support is currently receiving through Medicaid, and which of those services are waiver-funded rather than mandatory. Waiver programs are the most legally vulnerable to cuts. A state Medicaid office, a local Area Agency on Aging, or a benefits counselor through the SHIP program — the State Health Insurance Assistance Program — can give you a clear picture of what exists and what is at risk. This is not a call to schedule for next quarter. Make it this week.

Second, if the person you support is not yet enrolled in all the Medicaid programs they qualify for, now is the time. Waitlists for HCBS waivers are already long in most states. Getting on a list before a funding freeze is materially better than applying after one. Enrollment freezes are one of the first tools states reach for when federal dollars contract, and once a freeze is in place, qualifying does not guarantee access.

Third, if you are personally approaching Medicare eligibility age, separating your own coverage decisions from the coverage decisions of the person you care for is not a luxury — it is a structural necessity. Conflating them leads to gaps in both. The Medicare eligibility resources that exist were written for the beneficiary, not for the person managing someone else's care while also reaching Medicare age themselves. Medicaid advocacy tends to target either policy professionals or care recipients directly. The caregiver in the middle — exhausted, making coverage decisions for someone else, watching home care hours disappear, trying to hold their own life together — is the person most exposed to the cut and least served by the information available.

Caregiver burnout in this context is not a soft wellness concern layered on top of a policy problem. It is the direct downstream consequence of policy choices that reduce formal care infrastructure and transfer the load quietly onto unpaid family members who were already at capacity. The mechanism is not subtle: public funding contracts, aide hours shrink, the gap opens, and the caregiver absorbs it. That transfer does not show up in a budget line. It shows up in the person who has not slept through the night in two years.

What Remains Uncertain — And Where Direct Action Still Works

The final shape of federal Medicaid spending over the next decade is not fixed, and that is worth naming plainly. Budget projections shift. State responses vary. Some states will find ways to protect HCBS funding that others will not, and the political environment around Medicaid cuts remains actively contested. The $911 billion figure reflects a trajectory, not a locked outcome.

That uncertainty cuts both ways. It means the worst-case scenario is not inevitable. It also means that waiting for certainty before taking action is a losing strategy, because the systems caregivers need to access now — waiver enrollments, SHIP counseling appointments, benefits reviews — do not move quickly. The window to act before conditions deteriorate is open now. It will not stay open indefinitely.

There is also something that individuals can do that sits entirely outside the policy fight: direct support toward the organizations that are actively housing and caring for disabled adults when public funding contracts. The Stable Home Monthly Giving Circle exists inside that gap. It is not an awareness campaign or a broad disability advocacy fund. It is a structured, recurring giving membership that routes monthly contributions directly into housing stability and care support for adults with disabilities — and unlike most nonprofit giving, it answers back. Quarterly impact updates show what the circle specifically funded. An annual visit day puts members in the room with the people and the outcomes their contributions supported.

For caregivers and family members who understand firsthand what it costs when care infrastructure fails, this is giving that does not disappear into a budget line. It is a seat at the table where the work is actually happening — not a gesture toward a problem, but a recurring, accountable investment in the people the policy leaves most exposed.

The cuts do not announce themselves at the door. They accumulate in cancelled aide visits, frozen waitlists, and reduced hours that each look like an administrative adjustment and together reshape what care is available and who absorbs the gap. The caregiver absorbs it. That is the mechanism, and it is already running.

The most common question caregivers ask about Medicare is which plan to choose. The more urgent question right now is whether the home care that makes everything else possible will still exist in a year. Those are two different questions, and answering only the first one leaves you exposed on the second. Find out what waiver services the person you support is currently enrolled in. Get on the waitlists that are still open. Separate your own Medicare decisions from theirs. And if you can direct any recurring support toward the organizations holding the line when public funding contracts, do that too. The time to move on all of it is now — before the erosion that is already underway makes the options narrower.
