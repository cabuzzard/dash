Your Medicare Part B Preventive Benefits, Decoded for Caregivers

2026-10-27

You have managed Medicare for everyone else. You have not had a wellness visit in three years. Medicare Part B already covers zero-cost preventive screenings, an annual wellness visit, and outpatient mental health services — and if you are an unpaid caregiver past 65, those benefits are sitting there unused. Here is what they cover, how to access them, and what support infrastructure exists right now for caregivers who are inside the burn.

The news in brief

An estimated 53 million unpaid caregivers in the United States — most of them women in their 40s, 50s, and 60s, according to AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving — are managing Medicare coverage decisions for the people they care for while leaving their own Part B preventive benefits entirely unused. A significant portion of those caregivers have already crossed the Medicare eligibility age threshold. The benefits they are not using include zero-cost annual wellness visits, depression screenings, cardiovascular and diabetes screenings, cancer screenings, and outpatient mental health visits covered at 80 percent after the Part B deductible.

What happened

According to the pillar reporting on this topic, the caregiving system in the United States was built entirely around the person receiving care and structurally treats the person providing it as infrastructure — something that functions in the background and does not require maintenance. The result is a documented gap: caregivers who can explain Medicare Advantage coverage gaps, Part D formularies, and supplement plan comparisons for someone else have never looked up what their own Part B covers for their mental health or preventive care. The Medicaid home care landscape has also tightened in multiple states in recent years, reducing formal respite options and pushing more daily caregiving load onto unpaid family members — increasing hours, stress, and physical toll without a corresponding increase in caregivers using their own Medicare benefits. That gap, the reporting notes, is a scheduling and awareness problem, not an inevitability.

Why it matters

If you are a caregiver past 65 with Medicare Part B, you already have access to services that are directly relevant to what caregiving does to a body over time. The Annual Wellness Visit — covered at 100 percent, no copay — creates a personalized prevention plan, reviews risk factors, and screens for cognitive impairment. Depression screening is covered annually as a preventive service in a primary care setting, no separate referral required. Cardiovascular disease screening is covered every five years. Diabetes screening — relevant because chronic stress, disrupted sleep, and erratic eating are documented risk factors — is covered up to twice a year. Cancer screenings, bone density measurement, and lung cancer screening for high-risk individuals are all included. Outpatient mental health visits — individual therapy, group therapy, psychiatric evaluation — are covered at 80 percent after your deductible, with the remaining 20 percent reduced or eliminated if you have a Medicare supplement plan. If you are on a Medicare Advantage plan, your preventive benefits are required to be at minimum equivalent to Original Medicare Part B; many plans add benefits on top. Caregiver burnout is not a soft concept. It is a documented clinical presentation — emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, reduced sense of personal accomplishment, and accumulating physical symptoms. The American Medical Association has named it. It is treatable. The mechanism behind the problem is chronic overload without recovery, and the solution is not willpower. It is a scheduled appointment.

Our perspective

The practical obstacle here is not eligibility — it is framing. Every search result a caregiver has run for the past two or three years has been written for the person they are caring for. The programs and benefits written for caregivers themselves are underused because nobody aimed a search result at the caregiver until now. One concrete action closes most of the gap: schedule the Medicare Annual Wellness Visit and say those exact words when you call — 'I want to schedule my Medicare Annual Wellness Visit.' That specific framing protects you from being billed for a general physical. Use the visit to name the caregiving load directly, ask to be screened for depression and anxiety, and request a referral to an outpatient mental health provider if the screening warrants it. That visit is your formal entry point into a preventive infrastructure you have already paid for through your Part B premium. For mental health providers specifically, Medicare.gov's Care Compare tool filters for licensed providers who accept Medicare — clinical social workers who accept Medicare are often more accessible on short timelines than psychiatrists. Telehealth mental health visits are covered for many services under Medicare, which matters if you are in a rural area or have mobility constraints. Beyond Part B, the Eldercare Locator (operated by the U.S. Administration on Aging) connects caregivers to local Area Agencies on Aging for respite care, caregiver counseling, and support groups. The ARCH National Respite Network can locate respite options when the situation has become genuinely unsustainable. Most of these programs are free or income-scaled. They were built for you.

What happens next

The Part B deductible adjusts annually — check Medicare.gov for the current figure before scheduling. If you are on a Medicare Advantage plan, call member services and ask specifically what your preventive and mental health benefits include, what your cost-sharing looks like, and whether you need a referral for mental health services. That conversation takes fifteen minutes and the information is owed to you. The open question worth watching: as Medicaid home care options continue to tighten in multiple states, the pressure on unpaid caregivers increases. Whether awareness of caregiver-directed Medicare benefits keeps pace with that pressure remains to be seen. The benefits exist. The scheduling gap is the one thing you can close right now.
