The Direct Care Wage Crisis: What Foundation Executives and Impact Investors Must Do Before 2026

2026-09-03

When a workforce loses more than half its people every year, the problem is no longer recruitment. It is the fundamental economics of the job itself — and for foundation executives and ESG-aligned investors, the window to act on that reality is closing faster than most funding cycles acknowledge. The scaffolding that kept the direct care system nominally functional through the pandemic years is coming down, and what is exposed underneath was never viable to begin with.

Why Sub-$15 Wages Are the Master Variable — Not One Problem Among Many

Reported median wages in direct care have remained anchored below fifteen dollars an hour nationally. Adjusted for inflation, transportation costs, and the sustained physical and emotional demands of the role, that figure represents compensation most workers cannot maintain across multiple years. The outcome is annual turnover exceeding sixty percent in many states — a churn velocity so high that training investments evaporate before they compound, supervisory relationships never stabilize, and the people receiving care cycle through an endless series of strangers during some of the most intimate hours of their lives.

Policy conversations often treat turnover as one challenge among several in the long-term care staffing crisis, alongside recruitment pipelines, training quality, career ladders, and supervisory culture. All of those things matter. But when turnover exceeds sixty percent annually, every other intervention acquires a hard ceiling. You cannot build career ladders for people who are gone in ten months. You cannot measure the impact of a wellness program on a workforce that reconstitutes itself entirely within two years. Sub-fifteen-dollar wages are not simply one input into a complex system. They are the primary exit mechanism that makes every other investment fail before it can demonstrate results.

Understanding why this matters specifically to funders requires looking at the architecture of disability services delivery. Unlike acute hospital care or skilled nursing, home and community-based services for people with disabilities are extraordinarily labor-intensive in ways that cannot be automated or consolidated away. The ratio of worker to recipient is often one-to-one or close to it. Every hour of supported independent living, every community outing, every communication assistance session depends on a specific human being showing up, prepared and present. When that worker leaves — because she found a retail job paying two dollars more per hour, or because she burned out after months without adequate mental health support — the gap is not filled by technology or a process improvement. It is filled, inadequately, by another underpaid worker who will face the same pressures on the same timeline.

This is the mechanism that makes compensation the master variable. And it is the mechanism that the temporary federal funding that followed the pandemic effectively obscured. The American Rescue Plan Act delivered a real infusion of workforce stabilization funding to states. That money is largely gone. Medicaid — the primary payer for home and community-based disability services — is facing federal budget pressure that is unlikely to ease regardless of which political coalition holds the majority. State-level rate increases, where they have occurred at all, have generally lagged inflation and have rarely translated into sustained compensation gains for frontline workers. What is emerging now, stripped of that temporary scaffolding, is the unmasked structural reality: a compensation model that was never viable.

The Investment Logic Philanthropy Hasn't Organized Around Yet

For impact investors, this framing opens a different kind of conversation. ESG mandates and DEI reporting cycles have created genuine institutional demand for measurable social outcomes — not aspirational narratives, but quantifiable evidence that capital deployment changed something real. Caregiver retention is an unusually tractable target by that standard.

Turnover has a calculable cost. Most workforce research places the fully loaded cost of replacing a single direct care worker — accounting for recruitment, onboarding, temporary coverage, and the productivity loss of new-hire learning curves — between three thousand and five thousand dollars per departure, and often higher in specialized disability service settings. An organization running one hundred direct care workers at sixty percent annual turnover is absorbing sixty or more replacement cycles per year. A sustained reduction in that turnover rate to thirty-five percent produces savings measurable in six figures annually for a mid-sized provider — savings that can be traced directly against the cost of the wage or benefit intervention that produced the reduction. That is the kind of ROI architecture that ESG-aligned capital is designed to evaluate, and it is largely absent from current philanthropic framing of this crisis.

Individual foundations have made grants to direct care workforce initiatives. Some impact investors have experimented with social impact bonds in adjacent healthcare workforce spaces. But there is no coordinated philanthropic response that treats caregiver compensation and retention as a first-order infrastructure problem — equivalent to affordable housing or broadband access — despite the fact that the disability population's capacity to live independently in the community depends on it as directly as any physical infrastructure.

Several funding mechanisms have shown early evidence of effectiveness, though the field's evaluation infrastructure is still catching up to the urgency of the problem. Supplemental compensation funds — structured as bridge grants to providers who commit to wage floors above the Medicaid reimbursement ceiling — have demonstrated retention improvements in pilot contexts, but require coordination with state Medicaid agencies to be sustainable beyond the grant period. Direct cash supports to workers, modeled on emergency assistance programs adapted for recurring use, address the financial instability that drives attrition without requiring regulatory renegotiation, but lack the systemic durability that investors seeking long-term outcome data require. Workforce development partnerships that combine compensation guarantees with credentialed training pathways and mental health support have shown the most promising retention profiles in the disability services literature — but they are resource-intensive to design and require provider partners with sufficient organizational capacity to implement them faithfully.

The dual-mandate alignment this space offers is rare. A single funding relationship in this sector can generate outcomes relevant to both healthcare workforce metrics and disability-inclusive employment reporting — two separate ESG reporting obligations that most institutional investors currently satisfy through entirely separate funding relationships. That convergence is not incidental. It is structural, and it is underutilized.

What Funders Can Realistically Do Before 2026 — And Why the Timeline Is Operational, Not Aspirational

The math of the compensation gap is sobering, and honesty about it is prerequisite to any useful funding strategy. Bringing a direct care worker's total compensation to a livable wage level in most metropolitan markets requires an increase well above fifteen dollars — in many cities, closer to twenty or twenty-two dollars — when benefits, scheduling stability, and paid time off are factored in. The distance between current median compensation and that target is not bridgeable through philanthropy alone at any realistic funding volume. Acknowledging that is not defeatism. It is the foundation of a coherent theory of change.

What philanthropy can do is stabilize specific providers and geographic markets long enough to generate replicable data, demonstrate proof of concept for compensation-linked retention models, and create the evidentiary foundation that makes the case for sustained public investment. That is a genuinely important role — not rescuing the direct care workforce single-handedly, but funding the demonstration infrastructure that transforms this from an advocacy argument into a policy-legible evidence base.

What remains genuinely uncertain is whether any private capital strategy can move fast enough and at sufficient scale to prevent meaningful service infrastructure collapse in high-turnover states before Medicaid reimbursement reform catches up — if it does at all. That uncertainty is not a reason to wait. It is precisely the reason the window before 2026 functions as an operational deadline rather than a planning horizon. Providers in high-turnover states are already making service reduction decisions. Disability service recipients are already experiencing the consequences of a workforce that cannot stabilize. The demonstration pilots that generate the data needed to move public policy take eighteen to thirty-six months to produce credible outcomes. That timeline, counted backward from any realistic legislative or regulatory moment, points to funding decisions that need to happen now.

The practical question for funders is not whether to act but through which mechanisms, at what scale, and in which geographies. Prioritizing states where reimbursement reform efforts are already active creates the best conditions for demonstration pilots to translate into durable policy change. Partnering with providers who have the organizational infrastructure to implement compensation interventions faithfully — and the data systems to document outcomes — is prerequisite to generating the evidence base that moves institutional capital at the next tier. And treating caregiver retention metrics as core ESG reporting infrastructure, rather than a secondary output of a workforce development grant, positions the investment correctly from the start: not as charity for a struggling sector, but as the measurement foundation that shapes how this crisis is understood and funded for the decade that follows.

The foundations and institutions that move in this window with serious, data-anchored capital strategies will generate the social returns their ESG mandates require. More durably, they will have built the measurement infrastructure that shapes how this crisis is understood, funded, and ultimately resolved. The care gap is not waiting for a perfect funding model or a favorable legislative moment — and neither should they.
