Hiring a Property Caretaker: What Absentee Owners Must Check

2026-10-23

You've decided a neighbor glancing at the driveway isn't enough. Maybe you came back to a slow leak that had been running for weeks, or the yard looked like you'd been gone twice as long as you actually were. You know the property needs someone accountable — a named person, not a platform — but you've never hired a caretaker before and you're not sure what separates a good one from a liability. That uncertainty is worth resolving before a key changes hands.

The short answer

There is no licensed professional category behind "property caretaker" the way there is behind a contractor or real estate agent. That means the vetting has to be yours. What you're looking for is a local reputation you can verify, a clear understanding of what's legal under unlicensed handyman scope in California, written terms before any access is granted, and evidence — through references and a site visit — that this person is honest when something goes wrong, not just when everything is fine.

How do you actually verify someone's reputation when there's no license board?

Platform ratings tell you whether someone was pleasant. They don't tell you whether the person called the owner immediately when the water heater failed, or whether they quietly hoped it resolved itself.

A referral from someone who already uses them — a neighbor on the same road, an estate attorney, a real estate professional who works with second-home buyers in the area — is more reliable than any five-star average from strangers who don't own property like yours. Anonymous reviews don't tell you what happened on the one visit that mattered.

Ask for two or three references from current or past clients, and ask them a specific question: "Tell me about the last time something went wrong, and what the caretaker did." The answer to that question tells you more than any résumé.

Local reputation also matters practically. A caretaker who has been working in a specific area — the Sierra foothills, the wine country, the coast — has trade relationships, knows the local permit landscape, and understands the seasonal exposures specific to that geography. That context isn't transferable from a general handyman background.

What does legal handyman scope actually mean in California, and why does it matter?

In California, home improvement work valued under approximately $1,000 — materials and labor combined — that does not require a permit generally falls within legal unlicensed handyman scope. This covers door hardware, minor drywall, caulking, basic plumbing fixture repairs, fence repairs, weatherstripping, and similar work. Structural work, electrical panel work, and gas line work require a licensed contractor regardless of cost.

A caretaker doing small repairs on your property should be able to draw this line clearly and without prompting. If they can't — or if they're vague about what they'll take on versus what they'll refer out — that's a liability problem for you, not just them. Work done outside legal scope voids insurance coverage and puts you in a difficult position if something goes wrong afterward.

The right answer from a caretaker is specific: they know where the line is, they stay on the right side of it, and when licensed trade work comes up, they source the vendor, provide site access, and oversee the job on your behalf. That coordination function is part of what you're hiring for.

What written terms should be in place before access is granted?

A handshake is not a caretaking arrangement. Written terms aren't about distrust — they're about clarity, and they protect both sides.

Before a key changes hands, you should have documentation that covers:

  • Scope of work — exactly what the caretaker is responsible for and what falls outside their scope
  • Visit frequency and schedule — how often they're on-site and what triggers an unscheduled check
  • Reporting format — written reports after every visit, with photographs when something warrants documentation; "all good" by text is not a property update
  • Spending authorization — a clear dollar threshold below which they can act on small repairs without calling you, and above which they contact you first
  • Emergency contact protocol — who gets called, in what order, and how fast
  • Access terms — who holds keys, who has alarm codes, what they're authorized to enter

This documentation isn't a bureaucratic formality. It's the mechanism that makes the arrangement work when you're in another state and something happens that requires a decision.

What does the first site visit tell you that no interview can?

A caretaker who hasn't walked the property before taking it on is a caretaker who doesn't know the place. That matters immediately the first time something goes wrong, and it matters on every routine visit — because the whole value of professional caretaking is that the person knows what normal looks like for your specific property.

The site visit also tells you something about how they work. Do they ask about the systems — the irrigation setup, the HVAC, the propane or water supply configuration? Do they walk the exterior perimeter, check the drainage, look at what the seasonal exposure actually is? Do they ask about the quirks — the door that sticks, the pressure regulator that's been slow, the area of the roof that needs watching?

A caretaker who shows up and takes notes is showing you how they'll work when you're not there. One who does a quick walk and moves straight to the price conversation is showing you the same thing.

How to apply it

If you're starting from scratch, work through this in order:

Step 1 — Get a referral, not a search result. Ask a local real estate agent, an estate attorney, or a neighbor who already uses a caretaker in the same area. A verified referral outweighs any online profile.

Step 2 — Ask the wrong-thing question in the reference call. "When something went wrong, what did they do?" is the only reference question that surfaces character under pressure.

Step 3 — Confirm scope clarity on California handyman law before the conversation goes further. If they can't explain the $1,000 threshold and what it covers, stop there.

Step 4 — Request written terms before any access is granted. Scope, visit schedule, reporting format, spending authorization, emergency contacts — all in writing, all agreed before a key is handed over.

Step 5 — Schedule the site visit. Let them walk the property, ask questions, and show you how they think about it. That walk is the interview.

The difference between a caretaker who works and one who doesn't usually shows up in steps two and five — not in whether they have a nice website or a long client list.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a license or certification I should look for in a property caretaker?

There is no state license for residential property caretaking in California. The relevant credential question is whether a caretaker who performs repairs understands and operates within legal unlicensed handyman scope — under approximately $1,000 combined materials and labor for work not requiring a permit. Beyond that, vetting relies on references, a site visit, and written terms.

How much does it cost to hire a vacation home caretaker?

Cost depends on property size, location, visit frequency, and scope of work. A visit-based arrangement with biweekly checks and written reporting typically costs less than full property management while covering more of the hands-on maintenance and monitoring an absent owner actually needs. Pricing is agreed per property after a scope review.

What's the difference between hiring a caretaker and using a home watch service?

The terms are often used interchangeably. A home watch service typically means scheduled visits and written reporting with no repair or grounds work included. A full caretaking arrangement adds small repairs, seasonal work, contractor coordination, and sometimes resident care. Which structure fits depends on what the property actually needs.

What should a written caretaking report include after each visit?

A useful visit report includes the date, a brief exterior and interior summary, any issues found and what was done or recommended, photographs of anything notable, and confirmation of system status — water, HVAC, irrigation. A report that says "all good" is not a report. It's an occupancy confirmation, and that's not what you're paying for.

What happens when something requires a licensed contractor?

A caretaker working at full scope identifies the issue, documents it, contacts you with a specific assessment, sources qualified local vendors, coordinates site access, and oversees the work on your behalf. You are not project-managing a repair from across the state with a contractor who doesn't know you or your property.
