Resident Caretaker & Home Watch — Private Property Care

2026-10-22

If you own a second home or vacation property in Northern California, the honest question is simple: does anyone reliable actually know what's happening there right now? Not a contractor who won't return calls for a job under a few thousand dollars. Not a house-sitter from an app who showed up once and doesn't know where the water shutoff is. One person — with real skills, a defined scope, and written terms — who takes responsibility for the property while you're away. That's what this arrangement is.

Who This Is For

Second-home owners, estate holders, and frequently absent homeowners — mostly in their 40s to 60s, financially established, with a property base in Northern California. A vacation cabin in the Sierras. A wine-country estate. A coastal property used three months a year. You own more house than you can actively manage from a distance, and you've already learned what happens when no one reliable is watching: the slow leak that runs for weeks before you find it, the broken gate nobody mentioned, the yard that announces exactly how long you've been gone.

You're not looking for a property manager with a portfolio and a commission structure built around rental income. You want one person you can call who already knows the house, knows what belongs there, and will tell you honestly when something is wrong.

What's Included

The scope is agreed per property — not a packaged tier. A base arrangement typically covers:

- **Scheduled property checks** — interior and exterior walk-throughs at agreed intervals, with a written record after every visit. Not a text saying "all good." An actual dated report, specific to your property, with photographs of anything worth documenting. - **Grounds and seasonal oversight** — lawn, irrigation, storm debris, seasonal tasks tied to the property's own calendar, not a generic checklist. - **Seasonal opening and closing** — winterizing, startup prep, storm readiness. The property is in the right condition before you arrive and properly closed before a long vacancy. This is where deferred damage accumulates; it's also where it gets caught. - **Small repairs** — work that falls within California's unlicensed handyman scope (jobs under roughly $1,000, no permit required). The stuff that never makes a contractor's calendar and sits undone for months. It gets handled before it becomes a contractor-sized problem. - **Contractor coordination** — when licensed trade work is needed, the caretaker sources the vendor, provides site access, and oversees the job on your behalf. You are not project-managing a repair from another state with a contractor who doesn't know you. - **Pet and animal care** — where it fits the arrangement, provided by someone who knows the property and the animals. Not a rotating stranger from an app. - **Written owner updates** — after every visit, in writing, specific to what was checked and what was found. You know what's happening at the property without having to ask twice.

Resident or visit-based structure is matched to the property's actual situation. Work-trade arrangements — where housing is provided in exchange for caretaking duties — are available for properties where that structure fits.

How It Works

The process starts with a direct conversation and a site visit. The caretaker needs to know the property — the systems, the quirks, the seasonal exposures — before any scope is set. From there: agreed terms in writing covering visit frequency, specific responsibilities, reporting format, emergency contact protocol, and authorization for any spending. No access changes hands before that agreement is in place.

Once the arrangement is active, the owner gets a written report after every check. Issues that can be handled within scope are handled. Issues that require licensed trade work are escalated with a specific assessment — not a vague heads-up — and coordinated on the owner's behalf. There is no ambiguity about who is responsible for what. One person. One agreement. One property.

Why It Works

The reason the gap exists is structural. Contractors are built for large jobs. Sitting apps are built for occupancy, not maintenance. Property managers are built around rental income — their overhead requires a cash flow to justify the commission. None of those models serve the absent owner who wants the property maintained, not monetized.

This arrangement is built around exactly that situation. A single named person with unlicensed handyman capability, caretaking experience, knowledge of Northern California property conditions, and a referral-first reputation. The scope is honest about what can be done without a license and what requires a licensed contractor. The reporting is specific and in writing. The relationship is direct — not routed through a dispatch system, not shared across a portfolio.

The practical result: small problems get caught before they compound. Seasonal damage gets prevented rather than repaired. The owner arrives to find the property as it should be, and leaves without the low-grade dread that comes from not knowing what's accumulating while they're away.

The Main Objection Answered

*"I don't know you."*

That's correct, and it's the right instinct. This doesn't start with a contract — it starts with a referral or a direct conversation, a site visit, and written terms that define exactly who is on your property and what they are authorized to do before any money or access changes hands. A house-sitting app sends you a stranger with an anonymous review score. This arrangement starts with a person who can be vouched for, a scope you've agreed to in writing, and a structure that puts accountability on the record from day one.

Terms and Next Step

Scope and pricing are agreed per property — visit frequency, specific responsibilities, and whether the arrangement is paid, work-trade, or a documented hybrid are all determined by what the property actually requires. There is no standard tier, no management percentage, and no portfolio model. Pricing is not published here because it isn't fixed: a seasonal check schedule for a cabin costs less than a resident arrangement for an estate, and the agreement should reflect that.

To move forward: reach out to start a conversation about the property. What it is, where it is, how often it's occupied, and what the owner currently does — or doesn't have — for oversight. From there, a site visit and a written scope proposal. No commitment before both sides know the arrangement is the right fit.
