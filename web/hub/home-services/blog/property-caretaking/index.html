Resident Caretaker & Home Watch — Private Property Care

2026-10-22

You own a second home — a Sierra cabin, a wine-country estate, a coastal property used three months a year. And the honest answer is that nobody reliable is watching it right now. Not a person who already knows the place, not someone who will notice the slow leak before it becomes a ceiling, not anyone accountable for what happens between your visits. This is that person. One named individual, one agreed scope, one property at a time.

The Low-Grade Dread Has a Source

You know what can go wrong. A pressure regulator starts failing quietly. A pest finds an entry point in September and by March it's a significant problem. A gate latch backs out — nobody mentions it. You come back to something that's been running for weeks, or a yard that looks like you've been gone a lot longer than you have. The dread isn't irrational. It's accurate. The property is exposed, and no one with real skills and real accountability is watching it.

Contractors won't return calls for jobs under a few thousand dollars — the small stuff never gets done. House-sitting platforms send strangers who have no idea how to handle a minor repair, won't document anything, and won't call you before something gets expensive. Property managers are built around rental income and tenant relations; if you're not monetizing the property, you're a mismatch for their model. None of those options produce what you actually need.

What Changes When the Property Has a Person

You stop managing it. You start receiving updates. The place is on a set schedule — checks happen, small problems get handled before they reach contractor scale, seasonal prep happens on the property's own calendar, and anything requiring a licensed trade contractor gets sourced, scheduled, and overseen on your behalf. You are not project-managing a repair from another state with a contractor who doesn't know you. You arrive to find the place as it should be. You leave without the dread.

One person who already knows the property is categorically different from a dispatch system. Familiarity is the mechanism — it's what catches the failing pressure regulator before it becomes an emergency, notices the pest entry point before September becomes March, and produces a specific assessment instead of a vague heads-up.

What the Scope Covers

The scope is agreed per property, in writing, before work begins. Base service covers:

Scheduled property checks — interior and exterior walk-throughs at agreed intervals, with a written report after every visit. Not a text saying "all good." An actual record, dated and specific, with photographs of anything that warrants documentation.

Grounds and seasonal oversight — lawn maintenance, irrigation management, storm debris, and anything tied to the property's seasonal calendar.

Seasonal opening and closing — winterizing, startup prep, storm readiness, and a documented condition report so you know what you're arriving to before you walk through the door.

Contractor coordination — when licensed trade work is needed, the caretaker sources the vendor, provides site access, and oversees the work on your behalf.

Small repairs — sub-threshold work within California's unlicensed handyman scope (jobs under roughly $1,000, no permit required) handled before they reach contractor scale. The line between what falls here and what requires a licensed trade is clear and honest before any work begins.

Pet care — where it fits the arrangement, provided by someone who knows the property and the animals, not a rotating stranger from an app.

Resident or visit-based structure is matched to what the property actually requires. A seasonal check schedule with defined visits costs less than a resident arrangement and still covers the gap.

How It Starts

The engagement starts with a direct conversation and a site visit. The caretaker walks the property with you — the systems, the quirks, the seasonal exposures. Scope, visit frequency, reporting format, emergency contact protocol, and any spending authorization are agreed in writing before a key changes hands. From there, the property is on schedule.

Pricing is paid, work-trade, or a documented hybrid — determined by the property and the agreement. No packaged tiers, no management percentage, no portfolio model. One person. One agreement. One property at a time.

The Objections Worth Answering

"I don't know you." That's the starting point, not a problem to talk past. This begins with a referral or a direct conversation, a site visit, and written terms before any money or access changes hands. You will know exactly who is on your property, what they are authorized to do, and what they are responsible for reporting.

"I already have a property manager." Property managers are built around rental income. If you're not renting the property out, their systems and their staff are built for a different situation. This is a different function — one person, built around the owner's direct interest in the property.

"A house-sitter costs less." A house-sitter from an app is an unvetted stranger with no repair skills, no accountability structure, and no written reporting. The cost difference is the difference between someone occupying the house and someone actually caring for it.

"I'm not sure I need this full-time." The scope is matched to the property. The agreement is built around what the property actually requires, not a packaged tier.

One Clear Next Step

Scope and terms are agreed per property — there is no packaged price because the arrangement is built around what the property actually requires. The first step is a conversation, not a contract.
