What a Municipal Vacation Check Actually Covers — And the Gaps That Leave Second Homes Exposed

2026-10-26

Municipal vacation check programs are free, widely available across Northern California counties, and genuinely useful — for the use case they were built for. A seasonal second home is not that use case. Here is exactly where the program stops and where your exposure begins.

The news in brief

Vacation check programs run by sheriff and police departments exist in most Northern California counties. A patrol officer adds your address to a route and performs exterior visual passes while you're away — at no cost to the homeowner. The program is designed for primary residences during short absences, not for seasonal second homes sitting empty for months at a time.

What happened

According to the pillar research underlying this post, the mechanics of a municipal vacation check are straightforward: you register your property with the local sheriff or police department before you leave, a patrol officer adds the address to a route, and on some number of unspecified passes — daily, every few days, or whenever patrol density allows — someone drives by or walks the perimeter. If the front door is standing open or a window is broken, dispatch gets a call. If everything looks intact from the driveway, the pass is logged and the route continues. That is the complete scope. The officer has never been inside the house, does not have a key, and will not enter under normal circumstances. The program's underlying assumption is that someone will be back soon, the neighbor knows you're gone, and anything seriously wrong will be visible from the street. A seasonal property changes every one of those assumptions.

Why it matters

For second-home owners in Northern California — a Sierra cabin, a wine-country estate, a foothills property that sits empty from November through March — the gap between what a vacation check covers and what the property actually needs is significant and specific.

The program does not cover interior conditions. A water heater leaking in the mechanical room, a roof slow-dripping into insulation after a November storm, a rodent entry point behind the garage — none of these are visible from a driveway pass. In rural counties like El Dorado, Nevada, Placer, Sonoma, and Mendocino, patrol coverage thins out fast. A cabin twenty minutes off a main road may see a check once a week or not at all, depending on call volume and staffing. There is no service-level agreement. There is no accountability if the check does not happen.

Northern California's conditions make this more consequential than it might be elsewhere. A deep freeze in January can split supply lines if the heat setpoint wasn't left high enough and nobody is checking that the HVAC is actually running. Heavy wet snow in February can do structural damage to a deck already carrying deferred maintenance. Spring snowmelt turns into a drainage problem if the gutters are packed from fall. A late frost event in wine country can damage irrigation systems and unprotected pipes in outbuildings. Fire-season prep — clearing defensible space, confirming water storage, checking that the generator runs — has a real deadline that does not care whether you happen to be at the property.

Beyond detection, there is no repair capability. A patrol officer who notices a branch on the fence line notes it and moves on. The dripping exterior faucet that will freeze and split the supply line before you get back is outside their scope even if they see it. Vendor and contractor access — propane delivery, well service, septic, pest control — all require someone present with a key and authority to let work proceed. The vacation check program does not provide that.

Our perspective

The market currently offers three options for unoccupied second homes, and none of them solve the problem completely. Full property management is built around rental income — the model requires a rent roll to justify the management fee, which means it does not fit a property used personally or rented only occasionally. House-sitting apps send a stranger who is occupying the house, not caring for it — no repair skills, no accountability structure beyond a star rating, no written record of what was checked. Municipal vacation checks are a patrol-based exterior visual, free, availability-dependent, and designed for a different use case entirely.

The gap between those three options is where the actual problem lives. Licensed contractors in rural Northern California will not schedule a job under a few thousand dollars — that is an economics problem, not an attitude problem. A plumber who drives forty-five minutes to look at a dripping faucet is losing money on the call. So the faucet drips, the list of small things grows, nothing moves, and the owner arrives to find that the list has turned into a budget because nobody caught the items when they were still cheap.

What real coverage requires for a seasonal second home is a set of functions assigned to one accountable person with interior access, a written inspection record, the ability to handle small problems before they scale, and a direct communication line to the owner specific enough to be useful. 'Everything looks fine' is not a report. 'HVAC is running, water heater normal, checked under sinks, no moisture, back fence gate latch was loose and I tightened it' is a report. A private caretaker operating under a written agreement — with a defined scope, a known visit schedule, and handyman-range repair capability for work that falls under California's unlicensed threshold (jobs under approximately $500 in combined labor and materials not requiring a permit) — is the functional structure that fills this gap. It is not a packaged product any existing service category is currently built to deliver.

What happens next

Second-home owners relying on municipal vacation checks heading into a winter absence should treat this as a decision point, not a safety net. The practical questions worth resolving before the property goes unoccupied: Who has interior access and a key? Who is responsible for confirming the HVAC is running and the heat setpoint held through a freeze event? Who coordinates the propane fill, the septic service, the well check? Who handles the repair that is too small for a licensed contractor and too important to leave? Who writes a report after each visit and sends it to the owner in plain language? If the honest answer to any of those is 'nobody,' the vacation check program is not filling that gap — it was never built to.
