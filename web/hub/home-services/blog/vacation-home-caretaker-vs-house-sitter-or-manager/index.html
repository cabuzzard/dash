Vacation Home Caretaker vs. House Sitter or Manager

2026-10-23

You own a second home in Northern California. It might be a Sierra cabin you use in summer, a wine-country property you visit on weekends, or a coastal place occupied three months a year. You're not there most of the time, and you know — honestly know — that nobody reliable is watching it. You've thought about a property manager, looked at a house-sitting app, maybe asked a neighbor to "keep an eye on it." None of those feel quite right, and you're not sure why. This article explains exactly why — and what the right option actually is.

The short answer

A professional vacation home caretaker is a named individual who takes agreed responsibility for your property on a defined schedule, handles small repairs within legal unlicensed scope, coordinates contractors for larger work, and documents every visit in writing. A house sitter occupies the property without maintaining it. A property manager is built around rental income, not owner care. A neighbor check is goodwill — not accountability. The gap between these options is where most second-home problems get expensive.

What does a house sitter actually provide — and where does it stop?

A house sitter's job is presence. They stay in the property, which discourages opportunistic intrusion and keeps the place from looking empty. That's the full scope. They are not going to notice the drop in water pressure that signals a failing pressure regulator. They are not going to fix the door latch that keeps backing out of the frame. They are not going to document anything, source a plumber, or call you before a small problem becomes a contractor-scale expense.

House-sitting platforms also introduce a structural problem: the person walking through your property is, by design, a stranger. There's no site history, no knowledge of your specific systems, and no accountability structure when something goes wrong. When it does go wrong — and something eventually does — you're managing it from a distance with someone who has no real skin in the game.

The cost difference between a house sitter and a professional caretaker is not cheap versus expensive. It's occupancy confirmation versus actual care.

Why doesn't a property manager solve the problem?

Property management is a model built around rental income. The commission structure, the staff, the systems — all of it is designed for landlord-tenant relationships, occupancy rates, and monthly cash flow. If you're not renting the property out, a property manager is an expensive mismatch for what you actually need.

The absent owner who wants the property maintained — not monetized — needs a different function entirely. One person. One agreement. One property. No portfolio overhead, no commission on revenue you're not generating, no rotating faces walking through the house under a corporate umbrella.

Professional caretaking sits in the gap that property management doesn't reach. That gap is real, and it isn't small.

What makes a professional caretaker different from a neighbor doing a check?

A neighbor check is a favor. It is goodwill, not accountability, and you cannot hold goodwill to a standard. When a neighbor walks through and misses the slow leak under the sink, they didn't fail a job — they did a favor imperfectly. There's no scope, no documentation, no defined visit frequency, and no protocol for what happens when something needs action.

A professional caretaker operates on written terms: defined visit schedule, documented scope, written report after every visit, clear authorization thresholds for spending and repair. "All good" is not a property update. A real caretaking arrangement produces a dated record — interior and exterior notes, photographs of anything that warrants documentation, a log the owner can read and trust.

The difference between a neighbor's text and a caretaker's written visit report is the difference between a feeling and a fact.

How to apply it

If you're deciding which level of care fits your property, work through these three questions:

1. How long is the property vacant between your visits? If the answer is weeks or months, a house sitter — even a reliable one — doesn't cover the maintenance and monitoring function you need. A visit-based caretaker with a defined schedule does.

2. Are you generating rental income from the property? If yes, a property manager may make sense — their model fits rental relationships. If no, you're paying for infrastructure designed for someone else's situation. A caretaking arrangement is matched to owner care, not tenant management.

3. Do you have one person you could call right now who knows the property, has the skills to handle a minor repair, and would give you a written account of what they found? If the honest answer is no — not really — that's the gap this service is built to fill.

A resident or visit-based caretaker takes agreed scope, owns it completely, and keeps you informed in writing. The property stops being a low-grade worry every time you're away.

Frequently asked questions

Can't I just use a home watch company instead of an individual caretaker?

Home watch companies vary widely. Some operate with trained, consistent staff; others rotate personnel. The critical difference is whether the person visiting your property knows it — its systems, its seasonal quirks, its baseline normal. A named individual caretaker with a site history is categorically different from a dispatch system, regardless of the company's branding.

What does a caretaker actually report after each visit?

A visit report should be dated and specific: exterior walk covering roof (visible from grade), gutters, foundation perimeter, and all entry points; interior check of every room, plumbing fixtures, HVAC function, and visible moisture or pest signs; grounds status including irrigation, storm debris, and fencing; utility and system levels. Photographs accompany anything that warrants documentation. A text saying "all looks fine" is not a visit report.

Does a caretaker replace contractors, or work alongside them?

A caretaker handles small repairs within California's unlicensed handyman scope — generally work under approximately $1,000 combined materials and labor that does not require a permit. When licensed trade work is needed, the caretaker sources the vendor, coordinates site access, and oversees the job on your behalf. You are not managing a repair from another state with a contractor who doesn't know you.

What if I only need someone for seasonal opening and closing, not year-round?

Seasonal opening and closing is a legitimate standalone scope. A caretaker who knows the property handles winterizing, storm prep, irrigation shutdown, and startup systematically — not as a one-time favor but as a documented service with written terms. Many ongoing caretaking relationships begin with exactly this scope.

Is this service only for large estates or high-value properties?

No. The arrangement fits any property whose owner is absent long enough that problems accumulate between visits. A smaller cabin with fewer systems needs less coverage — which means lower cost, not a different category of service. The scope is matched to the property.
