What to Put in a Property Caretaker Agreement Before You Hand Over the Keys

2026-10-10

Most caretaker arrangements that go sideways do not fail because the wrong person walked through the door. They fail because nobody wrote anything down. The handoff happens over a phone call, a few texts, maybe a quick walkthrough, and everyone leaves with a different understanding of what was agreed — and six weeks later, the owner comes home to a repair that was not done, a pet routine that drifted, or a work-trade calculation that neither party can reconstruct. For homeowners in Northern California setting up any kind of caretaker arrangement — paid, work-trade, or a negotiated mix of both — a written agreement is not a formality. It is the document that makes the arrangement real.

The Basics and the Scope of Care

Start with the terms that are easy to skip because they feel obvious. Full legal names. The property address. Precise start and end dates. A line that spells out which structure applies — paid stay, work-trade, or hybrid. If it is paid, the rate and payment schedule belong here. If it is work-trade, the nightly or weekly value assigned to the stay goes here too, because that number is the denominator against which all labor will be calculated. Getting this wrong at the start creates disputes at the end. In California, any ambiguity in a service agreement tends to get resolved in the direction of whoever was not paid — which is reason enough to be explicit before the keys change hands.

The scope of care is where most agreements fall short, and where the document either earns its keep or fails both parties. This section needs to be specific enough that anyone reading it cold could execute the work. Vague language like "take care of the dog" has caused real problems. Explicit language does not.

For animals, that means species, names, feeding schedules, medications with dosages and timing, vet contact information, and clear instructions for anything that requires more than filling a bowl — aquariums, ponds, small livestock, birds, animals on medical routines. For the yard and property systems, the same standard applies: which irrigation zones run on what schedule, whether the drip system needs manual checking, how often the lawn gets mowed, what to do if a sprinkler head fails. None of this is complicated to write. It is only complicated to reconstruct after a dispute. Write it like an instruction manual, not a text message, and the scope section becomes the most useful part of the whole document.

Repairs, Access, and Emergency Protocols

The repair and maintenance section is where the agreement protects both parties simultaneously. In California, unlicensed handyman work is legal for jobs that come in under roughly one thousand dollars in combined labor and materials and do not require a permit. This is not a gray area — it is a defined legal threshold, and a well-written agreement stays inside it by being explicit about what is included and what is not. A list of agreed repairs with realistic estimated costs is more useful than a general description of "light handyman work." If the arrangement is work-trade, each repair should carry an estimated labor value, and the agreement should state how that value gets credited against the cost of the stay. It should also establish what happens if a job turns out to be bigger than the estimate — typically, the owner gets notified before additional cost is incurred, and anything that crosses into permit-required territory gets flagged for a licensed contractor, not absorbed into the arrangement. This protects the caretaker from liability and the owner from work that was not legal to do.

Access and security terms are often left out entirely, which is a mistake. The agreement should specify which keys and access codes the caretaker holds, whether guests are permitted on the property and under what conditions, how mail and deliveries are handled, and what the expectation is around the house appearing occupied — lights varied, car visible, that kind of thing. This matters both for security and for homeowner's insurance purposes. An occupied home carries different risk than a vacant one, and a written record of the caretaking arrangement can be relevant if an insurance question comes up.

Emergency protocols deserve their own section — and they are the section everyone skips until they need them. The agreement should name a local emergency contact for the owner, someone the caretaker can reach directly if something happens and the owner is unreachable. It should specify a dollar threshold below which the caretaker can make a repair decision without prior approval, and above which the owner must be consulted first. It should also address what happens if the caretaker has a personal emergency and needs to leave the property — who gets notified, how quickly, and whether there is any provision for a handoff. None of this assumes bad faith. It builds a system that runs without drama when something unexpected happens, which is exactly when a verbal agreement proves it was never enough.

Work-Trade Accounting and Tenancy Language

For work-trade arrangements specifically, the final accounting process needs to be spelled out before the stay begins. When does the reconciliation happen — midway through a long stay, at the end, on a rolling weekly basis? Who documents the work, how is it submitted, and how are disagreements about scope or quality resolved? The simplest version is a short written log the caretaker keeps, with photos, submitted to the owner for sign-off. This is not bureaucracy. It is the record that makes the arrangement defensible if either party feels shortchanged. Owners who have done multiple work-trade stays consistently report that the ones that felt clean and fair were the ones where the accounting was visible throughout — not reconstructed at checkout.

A few terms that sound optional but are not: a statement that the arrangement does not create a landlord-tenant relationship, that the caretaker's occupancy is contingent on the agreed scope and dates, and that early termination by either party requires written notice with a defined window. California tenancy law can apply to occupancy arrangements in ways that surprise people who did not think they were creating one. A single line that addresses this explicitly — drafted carefully, ideally reviewed by a local attorney if the stay is extended — is far cheaper than finding out what it costs to resolve later.

The document does not need to be long. A well-written caretaker agreement for a typical Northern California property covers two to four pages. What it needs to be is complete — every assumption made explicit, every scope item named, every threshold defined. Two to four pages is all it takes to make a caretaker arrangement clean, and to make sure both parties are walking away from the handoff holding the same set of keys.

The agreement was never the relationship — it was the container for the relationship. Without it, the whole thing is just goodwill waiting to run out. The arrangement starts when the keys change hands. The agreement is what makes sure both parties are holding the same set.
