Multifamily Deferred Maintenance: Price the Physical Risk Before You Close

2026-10-20

Deferred maintenance is the most expensive line item most first-time multifamily buyers never see coming. It doesn't show up in the seller's proforma. It shows up in your bank account 14 months after closing, right when you thought the deal was starting to work. You price that risk before you make an offer, or you inherit it after — and here's exactly how you do that.

What Deferred Maintenance Actually Is — and Why the Definition Matters

Deferred maintenance is not the ugly carpet or the dated kitchen. Those are value-add scope — they cost money, they're predictable, they improve rent, and they're part of why you're buying below market. Deferred maintenance is what happens when a building's operating systems — roof, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, foundation drainage, parking surfaces — are allowed to age past their useful life without replacement or repair. The seller knows. Sometimes the property manager knows. Often neither of them volunteers it.

The distinction matters because cosmetic scope is visible, priceable, and usually priced into a discount already. Deferred mechanical and structural scope is invisible at first pass, often absent from the seller's disclosures, and capable of turning a 7-cap deal into a capital trap inside the first 18 months. When buyers talk about value-add deals that "looked right on paper," deferred maintenance is almost always the line item that wasn't on the paper.

A structured walkthrough treats every major system as a line item with an age, a condition grade, and a replacement cost. You're not trying to find everything — that's what an inspector is for. You're trying to identify which systems are inside or outside their expected useful life so you know what to stress-test in your numbers.

Here's what you're grading on that walkthrough:

**Roof:** Ask for the last replacement date. If the seller doesn't have it, look at flashing condition, membrane wear on a flat roof, or shingle granulation loss on a pitched one. A 20-year-old roof on a 40-unit building in a freeze-thaw climate is not a risk you ignore. Full replacement on a mid-size multifamily roof runs $8,000–$18,000 per building — model it.

**HVAC:** Per-unit systems are easier to scope because you price them individually. Central systems serving multiple units are where the liability concentrates. A 15-year-old package unit on a commercial multifamily property is end-of-life in most climates. Use $3,500–$7,000 per unit as a rough ceiling if you can't confirm the system age.

**Plumbing:** Galvanized steel pipe is the one that kills deals. If the building was constructed before 1975 and you see galvanized at any exposed point, assume the entire supply line is galvanized until proven otherwise. Repipe scope on a 20-unit building runs $40,000–$80,000. That's not a renovation cost — that's a capital event.

**Electrical:** Look for Federal Pacific or Zinsco panels. Both are fire hazards, both are uninsurable in most markets, and both require full replacement. Budget $2,500–$5,000 per panel plus labor to bring service to current code.

**Foundation and drainage:** Walk the perimeter. Look for efflorescence on concrete block, cracks at corners, or any grading that pitches toward the building. Foundation repair on multifamily is expensive and disruptive — treat it as a pass/fail flag at this stage, not a priceable line item.

Most buyers walk a multifamily property the way they'd walk a house they're buying for personal use. They look at units, check the parking lot, form a general impression — and miss every system that's about to break. The walkthrough is a diagnostic tool. Run it like one.

How to Use Public Records and Build Your Deferred Maintenance Number

You don't have to walk a property cold. Most counties make building permit history publicly searchable, and that record tells you when major systems were last replaced — or that they haven't been replaced at all.

Pull the property's permit history and sort by permit type. Look for roofing, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing permits. A roof replacement leaves a permit. An HVAC replacement on a commercial property almost always does. If there's no mechanical permit in the last 15 years on a 1980s-built apartment complex, that's signal — either the work was done without permits, which is a separate liability, or the systems haven't been touched.

County tax records also show you the year built and, in many jurisdictions, the last recorded major improvement. Cross-reference that against system useful life benchmarks:

- Flat roofing membrane: 15–20 years - Asphalt shingle roofing: 20–25 years - Packaged HVAC units: 12–15 years - Commercial tank water heaters: 8–12 years - Galvanized supply plumbing: 40–70 years (functionally failing at 50+) - Electrical panels: 25–40 years, depending on load and brand

If the building is 35 years old and the permit history shows no mechanical work, you're buying systems at or past end-of-life across the board. That's not automatically a reason to walk — it might be exactly why the price is where it is — but you need to know what you're pricing.

Once you've walked the property and pulled the permits, you have enough to build a working estimate. The goal is not precision — precision comes from the inspector. The goal is a credible range that stress-tests the deal before you commit earnest money to due diligence.

Use a per-unit framework because it scales cleanly across property sizes and lets you compare deals without rebuilding the model each time. A conservative deferred maintenance reserve for a value-add multifamily property with aging systems and limited permit history is $4,000–$8,000 per unit as a one-time capital event estimate, separate from your ongoing CapEx reserve. That's what you're pricing in addition to renovation scope, not instead of it.

If your walkthrough and permit research surface a specific critical system — a galvanized repipe, a roof at end-of-life, a panel replacement — price that separately and add it to the total. Don't let it disappear into a round number.

Value-add proformas show you the renovation cost per unit, the rent bump, the new cap rate after you do the work. What they almost never show you is the deferred maintenance cost sitting underneath all of that — the systems already at end-of-life before your renovation even starts. That $4,000–$8,000 per unit is not a pessimistic assumption. That's what it costs when a building has been run instead of maintained.

How Deferred Maintenance Changes Your Offer — and What You Still Won't Know

The seller's asking price implies a cap rate. That cap rate is calculated on current net operating income — rents minus operating expenses — and it doesn't account for capital you'll need to spend to get the building to performing condition. If you buy at the asking price and then spend $120,000 on deferred maintenance across a 20-unit building, your effective cost basis just went up $6,000 per unit. That changes your cash-on-cash return. It probably kills it in year one and year two.

The correct move is to price the deferred maintenance scope before you make an offer, then back it out of your basis:

**Seller's asking price:** $1,200,000 **Estimated deferred maintenance:** $120,000 **Adjusted effective basis:** $1,320,000 **Maximum offer price (at your target basis):** $1,080,000

That's a $120,000 offer reduction — not arbitrary negotiation, but a documented, line-item-justified position. Sellers who've been running a building into the ground often know it. A credible deferred maintenance estimate, even a walkthrough-level one, is a better negotiating tool than a counter without backup. You're not lowballing. You're showing exactly how the math works.

Now, the part most buyers want to skip: a walkthrough and permit research gets you 60–70% of the physical risk picture. The rest requires access you don't have before the offer is accepted. What stays hidden until formal inspection:

- Interior plumbing condition behind walls and under slabs - Electrical wiring condition beyond panel age - HVAC performance under load, not just operational status - Foundation condition below grade - Structural member condition in attic and crawl spaces - Evidence of prior water intrusion that was cosmetically patched

This is why your pre-offer deferred maintenance estimate needs a contingency buffer built in. If your line-item estimate lands at $90,000, reduce your offer by $115,000. The inspection will find things the walkthrough missed. Size your buffer so that when it does, you're already covered — not renegotiating from a weaker position after you've already gone hard on earnest money.

You can estimate deferred maintenance costs without a contractor by using system-age benchmarks and replacement cost ranges per unit: roughly $3,500–$7,000 per unit for HVAC, $2,000–$4,000 per unit for water heaters, $2,000–$4,000 per unit for roofing as rough planning figures, with galvanized repipe scoped separately at $40,000–$80,000 for a mid-size building. The goal is a credible range for offer negotiation — not a final scope document. That's what the inspector produces. What you're building right now is the number that tells you whether you should pay for the inspector at all.

Deferred maintenance doesn't announce itself. It hides behind seller disclosures that don't mention it, proformas that price the upside without pricing the risk, and walkthroughs that feel thorough but aren't structured as diagnostics. The buyers who get hurt by it aren't careless — they just skipped the step where you build the number before the deal gets emotional. Run the walkthrough, pull the permits, build the per-unit estimate, and let the math tell you what the offer should be. That's not pessimism. That's how you stay in the game long enough to actually do more deals.
