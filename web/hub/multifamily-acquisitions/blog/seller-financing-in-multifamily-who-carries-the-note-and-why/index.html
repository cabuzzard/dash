Seller Financing in Multifamily: Who Carries the Note and Why

2026-10-11

Seller financing in multifamily circulates endlessly in investing circles while the practical mechanics stay frustratingly vague. Everyone knows the tool exists. Almost no one knows how to find the owners who will actually use it. That gap — between knowing a structure exists and knowing who holds it and why — is exactly where most budget-conscious investors stall out. They make a few cold calls, get shut down fast, and conclude seller financing is real but not for them. The problem is not the tool. The problem is reaching out without understanding what makes a seller willing to carry a note in the first place.

What Seller Financing Actually Is

Owner financing happens when the seller of a property acts as the lender. Instead of receiving the full purchase price at closing from a conventional loan, the seller agrees to receive payments over time — principal and interest — directly from the buyer. The property itself secures the note, just as it would with a bank loan. The buyer gets into the deal without qualifying for institutional financing. The seller gets a stream of income, often at a better yield than parking that capital anywhere else.

On paper this sounds straightforward. In practice it requires a seller with a very specific set of circumstances, and identifying those circumstances before you pick up the phone is the difference between a productive conversation and a wasted morning. The seller who will carry a note is not doing you a favor. They have a rational financial reason to do it. Miss which one is driving them, and your pitch lands wrong.

Three Reasons an Owner Will Carry

The rational case for carrying a note almost always comes down to one of three things: tax position, yield, or operational exhaustion.

Tax position is the most common. An owner who bought a 20-unit building in 2008 for $800,000 and is looking at $2.4 million today has a capital gains problem. Sell outright, and they recognize the full gain in one tax year. Carry the note and receive payments over ten years, and the gain is spread across the life of the note — that is an installment sale, a legitimate tax deferral strategy, not a loophole. The seller is not being generous. They are being strategic. When you understand that, your outreach stops sounding like you are asking for a favor and starts sounding like you understand their situation.

The yield argument is the second driver, and it applies most clearly to long-term owners retiring out of active management. A seller sitting on a free-and-clear or nearly paid-off property has a reinvestment problem. If they sell to a conventional buyer, they pocket cash they then have to deploy somewhere. In the current environment, deploying that capital safely at a meaningful yield is not easy. A seller-financed note at six or seven percent, secured by real property they know intimately, is a better risk-adjusted return than most fixed-income alternatives — and they do not have to manage anything anymore. They get monthly checks. The debt is secured by a building they understand better than any underwriter will. For this seller, carrying the note is not a concession. It is an investment decision.

The third driver is operational exhaustion, and it shows up most often in properties that look rough from the outside — high vacancy, deferred maintenance, management problems ignored for years. The owner is not a distressed operator in the dramatic sense; they are simply done. They have been managing this building for fifteen years, it has become a burden, and they want out without the friction of a conventional sale process. These sellers are often reluctant to list because listing means inspections, buyer financing contingencies, deals falling through, and months of uncertainty. Seller financing, from their perspective, means a cleaner close, a motivated buyer who is not going to walk on an appraisal gap, and a transition out of active ownership on a timeline they control. They are not carrying your note because they are desperate. They are doing it because it solves their exit problem faster and more quietly than going to market does.

How to Find These Owners

Understanding the three drivers changes how you look for these owners. You are not searching for sellers who are willing to be generous. You are searching for sellers whose financial and operational situation makes carrying a note the rational choice.

That means your targeting needs to account for long holding periods. Owners who bought ten, fifteen, twenty years ago have embedded gains large enough to make the installment sale argument relevant. It means looking at free-and-clear or low-leverage properties, because an owner with a large existing mortgage cannot structure a seller-financed deal without either paying off the underlying loan first or getting the lender to agree to a wrap — which complicates things significantly. And it means paying attention to property condition and vacancy, because the owner of a half-empty building with a leaking roof is far more likely to want a clean, quiet exit than to fight through a conventional sale process with a buyer whose lender is going to kill the deal over deferred maintenance.

None of this shows up on a standard MLS listing. You cannot tell from a LoopNet page whether a property has been in the same family for eighteen years, whether the owner is 72 and wants to stop fielding maintenance calls, or whether the capital gains exposure makes an installment sale worth exploring. That information exists in county records — long ownership tenure is a matter of public record — in property condition signals, and in vacancy data. The buyers who close seller-financed multifamily deals are not smarter than the ones who keep striking out. They are working from better information about who owns what and what that owner's situation actually is.

Decision paralysis in this search happens when investors keep asking whether seller financing is possible in general rather than asking whether it is rational for this specific owner in this specific situation. Generalized research does not close deals. Seller profiles close deals. An owner who has held a building for twenty-two years and is looking at a seven-figure gain does not need to be sold on seller financing. They need to meet a buyer who already understands why it makes sense for them and is not going to waste their time explaining conventional loan rejections.

Properties that have not been listed, owned by long-tenure operators with low leverage and high embedded gains — often with some vacancy or condition issue that makes a conventional sale complicated — are exactly where seller-financed deals happen. That inventory is not on the MLS. It is not on LoopNet. It lives in the gap between public inventory and the owners who have not yet decided to sell, or who have decided but do not want the noise of a traditional process. A database pre-filtered for seller motivation signals and financing flexibility closes that gap faster than any amount of cold outreach to random owners.

When you know going in that a property has long ownership tenure, low debt, and vacancy or condition signals consistent with an exhausted operator, you are not guessing whether the conversation is worth having. You are walking into a situation where the seller's rational self-interest and your financing need point in the same direction. That alignment is not luck. It is what targeted sourcing produces when the targeting is built around seller psychology rather than property type alone. The investors who keep closing deals in overheated markets are not finding better properties on the same platforms everyone else uses — they are working from information that changes who they call and why.
