Maturing Apartment Debt and the Equity Gap: Finding Multifamily Owners Who Need a Buyer

2026-10-23

A wave of short-term apartment loans originated at 2021–2022 peak valuations is maturing now and through 2028 — and inside that wave is a specific, time-bounded acquisition opportunity for buyers who can identify the equity gap before the property reaches a broker's desk.

The news in brief

A significant volume of multifamily bridge loans originated between 2021 and 2023 — underwritten against peak valuations, low exit cap rates, and a refinancing environment that no longer exists — are maturing now and will continue to mature through 2028. Owners who cannot refinance without injecting fresh equity face a binary decision: fund the gap or sell. A real fraction of that universe represents motivated sellers who would rather close a negotiated deal today than fight a lender over extend-and-pretend terms.

What happened

Between 2021 and 2023, apartment owners originated large volumes of short-term floating-rate bridge debt against properties valued at cycle-peak assumptions: continued rent growth, low cap rates, and a coming refi window that would be at least as favorable as the entry. None of those assumptions held. Rates rose. Cap rates expanded. Operating costs — insurance, taxes, maintenance — rose alongside rents, compressing net operating income relative to original pro formas. The refinancing math that was supposed to work at maturity now often requires the owner to bring fresh equity to the table just to qualify for a new loan on an asset that may have softened in value since the original appraisal. For owners who have that equity and still want the asset, the problem is solvable. For a specific subset who do not, or whose return profile on injecting more capital no longer pencils, selling at a negotiated price today is the better outcome. Interest-rate cap expirations compound the pressure further: many bridge borrowers purchased caps as a loan condition, expected to exit before those caps expired, and are now running unhedged floating-rate exposure on assets they cannot cleanly sell or refinance. Complex capital stacks — common equity, preferred equity, and mezzanine layers stacked together — add additional friction, because multiple capital classes must be satisfied before the common equity holder sees proceeds.

Why it matters

For multifamily buyers, the practical opportunity is not the macro headline — it is the specific window that exists before a distressed owner calls a broker. Once a property with a genuine equity-gap problem reaches listed inventory, one of two things has already happened: the owner has exhausted private options, or the situation is well known to the market and the motivation is fully priced in. The off-market window — the six to eighteen months when an owner knows the maturity date is coming and the equity shortfall is real, but has not yet committed to a public sale — is where the actual opportunity lives. Buyers who wait for deals to arrive through conventional channels are arriving after the window has closed. The sequence has to run the other direction: identify the properties where the refinancing math is likely to fail, reach the owner before they list, and show up with a clean offer and a fast close at the moment they are making a binary decision.

Our perspective

The equity gap is a specific calculation, not a feeling, and that distinction is what makes this opportunity actionable. Working backwards from loan maturity data — CMBS remittance reports for transparent loan-level detail, county deed-of-trust recordings for bank and insurance company loans — you can surface origination dates, original balances, lender names, and maturity windows. Cross-reference those against current estimated values built from recent comp sales and today's submarket cap rates, and the properties where the math is tight become visible. Layer in occupancy signals — above-market vacancy in a submarket where stabilized assets are running full is a NOI problem, which is a valuation problem, which is a refinancing problem — and operator behavior signals like recent management turnover or rising maintenance complaints in public records, and you are not building a list of distressed properties. You are building a list of owners who may not yet know they are motivated sellers. At the negotiating table, motivated does not mean desperate. The owner's calculation is specific: your offer versus the refinancing path, measured on certainty of close, speed, and net proceeds after transaction costs. Price matters, but proof of capital, a short inspection period, and a realistic timeline often matter more. Seller financing structures — where the buyer retires or assumes existing debt and the seller carries a portion of equity — come up frequently enough in these situations that buyers who never raise the structure are leaving options on the table. This is an interpretation of the mechanics described in the source analysis, not a guarantee of any specific transaction outcome.

What happens next

The shape of this window depends heavily on lender behavior as the maturity wave crests through 2027 and 2028. If lenders extend-and-pretend at scale — granting modifications and rolling maturities forward — the pool of owners who actually reach a forced-sale decision stays smaller than the raw loan maturity data suggests. That outcome is not unprecedented; lenders have historically preferred a performing borrower on modified terms over the cost and timeline of foreclosure. What changes that calculus is loan performance. A loan where the borrower cannot cover debt service from property cash flow moves through workout faster than an underperforming-but-current loan. Watch NOI trends in submarkets with the highest loan maturity concentration — that is where first-mover deals will surface first. Regulatory pressure on regional banks carrying large CRE loan concentrations is a second variable worth tracking; that pressure can translate into more aggressive workout timelines than borrowers expect, accelerating the decision window for some owners.
