Fed Holds Rates But Multifamily Sellers Are Cracking: What the 2025 Financing Stalemate Means for Off-Market Deal Flow

2026-10-10

The Federal Reserve held its benchmark rate steady again in 2025, and for most of the financial press that is a story about inflation patience and monetary policy credibility. For a specific class of multifamily property owner, it is something closer to a deadline. Loans originated in 2021 and 2022 — when rates were near zero and buyers underwrote to aggressive cap rates — are hitting their maturity walls right now. The result is a quiet, accelerating movement of motivated sellers who are not showing up on LoopNet, are not filing for foreclosure, and are not calling brokers. They are calling their accountants.

The Trap Behind the Headlines

The commercial real estate press keeps framing 2025 as an institutional distress story. It is not — or at least, that framing misses where the real movement is happening. The actual pressure is one level below the institutional floor: smaller apartment buildings, 8 to 50 units, owned by individual operators and regional holding companies who borrowed against optimistic 2021 valuations and are now watching their debt service coverage ratios deteriorate in real time. These owners are not showing up in CMBS delinquency reports yet. They have not listed their properties. They are quietly exhausting private options before the maturity date forces a public resolution.

The mechanics are straightforward. A building purchased in 2022 with a three-year bridge loan at 4.5 percent is now facing a refinance environment where comparable debt costs 7.5 percent or more. If the property's net operating income has not grown to match — and in high-vacancy markets or poorly managed assets, it often has not — the math breaks. The owner cannot hold the existing loan, cannot service new debt at current rates, and cannot sell at a price that clears the original note without absorbing a visible loss in front of every future lender and partner. That trapped position is exactly what creates a motivated seller. Not someone who wants to negotiate, but someone who has run out of other options.

Secondary and tertiary markets are where this pressure is most concentrated. Sun Belt metros with strong rent growth absorbed a lot of the 2021–2022 vintage debt without the same strain because NOI actually grew into the new rate environment. Smaller markets did not — Midwest industrial cities, mid-sized Southeast metros, secondary Appalachian corridors. Owners in those markets who underwrote to 2021 assumptions are sitting on assets that have not performed as projected and cannot refi their way out. The same markets, not coincidentally, are where cash flow on a per-unit basis still makes sense for a buyer who sources below market. A building that does not pencil at asking price with bank financing frequently pencils on seller-carried terms at a negotiated discount.

The maturity wall for 2021 and 2022 bridge loans is not approaching. It is here. Owners are making exit decisions now, in private, before their lenders force a public resolution. The investor who thinks they have another year to build their sourcing network before this window opens is mistaken. The window does not announce itself.

Why Seller Financing Is the Seller's Best Option

Here is the mechanic that most coverage of the distressed market ignores: seller financing is not a favor the seller is doing you. It is frequently the seller's best available option. An owner who cannot sell at full price without taking a visible loss, cannot refinance into profitable debt service, and does not want to hand the keys back to the lender has one remaining path that preserves some recovery and avoids a foreclosure on their record. Carrying the note for a qualified buyer at a negotiated rate gives them monthly income, a clean exit from operational headaches, and a way to spread the tax impact of the sale. The buyer who understands this is not asking for a concession — they are offering the seller the only realistic outcome left.

A seller with a maturing loan and six months of runway will consider seller financing, partial carry, flexible closing timelines, and below-ask pricing that a seller with two years of cushion simply will not. That window of flexibility is not permanent. It exists in the middle of the owner's decision process — when they still have agency and enough time to structure a deal that works for both sides. By the time a property reaches public distress, the owner has already tried every private option. Waiting for foreclosure listings and auction notices is waiting for the moment when all the workable deals have already closed.

The practical implication for a budget-conscious investor is this: the capital requirement to enter commercial multifamily is not fixed by the asking price on public listings. It is a function of seller flexibility, financing structure, and the gap between a listed price and what a motivated seller will actually accept when a credible buyer appears privately with a workable offer. That gap is widest right now. An investor sourcing off-market is not competing with institutional buyers, who are largely sitting on the sidelines of smaller assets. They are competing with other individual investors who are either still searching the MLS or do not yet know how to find the seller before the listing exists.

What remains genuinely uncertain is how long this window holds. The Fed's current posture suggests rates stay elevated through at least the first half of 2025, which extends the pressure on overleveraged owners and keeps the motivated-seller pool replenishing. But any meaningful rate cut changes the refinancing calculus for a portion of these owners and reduces the negotiating leverage a buyer currently holds. The maturity wave for 2021 and 2022 vintage bridge loans is concentrated in a roughly 18-month window. Investors who move inside that window are working with structural tailwinds. Investors who wait for distress to become obvious are arriving after the deals that could have been structured quietly have already been resolved one way or another.

The Sourcing Problem Is the Only Problem

Traditional channels do not surface this inventory because it is definitionally not listed. The MLS does not carry it. LoopNet does not have it. Wholesalers occasionally touch the edges of it, but their incentive is to move volume fast, not to hand a buyer a seller-financed deal with clean motivation signals and documented financing flexibility. Every property that shows up as a motivated-seller deal on a public platform is a motivated-seller deal that already failed to close privately. By the time a distressed owner lists publicly, they have either tried and failed to find a direct buyer, or they are working with a broker who has already shopped it to their preferred list. Either way, you are not first — you are the backup option.

The deals that close with seller financing, creative terms, and below-market pricing close before they list. They close because one investor had access to the owner's situation before everyone else did. That access does not come from watching the MLS more closely. It comes from sourcing systems that surface motivation signals before the decision to list is ever made. The investor who wants to work this window has to operate outside the visible market entirely — which means building or accessing a sourcing system that targets motivated sellers in the right property profiles before those sellers make a public move.

A database purpose-built to track off-market multifamily with seller motivation signals and financing flexibility built into the record is not a shortcut. It is the actual mechanism. It does not teach you how the market works — it hands you the inventory that exists outside the market, pre-filtered for the two variables that determine whether a deal is closeable: the seller's pressure to exit and their willingness to be flexible on terms. In an environment where the Fed's patience is forcing overleveraged owners into exactly that position, that kind of sourcing reach is the difference between watching 2025 pass and closing in it.

The challenge is finding these owners before they exhaust the private options and end up in public distress — or before a competitor who has been building a direct sourcing system gets there first. The motivated-seller window is open right now. Inventory is moving through it quietly. The buyers closing off-market deals in 2025 are the ones who already built a system to find motivated sellers before those sellers ran out of options.

The Fed's patience on rates is not a neutral holding pattern for everyone in the multifamily market. For owners who borrowed at 2021 assumptions and cannot refi their way out, it is a countdown. The structured exits happening right now — seller financing, discounted payoffs, private negotiations — are not showing up in the headlines because they are closing before anyone lists anything. The investor who understands the mechanic behind the problem, builds the sourcing reach to find motivated sellers early, and shows up with a workable offer while the owner still has options is the investor who closes in this market. Everyone else is reading about deals that already happened.
