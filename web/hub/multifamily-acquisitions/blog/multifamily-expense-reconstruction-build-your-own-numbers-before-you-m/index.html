Multifamily Expense Reconstruction: Build Your Own Numbers Before You Make an Offer

2026-10-14

The number that kills the deal is almost never the price. It is the expense line you believed. An offering memorandum is a sales document — and the operating expenses inside it are built to make the building look like it earns more than it does. That is not fraud. That is presentation. For a limited-capital investor who cannot absorb a $40,000 underwriting error in year one, the gap between what the OM shows and what you will actually pay is exactly where acquisitions go wrong.

Throw the OM's Expense Stack Out

Most deal-finding content stops before it gets here. It will tell you how to source off-market properties, how to identify motivated sellers, how to structure creative financing — and then it goes quiet right at the moment you have to decide whether to write an earnest money check. This is that gap.

Closing it starts with a specific mental posture. You are not fact-checking the OM. You are treating it as irrelevant and constructing a parallel expense model using external benchmarks, local market data, and property-level specifics you gather independently. The OM tells you what to question. It is not a source of truth about what the building costs to operate.

Start with management fees, because this is where seller-occupied or self-managed properties do the most damage. A current owner who manages their own eight-unit building is paying themselves zero in management fees, so the OM shows zero. But you are not going to self-manage — or if you are, you should still model the cost of professional management. You need to know what the property yields without your own unpaid labor subsidizing the return. In most markets, third-party management on a small multifamily asset runs between six and ten percent of collected rents. For a building pulling $8,000 a month in gross rents, that is $480 to $800 per month the OM is not showing you. Over twelve months, that is a $5,760 to $9,600 shortfall in the expense column before you have looked at anything else.

Repairs and maintenance is where optimism does the most work. Sellers — especially long-term owners who have deferred work or who maintain things themselves — routinely report actual cash spent on maintenance rather than what a stabilized building of that age and condition should cost to run. The industry benchmark for repairs and maintenance on a value-add multifamily property is typically $600 to $1,000 per unit per year, depending on age, condition, and unit mix. A seller who spent $1,200 total on a ten-unit building last year is not showing you a property with low maintenance costs. They are showing you a building with deferred problems. Model unit count times the market benchmark, compare it to what the OM reports, and the delta tells you something real.

CapEx is the most consistently omitted line in seller-prepared financials — and the most punishing to ignore. A seller who replaced nothing last year shows zero in capital expenditures. But you are not buying last year. You are buying the next ten, and the roof does not care what the offering memorandum said. Reserve $250 to $500 per unit per year as a real operating cost, regardless of what actually got spent in the trailing period. A value-add acquisition that looks profitable before CapEx reserves and looks marginal after is not a deal — it is a liability dressed up in favorable gross rent numbers.

Utilities, Insurance, and Taxes at Your Rates — Not Theirs

Once management, maintenance, and CapEx are rebuilt from benchmarks, you move to the line items where seller-specific circumstances create the largest gaps between their numbers and yours.

Utilities require line-by-line verification against actual bills. If the property has any landlord-paid utilities — common area electric, water and sewer, trash, gas — you pull the utility bills yourself during due diligence and model those costs at current rates, not at whatever the seller averaged over a favorable billing period. Water and sewer expenses in particular have been climbing across most secondary and tertiary markets. A seller who locked in favorable rates during an earlier period, or who simply reports utility costs selectively, can make a property look considerably more profitable than it will be under your ownership at today's rates.

Insurance is another place where seller financials routinely understate. A long-term owner with a grandfathered policy, a claims-free history, or a favorable prior relationship with their carrier will show premium costs that are not reproducible for a new buyer. Request a fresh quote from a commercial lines broker based on the property's actual characteristics before you finalize your expense model. In markets where property insurance has repriced significantly — coastal zones, areas with recent weather losses, markets where carrier withdrawal has reduced competition — the difference between the seller's premium and your real cost can run several thousand dollars per year on a small building.

Property taxes deserve the same skepticism. In many jurisdictions, a sale triggers reassessment. The tax figure in the OM reflects what the current owner pays — often a rate locked in years ago, before appreciation, before a renovation, before the market moved. That number has no bearing on what you will owe after you close. Research the local assessor's reassessment methodology before you underwrite and model taxes at the post-acquisition rate. This is not a minor adjustment on a value-add multifamily deal. In markets with aggressive reassessment schedules, the swing between the seller's current bill and your first-year obligation can reshape the entire return profile of the acquisition.

The complete rebuilt stack looks like this: management at six to ten percent of collected rents regardless of whether the current owner self-manages; repairs and maintenance at $600 to $1,000 per unit per year benchmarked to age and condition; CapEx reserves at $250 to $500 per unit per year; utilities at current billing rates pulled directly from the provider; insurance at a quote you obtained yourself; taxes at post-acquisition assessed value, not the seller's current rate. Every single one of those numbers comes from you — not from the OM.

Recalculate NOI, Then Set Your Price

When you have rebuilt the expense stack with your own numbers, you recalculate net operating income. Then you apply your required cap rate to that NOI and you get your offer price. Not the seller's asking price. Not a negotiated discount from the OM. Your price, grounded in what you know the building will cost to operate.

This is the step where most below-market deals either get made or get walked away from with information. The properties that appear to be cash-flowing strongly at asking price often stop looking that way once you replace the seller's expense figures with real ones. That is not a failure. That is the underwriting doing what it is supposed to do. The investor who closes on a deal with the seller's expense stack unexamined is not buying a cash-flowing property — they are buying the seller's story about the property.

The sourcing problem and the underwriting problem are connected at the hip. Finding a deal the market has not touched yet — a motivated seller, flexible on terms, below-market — is real. It happens. But the investor who finds that deal and then accepts the OM's operating expenses at face value has done the hard work and skipped the essential step.

When the rebuilt NOI comes back strong, you have conviction behind the offer. When it comes back thin or negative, you know before you write the earnest money check — not after you close and absorb the shortfall. Either outcome is useful. Neither is available to you if you underwrite against the seller's numbers.

The deal you source is only as good as your ability to know, before you make an offer, what it actually costs to own. That knowledge does not come from the offering memorandum. It comes from building your own model, line by line, from the outside in.

Value-add underwriting is not about finding the hidden upside in a property — it is about refusing to accept the seller's version of the downside. Rebuild the expense stack yourself every time, before every offer. The investor who does that consistently is not more sophisticated than the one who doesn't. They are just working from real numbers instead of a sales document.
