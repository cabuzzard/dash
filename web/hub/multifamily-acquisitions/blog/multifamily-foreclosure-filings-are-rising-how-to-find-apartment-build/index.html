Multifamily Foreclosure Filings Are Rising: How to Find Apartment Buildings in Default Before They Hit the Auction Block

2026-10-13

Multifamily foreclosure filings have been climbing steadily through 2026, and the pattern is not random. It is concentrated in small and mid-size apartment portfolios — the ten-unit to eighty-unit range — where owners took on floating-rate debt in 2021 and 2022 and are now bleeding at seven percent debt service on buildings that only cash-flowed at four. The most negotiable deals in commercial real estate are sitting in that gap, between the first missed payment and the courthouse auction. Most investors never see them.

The Pre-Foreclosure Window Is Where the Deal Lives

By the time a foreclosure auction is scheduled and publicly noticed, you are competing with cash buyers, hedge fund scouts, and every wholesaler in the county who scraped the same court filing. The price reflects that competition. The terms do not reflect a motivated seller — they reflect a distressed one who has run out of road.

That distinction matters more than anything else in this sourcing strategy. A motivated seller still has choices. A distressed seller at auction does not. Price flexibility, seller financing, extended close timelines, creative structure — those conversations happen with owners in the first category, not the second. Which means you need to be inside the pre-foreclosure window, before anyone else knows where to look.

The inventory that actually moves at favorable terms is never public. A small apartment owner who has missed two debt payments and knows a notice of default is coming is not posting on LoopNet. They are sitting on a problem they have not solved yet. That is the exact moment when a buyer who shows up with a real offer, flexible structure, and a clean close can negotiate terms that a courthouse auction never allows.

This distress cycle in small multifamily is being driven by debt maturity and rate reset — not vacancy collapse. Many of these buildings still have paying tenants and decent rent rolls. That is actually better for a buyer. You are not inheriting an operational disaster. You are stepping into a capital structure problem the current owner cannot solve. Improving the capital structure is what acquisition does. The building itself, in many cases, is functioning.

What nobody can tell you right now is how quickly lenders move from workout conversations to formal foreclosure on these files, or whether a meaningful rate shift in 2027 gives struggling owners a refinance path out. If rates compress, some of the pre-foreclosure inventory you are tracking will close its window without ever transacting. That is a real risk to the sourcing timeline. It is why working this pipeline with urgency matters now in a way it might not in eighteen months.

The Workflow: Pull Filings, Trace Ownership, Make Contact

Foreclosure in most jurisdictions begins with a notice — a lis pendens in states that use judicial foreclosure, a notice of default or notice of trustee's sale in non-judicial states. These are public records. They are filed at the county recorder's office, indexed by property address and owner name, and in most counties they are available online through the recorder's or clerk's database at no cost. The filing date matters. A lis pendens filed last week on a twelve-unit building is a very different opportunity than one filed eight months ago that is now sixty days from auction.

The practical workflow: pull foreclosure filings from the county recorder at least weekly in your target market. Filter for residential income property — in most recording systems, property type is listed alongside the filing, or you cross-reference the address against the county assessor database to confirm unit count and property class. Commercial multifamily typically shows up as apartment, residential income, or under a specific property use code that varies by county. Once you have confirmed it is a multifamily asset, trace ownership. Pull the assessor record to identify the vesting name — the LLC, trust, or individual that holds title — then use that name to find contact information. Most LLC owners are reachable through the Secretary of State business entity search in whatever state the entity was formed.

This is where most investors stop. They pull the filing, find the name, and either cannot locate a contact or send a generic piece of mail that reads like every other letter the owner has already thrown away. The approach that actually opens a conversation is different. You are not a buyer making an offer. You are someone who noticed a problem and has a direct path to solving it. That framing — specific, practical, no pressure — is what gets a callback from a seller who is embarrassed about the situation and tired of being circled by people who smell blood.

A defaulting owner is not looking for the highest price. They want the shortest path to resolution: stop the bleeding on debt service, avoid the reputational damage of a courthouse auction, and walk away with something rather than nothing. A buyer who opens the conversation by acknowledging the situation plainly — not prying, not predatory, just direct — and who can demonstrate a clean, certain close is more compelling than any mass-mail campaign. You are solving a problem they have not been able to solve. That is the pitch. Everything else is paperwork.

Secondary sources can supplement direct court record searches. PACER covers federal bankruptcy filings, which sometimes precede or run concurrent with state foreclosure — an owner who has filed Chapter 11 to stay a foreclosure is still a motivated seller, and filings are searchable by debtor name and district. Subscription platforms like ATTOM and PropertyRadar aggregate lis pendens and notice of default data with property characteristics pre-attached, which cuts the cross-referencing step from hours to minutes per record. They cost money. They also compress the timeline, which is the only thing that actually matters here.

Jurisdiction, Timing, and the Edge That Isn't About Data

The jurisdictional layer matters before you pick your target markets. Judicial foreclosure states — New York, New Jersey, Florida, Illinois — have longer timelines between default and auction, sometimes eighteen months or more. That gives you a larger pre-foreclosure window but also means more competition has time to find the same filing. Non-judicial states — Texas, California, Georgia, Arizona — move faster, sometimes ninety days from notice of default to trustee's sale. Shorter window, but fewer investors who have caught the filing in time. Neither is inherently better. What matters is knowing the specific timeline in your county so you can calibrate urgency: a filing in Texas requires a faster response than the same filing in New Jersey.

Every investor in your market can theoretically pull the same foreclosure filings you can. The county recorder is public. The data is not hidden. What is not equal is what you do with it in the first forty-eight hours after a filing lands. Most people who pull this data do nothing — they are waiting to see if the property hits auction, because that is the process they know. The investor who treats a new lis pendens as an immediate sourcing lead, traces ownership that week, and makes contact before the owner has fielded a single credible inquiry is not working better data. They are working the same data faster. That timing differential is the entire edge.

You do not need special access, special software, or a network of courthouse insiders to source pre-foreclosure multifamily deals. You need a disciplined weekly process: pull filings, cross-reference property type, trace ownership, make contact. You need to be faster and more specific than the other investors who theoretically have access to the same public records. The window between first default and auction is real. The motivated sellers in that window are real. The deals that close there — with flexible terms and below-market pricing — are real. What is not real is the idea that you will find them by refreshing a public listing site and hoping the market opens a door. That door is not on the MLS.

The math driving this cycle is simple and it is not going away overnight: buildings that cash-flowed at four percent debt service are bleeding at seven, and owners who cannot solve that problem are sitting on it quietly until they cannot anymore. The pre-foreclosure window is open right now, and it is wider in the small and mid-size apartment space than it has been in years. Work the public records, move fast, and show up as a solution — not a circling buyer. The investors who build that habit this year will be closing deals the rest of the market never knew existed.
