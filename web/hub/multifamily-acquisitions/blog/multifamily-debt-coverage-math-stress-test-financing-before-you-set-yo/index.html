Multifamily Debt Coverage Math: Stress-Test Financing Before You Set Your Offer Price

2026-10-26

Most buyers run DSCR after the offer price is set. That's the wrong order — and the gap between the benchmark rate you underwrote to and the terms a lender actually quotes can quietly flip a deal from viable to underwater. Here's how to build a financing stress test that belongs before you write the number down.

The news in brief

Multifamily buyers routinely underwrite to published benchmark rates, then receive actual term sheets that are 20 to 50 basis points higher — with tighter DSCR covenants, reserve requirements, and amortization schedules that don't match the pro forma. The gap between the prototype deal a rate sheet assumes and the specific building a buyer is actually financing is where underwriting falls apart. Running DSCR against a benchmark is underwriting a deal that doesn't exist.

What happened

According to the pillar analysis, institutional lenders publish benchmark rate sheets based on a prototype deal: stabilized asset, full occupancy, institutional sponsorship, optimal market. Individual buyers then underwrite to those sheets. When the actual term sheet arrives, it prices to the real deal — a specific building in a specific submarket with a specific vacancy profile and a specific sponsor. The delta between prototype and actual is where the model breaks. The pillar reports that conventional multifamily lenders typically require a DSCR minimum of 1.20 to 1.30 at stabilization, and that agency programs like Fannie Mae DUS products have DSCR floors baked in that don't flex for individual deals. It also reports that lenders reconstruct NOI independently — haircutting vacancy to a stabilized rate (typically 5–7%) even when the trailing occupancy is higher, adding a management fee of 4–6% of effective gross income when an owner self-manages, and adding per-unit replacement reserves of $300–$500 annually regardless of whether the seller ever funded them. On a 40-unit building, those adjustments alone can pull $30,000–$50,000 off the NOI a buyer was working from.

Why it matters

For multifamily acquisition teams, this isn't a refinement — it's a sequencing problem with direct consequences for offer price. If DSCR is run after the price is set, and the lender's reconstructed NOI produces a lower coverage ratio than modeled, the buyer faces one of three outcomes: bring more equity to close the gap, renegotiate the price downward from inside a due diligence period, or close on terms that don't pencil. None of those is a good position. The practical implication is that the stress test has to happen before the offer goes in. That means running at least three rate scenarios — a base case grounded in actual lender conversations, a stress case 40–60 basis points above base, and a floor case that works backward from the lender's DSCR minimum to the maximum supportable loan — and running each scenario against the lender's version of NOI, not the seller's. The inputs that move the most: vacancy assumptions, management expense, capital reserves, and whether rents are in-place actuals or projected post-renovation rents. Lenders underwrite to what the property earns today. Value-add upside doesn't count toward acquisition financing.

Our perspective

The pillar frames this as an arithmetic problem, not a judgment call — and that framing is correct. Working backward from DSCR to offer price is a defined sequence: take the lender's reconstructed NOI, divide by the required coverage ratio to get maximum supportable annual debt service, use the debt constant at your stress-case rate to derive the maximum loan amount, apply the LTV cap as a secondary check, and add your equity. The lower of the DSCR-derived ceiling and the LTV ceiling is your maximum offer price. If the seller is asking above that number, the gap is quantified — and it has to be closed by seller-side concessions, a credible NOI improvement plan that moves within 12 months, or additional equity. The pre-offer stress test converts that gap from a surprise into a negotiating position. This interpretation — that the scenario grid built before the offer becomes the documented basis for renegotiating price if actual terms come back harder than modeled — is consistent with standard acquisition underwriting discipline. It is interpretation, not reporting, and is offered as such.

What happens next

The open question every buyer faces at term sheet: do actual lender terms fall inside or outside the stress case you modeled? If inside — the rate is at or below your stress-case rate and DSCR still clears — the deal holds and you move. If outside, the pre-offer model gives you a factual, documented basis to go back to the seller before you're locked inside a due diligence period you can't exit cleanly. Two additional variables worth watching on bridge and value-add deals specifically: whether the lender grants an interest-only period (one to two years of IO changes early DSCR materially and should be run as a separate scenario), and the prepayment structure — defeasance and step-down penalties don't affect entry DSCR but they reshape exit math in ways that should be visible before you sign. The amortization period is also underweighted in most buyer models: the difference between a 25-year and a 30-year amortization schedule moves the debt constant more than most buyers expect and can be the difference between a deal that clears coverage and one that doesn't.
