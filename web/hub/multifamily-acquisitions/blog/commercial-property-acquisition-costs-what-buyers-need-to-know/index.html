Commercial Property Acquisition Costs: What Buyers Need to Know

2026-10-23

You have the capital, you have a buy box, and you're ready to move on a multifamily deal — but acquisition costs have a way of showing up late in the process and throwing off your return model. Due diligence fees, lender charges, title, legal, and environmental work all sit between your accepted offer and a closed transaction, and buyers who haven't budgeted for them precisely end up either renegotiating at the worst moment or closing into a deal that no longer pencils. Getting the cost stack right before you offer is not optional. It's the difference between a thesis that holds and one that quietly falls apart at the closing table.

The short answer

Acquiring a commercial or multifamily property typically costs between 2% and 5% of the purchase price in transaction-side expenses, separate from your down payment or equity contribution. The exact figure depends on deal size, financing structure, market, and how much due diligence the asset requires. The major cost categories are due diligence (inspection, environmental, appraisal), financing (origination, processing, rate buydown), legal (attorney, title, escrow), and closing (transfer taxes, recording, title insurance). None of these are negotiable in the sense of optional — they are real costs that should be modeled before you make an offer, not discovered after.

What does due diligence actually cost on a multifamily deal?

Due diligence is the category that varies most by asset condition and deal complexity. For a stabilized apartment building with clean financials and a recent inspection, the floor is relatively low. For a value-add or distressed multifamily asset, costs rise quickly because the inspection scope expands and the risk of uncovering problems is real.

The main components:

Physical inspection. A commercial property condition assessment (PCA) or building inspection runs roughly $2,000–$8,000 for a smaller multifamily asset and scales up from there for larger buildings or phased inspections. If the inspection surfaces concerns — roof, mechanical systems, foundation — follow-on specialist reports add cost and time.

Environmental assessment. A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment is standard on most commercial transactions and typically costs $1,500–$3,500. If the Phase I identifies recognized environmental conditions that need further investigation, a Phase II assessment can run $5,000–$30,000 or more depending on the scope of sampling required. Lenders require Phase I as a matter of course; some require Phase II before closing in certain markets or asset conditions.

Appraisal. Commercial appraisals are ordered by and paid to the lender's approved appraiser. Costs range from $2,500 for a smaller multifamily property to $10,000+ for a larger or more complex asset. The buyer pays this at application or shortly after, regardless of whether the deal closes.

Rent roll and financial audit. If you are working with a sourcing or analysis partner who provides packaged underwriting, part of this work is already done before you open the file. If you are doing it in-house, budget for the time and any third-party accounting review, particularly on deals where the seller's books are informal.

What do lenders and financing costs add to the stack?

Financing costs sit on top of due diligence and can be significant depending on the loan type, lender, and rate environment.

Origination fees. Commercial lenders typically charge origination fees of 0.5%–2% of the loan amount. A $2M loan at 1% origination is $20,000 out of pocket at close.

Loan processing and underwriting fees. Separate from origination, lenders charge processing fees ranging from $1,500 to $5,000 depending on loan complexity. These are generally non-refundable once the application is submitted.

Rate buydown points. In higher rate environments, buyers sometimes pay discount points to buy the rate down to a level where the deal cash-flows at the acquisition price. Each point costs 1% of the loan amount and reduces the rate by roughly 0.25%, though the exact terms vary by lender.

Lender legal and third-party report fees. Some lenders pass through their own legal review costs or charge for third-party reports beyond the appraisal. Read the term sheet carefully before you accept it — these can add $2,000–$5,000 in unexpected line items.

If seller financing is available — and on below-market multifamily deals with motivated sellers, it sometimes is — financing costs can be dramatically reduced. The tradeoff is that seller-financed deals typically carry shorter terms, balloon structures, or higher rates than conventional debt. Model both scenarios.

What do title, legal, and closing costs cover?

Title insurance. Commercial title insurance is split into lender's and owner's policies. The lender's policy is required when there's debt on the transaction. The owner's policy protects the buyer and is strongly advisable even when not required. Combined premiums vary by state and purchase price but typically run 0.5%–1% of the transaction value.

Attorney fees. Commercial real estate transactions require legal review of purchase and sale agreements, loan documents, and closing instruments. Buyer's counsel typically costs $3,000–$10,000 depending on transaction complexity and market. Do not skip this or use residential counsel for a commercial transaction.

Escrow and closing fees. Escrow companies or closing attorneys (depending on jurisdiction) charge closing fees of $1,500–$5,000. Recording fees for deeds and mortgage instruments are typically a few hundred dollars.

Transfer taxes. Many states and municipalities impose a real estate transfer tax on commercial transactions. These vary widely — some states charge nothing, others charge 1%–2% of the purchase price or more. This is a consequential number on a $2M+ transaction; check the rate for your specific market before you model closing costs.

How to apply it

Build a pre-offer cost model before you submit anything. The structure is straightforward:

1. Estimate your purchase price range based on your underwriting. 2. Apply 2%–5% of purchase price as your acquisition cost range, then itemize into the four categories above — due diligence, financing, legal, and closing — using the ranges in this article as floor estimates. 3. Check your specific state's transfer tax rate. This one number can shift your model more than any other single line item. 4. Add a contingency of 0.5%–1% of purchase price for items that surface during due diligence. Distressed and value-add multifamily assets regularly produce unexpected inspection findings; budget for them in advance. 5. Layer your total acquisition cost against your equity contribution to confirm you have enough capital for the down payment, the cost stack, and an operating reserve before you offer.

A practical example: a $1.5M stabilized multifamily acquisition with conventional financing might carry $15,000–$20,000 in due diligence (inspection, Phase I, appraisal), $22,500–$30,000 in financing costs at 1.5% origination plus processing, $6,000–$10,000 in legal and title, and $5,000–$15,000 in transfer taxes and recording — totaling roughly $50,000–$75,000 in acquisition costs on top of your down payment. On a value-add deal requiring a Phase II and expanded inspection scope, add another $15,000–$40,000.

Frequently asked questions

Are acquisition costs negotiable with the seller?

Certain costs can be allocated in the purchase and sale agreement — transfer taxes, for example, are sometimes split by custom or negotiation. Due diligence, lender fees, and your own legal costs are buyer-side and not typically shifted to the seller, though a seller credit at closing can offset them if the negotiation allows for it.

When do I have to pay due diligence costs — before or after close?

Most due diligence costs are paid during the due diligence period, which runs after an accepted offer and before closing. Inspection fees, environmental assessments, and appraisals are paid as those services are delivered — not at closing. They are generally non-refundable if the deal falls through, which is why a strong initial screen before entering due diligence matters.

Do acquisition costs change if I'm buying off-market versus a listed property?

The cost categories are largely the same. The difference is that off-market deals sometimes move faster, which can compress your due diligence timeline and budget. A packaged acquisition that arrives with pre-done underwriting and comp analysis reduces the analytical work on your side — though it does not replace physical inspection, environmental assessment, or legal review, which are always buyer-conducted.

Can I roll acquisition costs into the loan?

Some lenders allow origination fees and prepaid costs to be financed into the loan amount rather than paid out of pocket at close, but this is deal- and lender-specific. Most hard due diligence costs — inspection, environmental, appraisal — cannot be financed and must be paid directly. Ask your lender at the term sheet stage what is eligible to be financed versus paid at close.

What's the biggest line item buyers underestimate?

Transfer taxes, consistently. Buyers focus on inspection and legal costs — which are visible and familiar — and underestimate or miss the transfer tax entirely until it appears on the closing disclosure. On a $2M transaction in a high-transfer-tax state, this can be $20,000–$40,000 that was never in the model. Check the rate for your specific county and municipality, not just the state average.
