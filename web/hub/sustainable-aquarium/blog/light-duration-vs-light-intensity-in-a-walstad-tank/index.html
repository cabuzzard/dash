Light Duration vs. Light Intensity in a Walstad Tank

2026-10-15

Most planted-tank lighting advice treats duration and intensity as two dials that do the same job. Turn them up when plants look pale; turn them down when algae appears. That logic is wrong in any planted tank. In a Walstad tank it is actively dangerous — because the soil substrate underneath your plants is releasing nutrients continuously, whether your plants are photosynthesising or not. That is the mechanic most lighting guides miss, and it is the reason a Walstad keeper can follow every standard low-tech recommendation and still end up with algae that will not quit.

The Soil Does Not Pause

Your dirted substrate is an active biological system. Decomposing organic matter in the soil layer produces ammonia, phosphorus, and carbon compounds on its own schedule — not on yours. When the light is on, healthy, well-fed plants pull those compounds out of the water column before algae gets access. When the light is off, photosynthesis stops, plant uptake slows dramatically, and any nutrients that have leached up through the cap sit in open water, available to whatever wants them. Algae is not particularly demanding. It will take what it can get.

This creates a timing problem that has nothing to do with how bright your light is.

Duration — the number of hours your light runs each day — determines how long your plants are in active uptake mode. Intensity — the amount of light energy delivered per hour — determines how fast photosynthesis runs during that window. These are not interchangeable. A dim light running twelve hours gives your plants a long, slow feeding window and keeps algae suppressed across the full day. A bright light running six hours runs photosynthesis hard and then leaves the tank dark for eighteen hours while the substrate keeps releasing nutrients into unguarded water. The second setup is more likely to produce algae even if the total light energy delivered is comparable.

Intensity is about growth rate. Duration is about coverage. In a Walstad tank, coverage is the more important variable.

The guidance that ships with most aquarium lights was built around inert substrates. A tank on gravel or aqua soil does not carry a background nutrient load the way a Walstad tank does. When those lights go off, there is not much in the water column for algae to eat, so duration is less critical. In a high-tech tank, you can run a shorter photoperiod because CO2 injection and liquid dosing give you precise control over when nutrients enter the system — switch off the CO2, switch off the light, and you have effectively paused nutrient availability. A Walstad substrate does not pause. The biology underneath the cap runs continuously, and your lighting schedule needs to account for that.

Why Cutting the Photoperiod Makes It Worse

When algae shows up in a Walstad tank, most keepers do what every forum thread tells them to do — they cut the photoperiod. Six hours. Then five. The algae does not clear. It might get worse. Here is why: if your substrate is in an active release phase, cutting light duration does not reduce the nutrient load in the water column. It reduces the time your plants spend consuming it. You are not turning down the fuel supply. You are turning down the thing that burns the fuel. Plants lose ground. Algae, which needs almost nothing to keep going, does not.

The fix is usually the opposite of the instinct: enough duration for plants to stay ahead, with intensity dialled back so you are not driving accelerated growth that the substrate cannot support.

Eight hours is often cited as a safe ceiling for low-tech tanks. In a Walstad setup — especially in the first sixty days, when the substrate is releasing nutrients at its highest rate — eight hours of duration at modest intensity tends to outperform six hours at high intensity for algae suppression. The plants stay in uptake mode longer, the gaps between light-on and light-off are smaller, and the window available to algae is compressed.

Intensity matters, but its failure mode runs in the other direction. Too much light intensity drives photosynthesis faster than your plants can use available nutrients, which means excess light energy has nowhere productive to go. In plant physiology, excess light that cannot be coupled to carbon fixation generates oxidative stress — plants slow down or show bleaching at the tips. In the tank, the practical result is that very high intensity can actually impair uptake efficiency in low-nutrient conditions, the opposite of what you want from a substrate that is already releasing nutrients at its own pace. This is one of the reasons high-intensity planted-tank lights marketed for CO2-injected setups consistently underperform in Walstad tanks even when the lighting schedule looks reasonable on paper. They were not designed for a substrate that manages its own nutrient supply.

The usable intensity range for a Walstad tank is moderate by planted-tank standards. You want enough light to drive healthy growth in medium-demand plants without pushing into the range where intensity outpaces the substrate's ability to feed those plants continuously. The PAR values being chased in competitive aquascaping are not the target. Steady, consistent photosynthesis across a long-enough daily window is the target.

The Sixty-Day Window and the Long Game

There is one variable that compounds everything above: the establishment window. In the first two months after a Walstad build, the substrate is releasing nutrients at its peak rate. The soil biology is fully active, the cap has not fully settled, and ammonia readings will cycle in ways that look alarming if you do not know what you are watching. This is exactly the period when lighting discipline matters most, because your plants are not yet at full establishment density and their capacity to absorb the substrate's output is at its lowest.

Running a long-duration, moderate-intensity schedule during this window — rather than a short, bright one — gives developing root systems and establishing surface plants the continuous low-level light energy they need to grow into their ecological role. Spiking them with intensity they cannot use during this phase does not accelerate establishment. It creates the conditions for algae to exploit the gaps that new plants have not yet closed.

Most aquarium lights are engineered for one of two setups: a low-light fish tank with plastic plants, or a high-tech planted tank running CO2 injection and liquid fertilisers. Walstad tanks are neither. The substrate manages the nutrient supply. The plants do the filtration. The light is the one input you actually control — and if you are using a fixture designed for a system that works completely differently, you are tuning the wrong instrument. A high-intensity light designed for CO2-injected aquascaping will not improve your Walstad tank. It will drive demand your substrate cannot always meet, create the conditions for algae to exploit the gaps, and leave you troubleshooting a lighting problem that looks exactly like a plant problem.

The goal in a self-sustaining tank is not maximum growth. It is balanced growth — a plant mass large enough and active enough to consume what the soil produces, continuously, over the life of the tank. Lighting that supports that balance looks different from lighting designed to maximise growth rate. Getting that distinction right is what separates a Walstad tank that stabilises from one that spends its first year fighting algae that nobody can seem to explain.

Duration and intensity are not the same dial. In a Walstad tank they are not even close substitutes, because the substrate underneath your plants is always running whether the light is on or not. Treat duration as your primary control — it determines how long your plants stay ahead of the nutrient load. Keep intensity moderate enough that photosynthesis runs steadily rather than hard and short. Get those two things right during the first sixty days and the tank does what it was designed to do: it balances itself.
