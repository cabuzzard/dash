Know If Your Living Water System Is Buildable Before You Break Ground

2026-10-22

You can picture the system. A constructed wetland on the back of the property. A living filtration installation integrated with building drainage. An ecological water landscape that runs on biology rather than machinery. The concept is solid — you understand biological cycles, substrate chemistry, and plant selection well enough to know this is not a decorative pond. What you do not have is a clear line from that concept to a permit, a contractor brief, and a build that performs as designed.

That gap is where projects go wrong. Not because the biology was bad. Because the biology was never mapped against the site conditions, the compliance requirements, and the engineering constraints that determine whether a concept is actually buildable on a specific parcel.

This engagement closes that gap — before you spend construction money finding out where the friction was.

The Part Contractors Don't Scope

Every contractor you talk to wants to sell you a build. That is their job. They scope for materials, labour, and programme. They do not scope for biological function, water-quality compliance, or the site conditions that determine whether a living filtration system performs at its design load or quietly fails for reasons that were discoverable before the first shovel moved.

You may also have tried to research the permit question yourself — and found a fragmented, jurisdiction-specific maze with no single answer. A constructed wetland treating greywater may fall under building permits, environmental protection authority approvals, local drainage consents, or a combination. The relevant authority changes depending on whether your system discharges to ground, to a waterway, or recycles on-site. There is no single permit. There is a map of overlapping jurisdictions specific to your site and your system type. Most people discover that map mid-construction. That is consistently more expensive than scoping it first.

What It Looks Like When the Feasibility Is Done Right

You know whether your concept is buildable on your specific site before you commit construction money. The regulatory layer is no longer a black box — you have a compliance map for your jurisdiction, your system type, and your discharge pathway. Your biological design assumptions have been stress-tested against site-scale realities, not aquarium-scale intuition. You have a written concept document that an engineer or contractor can actually work from. And you stop losing time to contractor conversations that skip the feasibility layer entirely and price straight to build.

The specific relief is not optimism. It is accurate coordinates. You know what your site can support, what compliance requires, what the build will actually demand, and where the real friction points are. The system you imagined is either confirmed as buildable or corrected before it costs you anything structural.

What the Engagement Delivers

The work is a bounded consulting engagement — not a course, not a template, not a generic report.

Site and concept intake. Your system concept, site conditions, and intended function reviewed against what site-scale biological design actually requires. Soil permeability, groundwater depth, available footprint, topography, and existing infrastructure connections assessed against your design concept.

Compliance scoping. The permit categories, regulatory authorities, and approval pathways that apply to your system type and jurisdiction, mapped before a shovel moves. Land use, water quality, drainage, and — where food production is involved — the additional licensing dimensions that most feasibility processes miss.

Biological design review. Your substrate assumptions, hydraulic loading, plant selection, and nutrient-cycling logic stress-tested against site-scale failure modes. Substrate depth and anaerobic zone management at scale, hydraulic loading variation across seasons, temperature effects on microbial communities, wildlife and vector considerations — the places where aquarium-scale logic translates directly and the places where it breaks down.

Written feasibility and concept document. The deliverable. A decision-ready document you can hand to an engineer or contractor — an honest picture of what your system requires, what it will cost to develop properly, and where the real friction points are. Not a sales pitch for the next stage. Not a vague brief. A scoped work product.

Why This Comes From Biological Practice, Not Consulting Boilerplate

The same practitioner logic that makes a Walstad dirted tank self-sustaining applies at landscape scale. Substrate function, microbial community succession, plant competition — the three mechanisms that underpin a self-sustaining planted tank are the same three mechanisms that underpin a working constructed wetland. The biology does not change at scale. What changes is the engineering that contains and directs it, and the regulatory framework that governs its outputs.

Knowing where the translation holds and where it fails is the specific expertise this phase brings. A soil that goes anaerobic in one corner of a tank is a maintenance task. A constructed wetland cell that goes anaerobic across its full footprint is a redesign problem that may require regulatory notification and a revised engineering plan. Getting the substrate right before the water goes in, loading the system within its biological capacity, not rushing the maturation phase — that practitioner logic does not change at site scale. It becomes more consequential.

Almost no one in this space works from authentic low-tech biological practice and applies it at site scale with honest pre-construction scoping. Contractors sell builds. Consultants sell hours without deliverables. Academic ecologists do not talk to hobbyists. This comes from a practising Walstad-method aquarist who treats the feasibility phase as the real work — not a formality before the invoice.

The Objections Worth Answering Plainly

"I can figure the permits out myself after I start building." Some jurisdictions require approvals before any ground disturbance. Starting without them can require full reinstatement — not a fine, a full reversal. Scoping the compliance map before you commit anything structural is cheaper in every scenario where a permit problem exists. The feasibility phase exists precisely to build that map first.

"This is just a paid conversation." The output is a written document you can hand to an engineer or contractor. It is a scoped work product with a defined deliverable — biological design assumptions, compliance map, engineering constraints, and an honest assessment of what your concept requires to become a real build.

"My contractor will handle the design." Most contractors scope for buildability. If your system depends on living filtration and ecological balance, those need to be designed before the contractor gets involved — not delegated to them. The feasibility phase is the layer that precedes the build conversation, not a substitute for it.

"It's too early-stage for formal feasibility." If you are committing construction money, you are past the stage where informal planning is sufficient. Early-stage concepts cost less to scope now than they cost to redesign mid-build.
