Biogenerative Installation Feasibility & Design

2026-10-22

You can picture the system. A constructed wetland, a living filtration installation, an ecological water landscape that runs on biology rather than machinery. The concept is solid. What you do not have is a clear line from that concept to a permit, a contractor brief, and a build that performs as designed. This engagement closes that gap — before you spend construction money finding out where the friction was.

Who This Is For

Aquatic ecosystem builders who started with a planted tank and want something bigger. You understand biological cycles, substrate chemistry, and plant selection. You are self-directed and allergic to institutional gatekeeping. What you do not have is a map from concept to groundbreaking — the engineering constraints, the permit realities, the water-quality compliance layer that separates a working idea from a buildable one. If you are committing construction money to a living water system and have not yet scoped feasibility, this is the first step you need.

What's Included

- Site and concept intake — your system concept, site conditions, and intended function reviewed against what site-scale biological design actually requires. - Water-quality and land-use compliance scoping — the permit categories, regulatory authorities, and approval pathways that apply to your system type and jurisdiction, mapped before a shovel moves. - Biological design review — your substrate assumptions, hydraulic loading, plant selection, and nutrient-cycling logic stress-tested against site-scale realities rather than aquarium-scale intuition. - Engineering constraint identification — soil permeability, groundwater depth, available footprint, topography, and infrastructure integration points assessed against your design concept. - Written feasibility and concept document — a decision-ready output you can hand to an engineer or contractor; not a vague brief, not a sales pitch for the next stage, but an honest picture of what your system requires, what it will cost to develop properly, and where the real friction points are.

The engagement is a bounded consulting process: intake, scoping, review, and written output. It begins with a structured intake covering your site, your concept, your intended water-quality or hydrological function, and your jurisdiction. From there, compliance categories are mapped, biological assumptions are reviewed against site-scale failure modes, and engineering constraints are identified. The written feasibility and concept document is the deliverable — scope, timeline, and fee are defined at intake based on system complexity and site jurisdiction. No construction is sold at this stage.

Why It Works

The same practitioner logic that makes a Walstad dirted tank self-sustaining applies at landscape scale — but the translation breaks down in specific places, and knowing where it breaks down is the entire value of the feasibility phase. Substrate depth and anaerobic zone management, hydraulic loading variation across seasons, temperature effects on microbial communities, wildlife and vector considerations: these are invisible at aquarium scale and expensive to discover mid-build. The biology is not a black box. It has known mechanics, known failure modes, and known conditions under which it becomes self-sustaining rather than self-destructing. Getting those right before construction starts is not excessive caution — it is the only path to a system that performs as designed.

Almost no one in this space works from authentic low-tech biological practice and applies it at site scale with honest pre-construction scoping. Contractors scope for buildability. Consultants sell hours without deliverables. Academic ecologists do not talk to hobbyists. This comes from a practising Walstad-method aquarist who treats the feasibility phase as the real work — not a formality before the invoice.

The Objection Worth Answering

"I can figure the permits out myself after I start building."

Most permit problems surface mid-construction, not before. Some jurisdictions require approvals before any ground disturbance — and starting without them can require full reinstatement. A constructed wetland treating greywater may fall under building permits, environmental protection authority approvals, local drainage consents, or a combination, and the relevant authority changes depending on whether your system discharges to ground, to a waterway, or recycles on-site. There is no single permit; there is a map of overlapping jurisdictions specific to your site and system type. Getting that map wrong mid-build is consistently more expensive than scoping it first. The feasibility phase exists precisely to build that map before you commit anything structural.
