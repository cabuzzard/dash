Walstad Tank Livestock: Fish Load, Waste, and Soil Biology

2026-10-19

The livestock you put into a Walstad tank are not passengers. They are active variables in a closed biological system — and wrong stocking choices do not just add stress, they quietly dismantle the soil biology the whole system depends on. Most Walstad guidance spends its energy on substrate depth, soil choice, and planting density. Fish selection gets a paragraph, maybe two. Here is the mechanics-first resource that should exist.

Livestock Are an Ecosystem Variable, Not an Afterthought

The Walstad method works because it closes the nitrogen loop. Plants pull ammonia and nitrate directly from the substrate and water column. Soil bacteria convert organic waste into plant-available nutrients. Fish produce the waste that feeds that bacterial and plant community. In a healthy Walstad tank those cycles reinforce each other — waste goes in, biology processes it, plants grow, water stays clean without intervention.

That loop is load-sensitive. Every additional fish body increases ammonia output, increases organic matter settling into the substrate, and increases the oxygen demand on the bacterial community doing the processing. The margin for error is real but finite. Push past it and ammonia accumulates faster than the system can process it. The results look like a nutrient problem or a light problem, and most troubleshooting goes in those directions — when the actual variable that changed was the fish count three weeks ago.

Fish behavior matters as much as fish load, and this is the thing most guides do not address at all. A species that digs into the substrate cap disrupts the anaerobic zone that drives denitrification. A species that grazes biofilm off plant leaves is performing a real maintenance function. A heavy-bodied, high-metabolism fish in a 20-gallon Walstad tank is a different ecological event than the same gallon count of small rasboras. Load is not just numbers — it is mass, metabolism, waste chemistry, and physical behavior inside a closed system.

The standard hobby advice — one inch of fish per gallon — is useless in a Walstad context. It was never designed to account for bioload distribution, waste type, or biological filtration capacity. In a natural tank with no mechanical filter and no weekly water change, what matters is the ratio of organic input to the system's biological processing capacity. That capacity comes from three sources: the substrate bacterial community, the root zone activity of established plants, and surface-area nitrification happening on hardscape and plant stems. It builds slowly. In the first eight to twelve weeks of a new Walstad setup it is significantly lower than it will be at month six. Stocking early — or stocking heavily before the bacterial community has density — is one of the most common failure modes in a method that looks like it should be ready to stock as soon as the water is clear.

A practical working principle: a conservative upper bound in a moderately planted, established Walstad tank is roughly one gram of fish body mass per liter of water. That is not a hard ceiling — a heavily planted 40-gallon with two-year-old soil running strong rooted plants will handle more. But it is a better reference point than inches-per-gallon because it tracks actual metabolic output. A single Oscar at six inches produces orders of magnitude more waste than six one-inch neon tetras. The inch-per-gallon framing erases that difference entirely.

Which Behaviors Reinforce Walstad Soil Biology — and Which Ones Wreck It

Mid-column schooling fish are close to the ideal Walstad livestock profile. Small tetras, rasboras, ember tetras, celestial pearl danios — their waste is light, their behavior does not disturb the substrate, and their metabolic rate stays low if the tank is not overstocked. In a mature natural tank they graze incidentally on infusoria and microorganisms the ecosystem is already producing. That is a closed loop: the tank grows food, the fish eat it, the waste cycles back through soil biology. That is the Walstad method working exactly the way it is supposed to.

Biofilm grazers are not just aesthetically useful — they are actively performing maintenance. Otocinclus, nerite snails, and small bristlenose plecos kept in appropriate numbers consume organic surface buildup that would otherwise settle into the substrate and add to the bacterial processing load. Surface feeders like hatchetfish and smaller killifish species add visual dimension without disturbing the substrate zone; their waste drops through the water column and distributes evenly rather than concentrating at the substrate surface, where the most sensitive biology is happening.

Corydoras deserve a nuanced treatment. They are popular Walstad choices, and their bottom-dwelling nature seems like a natural fit. In practice they are fine in a capped substrate where the organic soil layer is under an inch or more of inert material. What you do not want is an active group of corydoras on a thin or poorly consolidated cap — they will breach it. When they do, they expose anaerobic substrate to oxygen, disrupt the denitrification zone, and can trigger localized off-gassing. The issue is not the species. The issue is the species in a substrate that is not physically ready for them.

Substrate disturbance is the most direct threat a fish can pose to Walstad soil biology. The anaerobic layer in the lower substrate is where denitrification actually happens — beneficial anaerobic bacteria convert nitrate to nitrogen gas down there, which is one of the core mechanisms that keeps a natural tank running without water changes. Physically disturb that layer and you aerate it. Aerate it and you kill the bacterial community doing that work. Nitrate accumulates. Algae spikes. From the surface it looks like a lighting problem or a nutrient imbalance. It is often neither. It is a fish with digging behavior that has been working the substrate cap for six weeks while you were troubleshooting everything else.

Goldfish are the clearest example: active substrate disturbers, enormous waste loads for their size, metabolic output that overwhelms the processing capacity of most natural planted tanks within weeks. They are not a Walstad fish. The same logic applies to most large cichlids with strong digging instincts — eartheaters, Oscars, many of the larger new world cichlids. Their natural behavior is ecologically appropriate in a river system with unlimited dilution. In a closed 30-gallon soil ecosystem they are running the biology in reverse.

Shrimp are underrated Walstad allies. Amano shrimp, cherry shrimp, and most neocaridina varieties graze constantly on organic detritus, algae, and biofilm. Their waste is fine-particulate and distributes evenly. They do not disturb substrate. In an established Walstad tank they perform continuous light mechanical breakdown of organic matter that would otherwise accumulate and create surface anaerobic hot spots — the wrong kind of anaerobic. Surface anaerobic pockets, forming under settled waste or dead plant material, produce hydrogen sulfide, smell, and are bad for roots. Shrimp and snails prevent that accumulation. They are doing maintenance that in a conventional tank you would handle with a gravel vacuum. Nerite snails in particular graze glass and hardscape aggressively, do not breed in freshwater, and have waste that mineralizes quickly. For a tank trying to stay low-intervention, they are close to a complete package.

Stocking Sequence Matters as Much as Stocking Choice

Even the right livestock can destabilize a Walstad tank if they are introduced in the wrong order or too fast. The sequence question is one that forums almost never address directly, because it requires understanding how biological capacity builds over time — and that varies with temperature, soil chemistry, planting density, and light. The tank that looks ready to stock at week three is not the same biological system it will be at month six.

A working sequence for a new Walstad build: establish plants first, wait for visible growth and early biofilm development, then introduce a small number of invertebrates — shrimp, snails — as the first biological load. Their waste is light enough that the developing bacterial community can absorb it without ammonia spikes. They are also doing useful work in those early weeks, grazing the surface organic buildup that accumulates before the plant root zone is fully established. Invertebrates first is not a compromise position. It is using the system's biology intelligently.

After four to six weeks with invertebrates present and the tank showing stability, introduce the first fish — and introduce them in small increments, not all at once. Each addition is a step-change in organic input. The bacterial community needs time to upregulate after each one. Dumping a full stocking plan into a tank in one session is asking the biology to handle a load it has never encountered before, at exactly the moment when it has the least capacity to respond.

Watch what the tank tells you between additions. A well-sequenced Walstad build shows consistent water clarity, no persistent surface scum, healthy plant growth, and no ammonia detectable in testing. If any of those signals shift after a stocking addition, the system is telling you the input has exceeded current processing capacity. The response is not a water change — it is waiting. Water changes in a Walstad tank flush the dissolved nutrients the plants are pulling from. The biology needs time to catch up, not dilution.

The practical upshot is that stocking a Walstad tank is a months-long process, not a weekend event. The temptation to rush it is real, especially after spending time and money on a careful soil-and-plant setup. But the livestock decision is where most Walstad tanks actually fail — not in the substrate layer, not in the light schedule, not in the plant selection. The fish go in too fast, or in the wrong combination, and the soil ecosystem that took months to build gets quietly dismantled before the aquarist knows what variable changed.

A Walstad tank is a biological argument: given the right conditions, the system sustains itself. Livestock are not an add-on to that argument — they are part of the mechanism. Choose species whose waste output, metabolic load, and physical behavior work with the soil ecosystem rather than against it. Sequence the introduction carefully. Pay attention to what the tank tells you between additions. Get those decisions right and the system does exactly what the method promises. Get them wrong and you spend months troubleshooting the wrong variables while the biology unravels underneath.
