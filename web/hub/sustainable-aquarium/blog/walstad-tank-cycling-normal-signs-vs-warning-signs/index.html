Walstad Tank Cycling: Normal Signs vs. Warning Signs

2026-10-15

The sixty-day cycling window in a Walstad tank is where most people lose confidence. You set it up correctly — soil capped, plants in, maybe a few fast-growing stems to outcompete early algae — and then the water goes cloudy, something coats the glass, a valued plant starts dropping leaves. The standard cycling guides do not help here, because they were written for bare-bottom or inert-substrate tanks. The Walstad method runs a different process, and reading it through the wrong lens is how people panic, intervene too early, and derail the very ecosystem they were trying to build.

What the Soil Is Actually Doing in the First Two Weeks

Organic matter in the dirt begins breaking down the moment you flood the tank. Bacteria and fungi that were dormant in the soil become active when submerged, and they start producing ammonia, carbon dioxide, and dissolved organics at a rate that exceeds what a young, partially rooted plant mass can process. This is not a failure. This is the system loading up before the biological machinery is in place to handle it.

The cloudiness you see in the first two weeks is almost always a bacterial bloom responding to that organic release. In a conventional tank with inert gravel, water that looks like that signals an ammonia spike, a dead animal, or an overfeed. In a Walstad tank, it means the substrate is doing exactly what it is supposed to do. The colony structure forming in that water column is part of the ecosystem that will eventually stabilise the tank. The mistake people make is reaching for water changes to clear it. A partial water change dilutes the organic load temporarily, but it also disrupts the developing bacterial population and resets the succession clock. Let it resolve on its own — it usually clears within ten to fourteen days.

The surface film that appears in weeks one and two is a related phenomenon. It is a biofilm — primarily bacterial — forming at the air-water interface, and it shows up because surface agitation is low in most naturally planted tanks by design. The Walstad method discourages heavy surface disturbance because CO2 outgassing is a real cost when your plants are depending on dissolved CO2 from the soil. So the biofilm builds. A thin, iridescent film that breaks when you touch it is normal. A thick, white, matted layer that reforms within hours is a different signal — it often means surface agitation is genuinely insufficient for gas exchange, and livestock will start showing oxygen stress before the plants show anything. The fix is not a water change; it is a minor adjustment to your filter outlet or the placement of a small circulation pump. You want surface movement without a waterfall.

Plant melt is the event that breaks most people's confidence. You planted healthy stems and rosettes, they looked fine for the first five to seven days, and then the older leaves started turning transparent, yellowing, or dissolving. In a conventional planted tank, this is a nutrient deficiency or an emersed-to-submerged transition signal. That mechanism exists in a Walstad tank too, but there is a second mechanism layered on top: the soil is releasing compounds that temporarily suppress root development in some species, and the plant is dropping its above-ground investment because it cannot support it from the roots yet. Fast-growing stem plants — hornwort, water sprite, hygrophila — typically hold through this phase because they can pull nutrients from the water column while the roots establish. Slow-growing rosettes like crypts will melt aggressively and look dead. They are usually not dead. The rhizome and root structure are intact, and new submerged-form growth will emerge from the base within three to five weeks — if you do not pull the plant. Pulling it and replanting resets that clock entirely. The melt looks like death. It is usually not.

Reading the Algae Pulse at Weeks Three to Five

Algae pulses during the cycling window are inevitable, and they follow a predictable succession pattern if the tank is establishing correctly. The first algae to appear is usually a green or brown film on the glass and hardscape — diatoms, primarily, feeding on silicates releasing from the substrate and any new sand or rock you added. Diatoms in the first two to three weeks are a reliable normal sign. They typically self-limit as silicates are consumed and as grazing populations of snails and shrimp establish.

What follows the diatom pulse — usually around week three to five — is where you can start reading whether your plant mass balance is right. If thread algae or green spot algae starts colonising plant leaves at that point, it almost always means the plant mass is not yet large enough relative to the nutrient load the substrate is producing. The plants are not winning the competition for available phosphorus and nitrogen. The soil is doing its job — breaking down organic matter and releasing ammonia and phosphorus compounds into the water column. The plants are supposed to intercept that load before algae can use it. At week three, if the algae is winning, the answer is more fast-growing plant mass, not a water change and not an algaecide.

Water changes dilute nutrients temporarily, but the plants do not benefit in proportion to the dilution, and you have not changed the underlying balance. Add hornwort, add water sprite, add hygrophila — something with a high uptake rate — and let the competition shift. The algae will retreat when the plants are taking the nutrients first. This is the correct intervention because it addresses the mechanism: insufficient plant mass relative to substrate output. Everything else is treating the symptom.

The algae pulse at weeks three to five is not evidence that something went wrong. It is evidence that the plant mass has not yet caught up to the nutrient load the soil is releasing. That gap closes as the root systems establish and the plant canopy fills in. Your job during this phase is to accelerate the plant side of the equation, not to suppress the algae directly. Suppressing the algae without addressing the nutrient competition just leaves the nutrients in the water column for the next algae species to exploit.

The Three Warning Signs That Actually Require Intervention

Everything described above — the cloudiness, the surface biofilm, the plant melt, the diatom pulse, the thread algae at week four — is succession. It looks alarming if you are running a conventional planted tank framework in your head. It is not alarming if you understand the mechanism. The warning signs that actually require intervention are different in character, and recognising the difference is the whole skill.

The first is fish dying from ammonia in week two. Ammonia high enough to cause fish loss is not a normal cycling event in a Walstad tank with adequate plant mass. If fish are dying early in the window, the plant mass was insufficient at setup. You need to address that immediately — either remove livestock temporarily or add significantly more plant material, fast. Waiting is not an option here because the ammonia is already at a lethal level and the plants cannot compensate fast enough without intervention on the stocking side.

The second is a persistent hydrogen sulfide smell from the substrate — the rotten egg odour. A healthy Walstad soil layer maintains a low-oxygen zone near the bottom that supports specific anaerobic processes the method depends on. But if anaerobic conditions extend too deep into the soil column, hydrogen sulfide production reaches a level that is toxic to roots and livestock. That is a substrate issue — often a soil depth problem or a compaction problem — and it will not resolve on its own. It requires diagnosing the physical structure of the substrate, not adding more plants or waiting out the window.

The third is water that stays black and does not clear within ten days. Tannin release from wood is common and usually harmless, and it often produces dark water in the early weeks. But water that stays intensely black and does not trend toward clearing may indicate that the wrong type of soil was used and is continuing to leach compounds the ecosystem cannot buffer. That is a soil compatibility problem, not a tannin problem, and the distinction matters for how you respond.

Everything else in those first sixty days is succession. The challenge is that succession and failure can look identical if you do not know the mechanism behind what you are seeing. Cloudy water. Algae. Plants dying back. Generic forum advice defaults to the conventional framework — water change schedules and product recommendations that make sense for inert-substrate tanks and actively undermine what a Walstad build is trying to do. Getting through the cycling window without wrecking the ecosystem you are building requires reading the specific signals of a soil-substrate system. That is a different skill set from standard planted tank troubleshooting, and it starts with knowing which signals belong to the process and which ones belong to a real problem.

The sixty-day window is not a test of patience — it is a test of whether you can read a living system correctly while it is still assembling itself. Most interventions that derail a Walstad tank happen because the hobbyist applied the right solution to the wrong diagnosis. Know the mechanism behind what you are seeing, and you will know whether to act or wait. In almost every case during those first eight weeks, the answer is wait — and add more plants.
