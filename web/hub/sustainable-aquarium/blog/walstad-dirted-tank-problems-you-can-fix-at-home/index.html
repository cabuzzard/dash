Walstad & Dirted Tank Problems You Can Fix at Home

2026-10-23

You followed the method — soil down, capped with gravel, planted densely, lights on a timer — and something still went sideways. The water turned the wrong colour, algae crept across every surface, or a fish that looked fine yesterday is now gasping at the surface. The frustration is specific: you did the research, you committed to the system, and it still does not look like the tank you built it to be. The good news is that nearly every common problem in a Walstad or dirted tank has a readable cause. This guide names each one and tells you what to do — and what not to do.

The short answer

Most Walstad and dirted tank problems trace back to four sources: the wrong soil, too much light, stocking that outpaces the biology, or intervention during establishment when the tank needed to be left alone. If you can identify which of those four levers is out of position, you can fix the problem at its root instead of chasing symptoms with chemicals and water changes that disrupt the very loop you are trying to build.

Why does my water look brown or green, and should I do anything?

Brown water is almost always tannins off-gassing from the soil during the first few weeks of establishment. It is cosmetically unpleasant and biologically harmless. The correct response is to wait. Activated carbon in a filter will clear it faster, but in a Walstad setup you are not running a heavy filter — and tannins clear on their own within one to three weeks as the soil settles and stabilises.

Green water is different. It means free-floating algae — phytoplankton — are blooming in the water column. The two most common drivers are excess light and elevated dissolved organics outpacing plant uptake. In an establishment-phase tank under three months old, a green water bloom is normal. The plant mass is not yet dense enough to compete. Add floating plants like *Salvinia* or frogbit to shade the surface, reduce the light period to six or seven hours temporarily, and resist the urge to do a large water change. A water change removes the bloom temporarily but also removes the dissolved organics that the plant roots are just beginning to process — you reset the clock without fixing the cause.

For tanks past the establishment phase, persistent green water almost always means the light period is too long or the fixture is too intense for the plant mass you have. Reduce the light period first, then reassess after two weeks.

My tank has algae on everything — what does that actually mean?

Algae in a planted tank is always a signal, not just a nuisance. Each type tells you something specific.

Green spot algae on the glass and hardscape means the light is outpacing CO2 and nutrients available to your plants — common in stable, mature tanks where plant growth has slowed. It is purely cosmetic unless it is spreading to leaves and smothering them. Nerite snails remove it from glass efficiently; it does not require intervention in the water column.

Hair algae during the first four to eight weeks is a normal establishment feature. The soil is releasing nutrients faster than the young root system can absorb them, and algae is filling the gap. The fix is patience and plant density — add fast-growing stem plants like *Hygrophila* or *Bacopa* if you have not already planted them at high density. Hair algae that persists beyond two months in a well-planted tank usually points to excess light or a bioload the plant mass cannot keep up with.

Black beard algae (BBA) is a sign of CO2 fluctuation — inconsistent surface agitation, a filter that runs intermittently, or a light period that creates sharp CO2 swings. In a low-tech setup without CO2 injection, BBA is most often controlled by reducing surface agitation to what is strictly necessary for dissolved oxygen, not more, and by ensuring the light period is consistent every day.

Diatoms — the brown dusty coating on every surface in the first few weeks — are a normal part of the silicate cycle in new tanks. They disappear on their own. Otocinclus and snails speed that up, but it is not a problem that needs solving.

What does it mean when the substrate smells like rotten eggs?

Hydrogen sulphide. It means anaerobic pockets have developed in the soil layer — areas where oxygen does not penetrate and sulphur-reducing bacteria are active. A small amount of anaerobic activity in a deep soil layer is not inherently dangerous, but when you disturb the substrate and get a distinct rotten-egg smell, the pockets are significant enough to act.

The most common cause is a soil layer deeper than 1.5 inches, especially in combination with low plant density — the roots are not aerating the rhizosphere adequately. A secondary cause is a soil type that compacted heavily over time, cutting off oxygen diffusion from the cap layer above.

Do not vacuum the substrate aggressively. That releases the hydrogen sulphide directly into the water column and can stress or kill fish quickly. The safer approach is to gently disturb a small section at a time over several sessions, allowing the gas to dissipate slowly. In severe cases, partial substrate replacement in the affected area — removing a section of the tank, not the whole thing — is recoverable. Prevention is the correct strategy: soil at 1 to 1.5 inches maximum, capped at the same depth, planted densely enough that roots penetrate the full soil layer.

My fish are gasping at the surface — what is causing it?

Surface gasping means dissolved oxygen is critically low. In a Walstad setup, this happens most often in three situations: a newly established tank where the microbial oxygen demand from the soil is at its peak, an overstocked tank where fish respiration is exceeding the system's oxygen production, and a tank where floating plant cover has become so dense it is sealing the surface and preventing gas exchange.

The immediate response is surface agitation — a small airstone or positioning a filter outlet to break the surface. This is temporary and does not compromise the Walstad system in the short term. Once the fish are stable, identify the actual cause. If it is floating plant overgrowth, skim back to roughly 50% surface coverage. If it is overstocking, you have a bioload problem that surface agitation will not solve long-term.

In an established, correctly stocked tank with reasonable floating plant coverage, dissolved oxygen depletion is unusual. If it is happening in a tank you consider mature, check whether plant die-back has occurred — decaying plant matter has significant oxygen demand.

How to apply it

When something goes wrong in your tank, the diagnostic sequence is the same every time: identify the signal, name the mechanism, address the root cause — not the symptom.

Start here:

1. Is the tank under three months old? If yes, most problems — algae, brown water, fluctuating parameters — are establishment-phase features. The intervention is usually nothing, or at most reducing the light period. Do not reach for water changes or additives.

2. Check the light first. More than 70% of persistent algae problems in a low-tech setup trace to a light period that is too long or a fixture that is too intense. Reduce the photoperiod to six hours and observe for two weeks before changing anything else.

3. Check the stocking. Run an honest assessment of your bioload against your plant mass. Small schooling fish and invertebrates are the correct load. If you are running large fish, messy fish, or more fish than the plant mass can realistically process, no amount of substrate adjustment fixes it.

4. Intervene physically, not chemically. Hair algae is removed by hand, not by algaecides. Cloudy water is managed by waiting, not by clarifiers. Hydrogen sulphide is addressed by controlled disturbance, not by additives. The biology fixes itself when the conditions are correct — your job is to correct the conditions, not to suppress the symptoms.

A tank that is reading its own chemistry back to you through algae patterns and water colour is a functioning system. Learn to read it.

Frequently asked questions

Should I do a water change when my new dirted tank goes cloudy?

In most cases, no. Cloudiness in an establishment-phase tank is typically soil particulates settling or tannins clearing, and a water change disrupts the biological loop that is just beginning to form. Wait at least two weeks before intervening with a water change unless ammonia is spiking to a level that is actively stressing fish.

My cap layer is mixing with the soil — what do I do?

Prevention is easier than correction: a fine gravel or coarse sand cap at 1 to 1.5 inches, added carefully over the soil without disturbing it, stays separated in most setups. If mixing has occurred in an established tank, targeted intervention — carefully adding cap material to the affected area — is safer than tearing the tank down. Avoid deep substrate vacuuming, which accelerates the mixing.

Can I fix a crashed tank without starting over?

It depends on the cause. An ammonia spike from wrong soil is recoverable with partial water changes and time if caught early. Hydrogen sulphide from anaerobic pockets can be managed with careful partial substrate replacement. A tank that was established with the wrong soil type and has been leaching slow-release fertiliser for months is very difficult to correct without a full restart — which is why reading the soil bag before setup matters more than any fix after the fact.

How do I know if my problem is the soil or the light?

Soil problems tend to show up in water chemistry — ammonia spikes, persistent cloudiness, hydrogen sulphide smell — and are most acute in the first eight weeks. Light problems show up as algae — on the glass, on leaves, in the water column — and get worse over time rather than resolving. If your water parameters are stable but algae is increasing, start with the light.

What is the one thing most people do wrong in a Walstad setup?

Intervening during establishment. The first four to eight weeks of a dirted tank look wrong by conventional fishkeeping standards — brown water, algae, fluctuating parameters — and every instinct tells you to fix it. The system is not broken. It is establishing. The most common way a Walstad tank actually fails is not bad soil or wrong plants; it is a keeper who changed the water, added a chemical, or tore the substrate apart because the tank didn't look right in week three.
