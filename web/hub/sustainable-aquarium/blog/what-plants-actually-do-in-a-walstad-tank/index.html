What Plants Actually Do in a Walstad Tank

2026-10-11

The plant list is always where people go wrong. You spend two hours cross-referencing species, pick something that looks right, and drop it into a six-week-old soil tank — and within a month you have either a thriving nitrogen sink or a passenger decorating a slow-motion algae problem. The difference is not aesthetics, and it is not growth rate. It is whether that plant is pulling nitrogen out of the water column fast enough to keep the ecosystem from tipping.

The Plants Are the Regulator

In a conventional high-tech tank, the aquarist is the regulator. Weekly water changes flush accumulated nitrates. CO2 injection and liquid fertiliser give plants everything they need regardless of what the substrate is doing. The system only has to look good between interventions. In a Walstad tank, none of that infrastructure exists. The soil off-gasses ammonia as it matures, livestock produce waste continuously, and if the plants are not converting that nitrogen load fast enough, the algae will. Algae is not a decoration failure. It is a signal that nitrogen is available and the higher plants are not claiming it.

That mechanism — the plant as a nitrogen sink — is the one the hobby almost never explains directly. Most planted-tank content sorts species by looks, by difficulty, by light requirement. None of those categories tell you what you actually need to know when you are running a no-water-change, soil-based system: which plants are doing the biological work, and which ones are along for the ride.

Not every plant in a Walstad tank is carrying equal load. Some are doing heavy nitrogen-cycling work through rapid uptake of ammonia and nitrate directly from the water column. Others are slow-growing, low-demand species that add structure and biological surface area but contribute almost nothing to nitrogen drawdown. Both have a place — but if you build a tank around slow-growing passengers and call it planted, you are going to be algae-limited inside sixty days and you will not know why.

The plants that pull hardest on the nitrogen cycle share a specific profile. Fast growth is part of it, but fast growth is the output, not the mechanism. What you are actually looking for is high demand for dissolved nitrogen — plants that are genuinely pulling ammonia and nitrate from the water column as a primary nutrient source rather than relying on root uptake from the substrate. Stem plants are the clearest example. Species like water wisteria, hornwort, and fast-growing hygrophila do the majority of their feeding through the water column. They grow quickly, strip nitrogen aggressively, and in a maturing Walstad tank they are doing the ecological equivalent of running an aerobic processing unit inside the water.

Floating plants deserve their own category. Species like frogbit, salvinia, and duckweed have direct access to atmospheric CO2 and to the water surface where off-gassed nutrients concentrate. Their nitrogen uptake rate is extremely high relative to their biomass because they are not spending energy building root systems into substrate. In the first three to six months of a soil tank — when the substrate is most actively releasing ammonia as organic matter breaks down — a surface layer of floating plants can be the difference between a tank that cycles smoothly and one that crashes. They are not pretty in the conventional aquascaping sense. They block light and compete with stem plants. But in functional terms they are the most efficient nitrogen processors you can put in a new soil-based system, and cutting them out because the tank looks tidier without them is prioritising aesthetics over ecology in a system where ecology is the whole point.

The Algae Is a Math Problem

When your tank tips into algae in the first eight weeks, the reflex is to dim the lights. That is almost always the wrong fix. What you are looking at is a nitrogen processing failure — the plant mass you chose does not have the uptake capacity to match what your maturing soil substrate is releasing. Algae has lower metabolic overhead than higher plants. It colonises available nitrogen faster. The solution is not less light; it is more competitive nitrogen demand from plants that feed through the water column aggressively.

Contrast fast stem species with slow-growing rhizome plants like anubias and java fern. These are not bad choices. They handle the high-organic, lower-light conditions of a soil substrate better than most species, and they contribute to the biological surface area that supports the microbial community. But their nitrogen demand is low. They draw nutrients primarily through their root system and at a pace that does not significantly reduce water-column nitrogen. In a tank that is still in its ammonia-spike phase — the first eight to twelve weeks after capping a soil substrate — putting anubias front and centre and calling it a planted tank is a setup for an algae event that most people misread as a lighting problem or a stocking mistake.

The misread matters because it sends you chasing the wrong fix. You dim the lights. You reduce feeding. You buy a different fertiliser. None of it addresses the actual problem, which is that the nitrogen processing capacity of your plant mass is not matched to the nitrogen load your system is generating. This is not a theory abstraction — it is the specific mechanical reason that Walstad tanks with heavy fast-stem plantings stabilise earlier and hold balance longer than tanks planted primarily for aesthetics.

If ammonia is climbing in weeks two through six and your plants are not visibly growing, you do not have a light problem. You do not have a dosing problem. You have a nitrogen sink problem — your plant mass does not have the uptake capacity to match what the soil is releasing. That is a diagnosable, fixable mechanical failure. Add fast column-feeding stem plants. Add floating cover. Give the system more biological processing capacity. The instinct to adjust chemistry or reduce feeding is almost always wrong at this stage. The tank is telling you what it needs. It needs more plants doing the right kind of work — not a different water conditioner and not a stronger filter.

Ammonia test kits in weeks two through six tell you more about your plant selection decisions than any species guide will. If the reading is climbing and the plants are not moving, you have your answer. The fix is biological, not chemical.

The Plant Community Is a Succession

Most plant guides treat a planted tank as a fixed installation. Pick your species, arrange them, done. In a Walstad tank that framing will fail you, because the nitrogen demand profile of the system shifts as the substrate matures — and the plant community has to shift with it.

In the first three months, the soil is actively decomposing and releasing ammonia — high nitrogen availability, high plant demand. Fast stem species and floating cover are doing real ecological work during this window. By month six to nine, off-gassing slows, the substrate starts to behave more like a stable root medium, and the water-column nitrogen load drops significantly. A tank that needed heavy stem plantings at month two can be over-planted by month eight, with decaying stems adding organic load rather than removing it. When the balance shifts and you have not shifted with it, you get a crash you cannot diagnose because you never tracked what the system was actually doing.

This is the timeline dimension that most plant guides skip entirely. What the ecosystem needs at month two is not what it needs at month twelve, and planting for aesthetics from day one means you are ignoring the ecological arc entirely.

The practical implication is that choosing plants for a Walstad tank requires thinking in ecological roles before it requires thinking in species names. Establish your nitrogen sink first — fast stem species and floating plants, matched to the expected nitrogen load from your substrate depth and livestock plan. Then add structural and slow-growing species for the long-term community. Read the system as it matures and reduce fast-growth mass as off-gassing slows. The sequence is not complicated, but it is invisible in any plant guide organised by looks or light level.

What remains genuinely difficult is quantifying the nitrogen load of a specific substrate batch. Soil depth, organic content, and source material vary enough that two tanks built identically can behave differently in their first months. There is no formula that replaces watching the tank. Watch ammonia in the early weeks. Watch plant growth rate — not as an aesthetic signal but as a readout of whether your nitrogen sink is functional. If it is climbing and they are not growing, the fix is more biological processing capacity, not a tweak to your water chemistry. The tank is a system running its own succession. Your job is to read it and plant accordingly.

Understanding the nitrogen sink is the one thing that separates a Walstad tank that stabilises from one that spends six months lurching between algae events and emergency rescapes. The species names matter less than the ecological roles — which plants are processing water-column nitrogen aggressively, which are structural passengers, and how that ratio needs to shift as the substrate matures. Get the functional logic right first. The aesthetics follow from a system that is actually working.
